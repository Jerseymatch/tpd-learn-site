A premature card is any flop, turn or river the dealer brings before the betting round is complete. Somebody still had action, and the next card is already on the board. It's a rough feeling as a dealer when it happens, but it is what it is. What matters is what you do next.

Keeping that card would be an insanely unfair advantage. A player would get to decide whether to put money in while knowing what the next card is. You might as well show one player the turn and the river and then ask if they want to keep playing. Players also feel like the card they were supposed to get was taken from them. So poker has a procedure for it, and that procedure changed a few years ago. I was at the TDA Summit where it was voted on.

The dealer never fixes a premature card alone. Rules and the exact steps can differ room to room. When yours differs from what I describe here, your room wins.

It's always a floor call

A premature card is a supervisor call every single time, including the flop. Call it even if you think it's obvious what should happen, and even if a player objects. If you ever skip it intentionally at a job, that's a serious violation.

It doesn't matter if the player you skipped would have checked anyway. Say you bring the turn before the button checks. Maybe he was always going to check. You still call the floor. If you made the error, own it. Letting things like this slide is how a game loses its integrity.

Say "Floor" and your table number, the same as any other floor call. Then tell the supervisor exactly what happened, without the drama:

"These two players were in for 1,000. This player only put in 225. I brought the bets in, and I brought the turn."

You don't need to say "I made a huge mistake." Players who play a lot see this sometimes. If a player starts complaining, the rest of the table is likely to say, "It happens all the time, you're fine." But if you call it a huge mistake, nobody can defend you anymore. And this isn't the moment for jokes. If there's $4,000 in the pot, think about how the players feel about a silly dealer right after an error. Calling the floor covers how to make any floor call clean.

What I correct in class

Putting the bets back and letting it ride

A student pulled the bets in, burned and brought the turn while one player was still short. Then they pushed the bets back and asked the player if he wanted to call, with the turn sitting right there. He said sure. We can't do that. Even though he called, it's still a floor call, and that turn card has to go back and get shuffled into the rest of the deck.

Calling it a huge error

A student made the floor call more colorful than it needed to be. Just say what happened, in order, and let the supervisor take it from there.

What the supervisor does

As a dealer, you aren't the one deciding any of this. But you should know the steps, because the supervisor is going to talk you through them at your table, and it goes roughly like this:

  1. Get the dealer's account. What did each player do, and when did the card come out?
  2. Check for substantial action. A card is only premature if there hasn't been substantial action after it. If several players have already acted on the new street and the skipped player said nothing, it isn't a premature card anymore. More on that below.
  3. Put the bets back where they were, and confirm the amounts with everyone. The supervisor might even move the chips back themselves.
  4. Set the premature card aside. The supervisor tells the players it's going to get shuffled back in randomly, and that a new card will come out. They might have you put it face up on top of the deck for a moment. Rooms handle that detail a few different ways.
  5. Finish the missed action. The player who was skipped acts, and the betting round gets completed. What options that player has is the supervisor's call.
  6. Reshuffle only when the card is needed. The burn is already out, so there's no second burn. The supervisor waits until the moment the next card is actually due, then has the dealer put the premature card back into the stub and shuffle it.
  7. Shuffle face down. The supervisor will tell you very deliberately: without exposing any cards in the stub, riffle, riffle, box, riffle, and cut. Then that new card comes out.

Don't rush to fix it

A dealer who just made a mistake feels panicked and wants to fix it immediately. That's exactly the moment to slow down. Wait until the supervisor tells you, and wait until the card is actually needed. A good supervisor knows you're rattled and will tell you to hold off.

[[moment W_B8yAuYz90 21:23 | Finish the missed action before you reshuffle, and don't rush the dealer]]

Mix the stub face down and low

A dealer's habit when squaring the deck is to expose the cards, because that's what you do at the start of a hand. Mid-hand, you can't. You mix and square the stub face down, keep the deck low, and never flash a card. I had hoped the TDA would write that into the procedure itself, for new dealers who learn from it.

Substantial action: when it's no longer premature

The TDA defines substantial action as any two actions in turn where at least one of them puts chips in, or any three actions in turn. Posted blinds don't count. There are gray areas when things happen in a fraction of a second, which is one more reason the supervisor makes the decision, not you.

So if several players have already acted on the new street and the skipped player never said anything, the supervisor can decide it isn't a premature card. For a similar situation with a skipped player, see action out of turn. Misdeals has the full explanation of substantial action.

The old way vs the new TDA way

For the longest time, the premature flop and the premature turn were handled differently.

  • A premature flop was left with its burn, and the flop cards got shuffled back in. Pulling out the turn and river cards to preserve the order is error-prone, because cards get flipped or mistaken for the muck.
  • A premature turn was handled by preserving the card order. The card that would have been the river became the turn. Then the premature card got shuffled back into the stub for the river. People called that keeping the "natural river."

At the 2022 TDA Summit, that changed. The TDA now handles every premature card the same way, the turn included: leave the burn card where it is, return the premature card to the deck stub, and reshuffle the entire stub. It's random. Nobody cares which card it was supposed to be. That came out in the rules published in early 2023.

Now most properties reshuffle. Some places still use the old version. Learn which one your room uses, and do it their way.

Why it's a "recommended procedure"

The premature-card procedure lives in the TDA's recommended procedures, not its numbered rules. The numbered rules are what any room that says "we follow TDA" has to follow. Recommended procedures are basically tests. The TDA recommends a procedure, and two years later, at the next summit, they ask all the poker rooms that used it how it went. If everybody loved it, it can become a real rule. If not, they might get rid of it.

That helps the rooms. Players get really upset when a poker room changes its procedure. But if the room can show them it's now a TDA recommended procedure, the players don't get as upset. The room can say, "We're trying this out." You can read the rules yourself on the TDA's website, including the redline version that shows exactly what changed. And being able to talk about this intelligently will certainly help you in the interview side of an audition.

How the vote went

I was a participant at that summit. On day one, the room was ready to pass the change. Then one comment about slowing the game down (you often never need the river at all) swung the vote, and it failed. I had never seen the TDA revote on something before, but on day two they brought it up again and it passed. I was thrilled, because it's the version my emotions were behind. When I recorded my older course lesson on this, the natural-river procedure was still the standard, so if you've watched that lesson, this is the update.

Why the reshuffle is fair: random card theory

Players feel like the card they were "supposed" to get matters. Here's an argument from an earlier summit, in 2019, that I loved. Full Tilt Poker never built a shuffled 52-card deck. It drew each card randomly from the cards that were left, and nobody minded. Card order isn't real information. Before anyone sees it, the "natural river" is just a random card.

And keeping the card order is actually worse math. Under the old way, the premature turn card can't be the turn anymore. It can only come as the river, so it gets half the chance of any other unseen card. Shuffling it back into the whole stub puts every card back on equal footing.

Poker isn't a game for the dealer or the house. The procedure exists so the outcome stays random.

Explaining it at the table

This is advice the TDA gives poker rooms and supervisors, and it works for anyone explaining the rule: don't argue random card theory with players. It's triggering. Their advice for explaining the new procedure is just to say that every premature card is handled the same now: they all get reshuffled. In their experience, players and staff accept that right away.

Premature cards in stud

Stud had its own premature-card procedure, built around preserving the card order with the supervisor present. Even longtime stud players at the summit agreed to simplify it: the premature card goes back to the stub, the stub gets reshuffled, and the dealing continues without another burn. Either way, the rule for you is the same as in hold'em. Never handle a premature card on your own. Stud, draw and mixed games covers dealing stud.

How to never bring a premature card

The best fix is never needing one. These habits are what keep it from happening:

  • Tap the table before every flop, turn and river. It notifies everybody at the table that the next card is coming. You'd be surprised how often that one tap prevents a premature card, because the player who hasn't acted says, "Wait, I didn't act yet."
  • Count the players in the hand. When you bring the bets in, you know how many players there are. As they act, you go one, two, three, up to that number. If you're missing somebody, instead of bringing the next card, you look around: how did I miss somebody? I don't literally count. It's more like a beat. This person acts, this person acts, this person acts, and I feel four. If I'm missing the fourth beat, something's wrong.
  • Watch for players who hide their cards. Some players keep their cards behind their stack, so you can't see that they're still in. That's why you track the count, not just the cards you can see.
  • Get a clear action from the big blind. Before the flop, get a tap, a "check," chips or a "raise." If the big blind never clearly acted, the flop you bring can turn into a premature flop.
  • Get the last chips in. If a player moves in and keeps a couple of white chips behind, motion for them to put it in. A premature river because seat four still had $10 left is the most annoying thing in the world, and I'm not exaggerating.

How to bring the flop, turn and river walks through the whole routine, and practicing at home is where these habits get built.

What I correct in class

Forgetting to tap the table

This is something I correct a lot. A student brings the bets in smoothly, makes the right change, and then brings the flop without tapping. The tap needs to be automatic before every street, loud enough to alert the players, but not so loud it wakes anyone up.

Losing the count

When a player leaves their cards somewhere you can't see them, it's easy to lose track of who's still in. Hold the number of players in your head and update it every time it changes. If you do that with real attention to detail and consistency, you'll notice when you're missing a beat.

Common questions about premature cards

What happens if the dealer flips the turn too early?

The dealer calls the floor. If it's caught before substantial action, the betting gets finished, and then the premature turn goes back into the stub, the stub gets reshuffled, and a new turn comes out. The burn card stays. Some rooms still use the older procedure, where the card that would have been the river becomes the turn.

What happens if the dealer deals the flop too early?

Same thing. It's a floor call. The missed preflop action gets completed, and the flop cards get shuffled back into the stub before a new flop comes out. The burn card stays where it is.

What happens if the dealer burns and turns before the action is over?

That's a premature card. The burn stays the burn, the missed betting is finished, and the premature card gets reshuffled into the stub when the next card is needed. There's no second burn.

Can the dealer just fix a premature card without the floor?

No. It's always a floor call, even when it seems obvious and even if the skipped player would have checked. The supervisor decides whether there was substantial action and how to handle it.

Is a premature card a misdeal?

No. The supervisor has the missed betting finished and the premature card reshuffled, and the hand goes on. See misdeals for what does kill a hand.

What is the natural river?

Under the old procedure for a premature turn, the card that would have been the river was used as the turn, to keep the card order. The TDA dropped that in favor of reshuffling every premature card, though some rooms still use it.

What is TDA random card theory?

It's the idea that any unseen card is random, so the order the cards were in doesn't matter. That's why the TDA now reshuffles every premature card instead of preserving the "natural" next card.
