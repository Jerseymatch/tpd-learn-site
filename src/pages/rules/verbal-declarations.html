In poker, when it's your turn, what you say is your action. Fold, check, call, raise, a specific amount, "pot," "all in": say it in turn, and you're held to it. That's what people mean by verbal is binding. And when what a player says and what they do happen at the same time and contradict each other, the words win.

The rules of betting exist to read the minds of the players, as well as anyone can, in a way nobody can manipulate. If a player could say "call," watch how the table reacts, and then take it back, they'd be using everybody else's reactions to play their hand. That breaks one player per hand. The basics, along with string bets and the 50% rule, are in the betting rules guide. This page is the situations that come up around them.

A player's action can't be undone by changing their mind or claiming they meant something else. The only way it changes is with a supervisor at the table. Rooms have their own rules on some of this, and when yours does, follow your room.

The words beat the chips

Verbal is always going to take priority over what a player physically does, and they can't undo a verbal declaration. A few spots where that matters:

"Raise," then not enough chips

A player says "raise" and puts out 95 when a raise would take more. Because they said raise, they're raising, and it has to be at least a minimum raise. They can't take the word back. If they'd said nothing and put out 95, it would've been a call. Same chips, different result, because of the word.

"Call" while throwing their cards

A player says "call" and tosses their cards forward at the same time. Is that a fold? When two actions happen at the same time, the dealer enforces the verbal action: "You said call when it was your turn, and you folded at the same time. Verbal is binding, so I need you to call." If they say, "Oh, yeah, I did call," they take their cards back, and you keep going. The only time you do anything else is if they resist, and then you get the floor and explain what happened.

Pay attention to that player now. Who does this? Somebody who wants the table to think they meant to fold. They often have a monster.

And if a player says "call" and their cards are already gone, they still owe the call. If you see a scared player trying to get their hand into the muck before paying, protect the muck.

"Call," but no chips

When a player says "call," have them put at least one chip in: "I need you to put a chip in the pot, please." Once a chip is in, they can't later claim they never said anything, or that they never meant to put money in.

"Pot" and "all in" are binding

This one matters a lot in pot-limit Omaha. There's a dramatic difference between:

  • "How much is in the pot?" A question. You tell them what a pot bet would be, and then they decide what to do.
  • "Pot." An action. Once a player says pot, they're betting the pot. They absolutely have to.

Saying pot is like saying all in. Once you say you're all in, you're all in. If you're in an audition and a player says pot and you let them fold, you fail. If a player says "I'm all in" in a no-limit game and you let them fold, you fail. Just as serious. All-in rules covers every way a player can go all in.

When a player says pot and then tries to fold, you don't need the floor yet. "No, no, I need the 25. You said pot." Keep it moving. Only if they resist does it become a floor call. The Omaha guide covers how to work out the pot.

What I correct in class

Giving them an option after "pot"

A student hears "pot," counts out the amount, and asks the player, "Do you want to put it in?" Even if the player does put it in, you just gave them an option they didn't have. Once they've committed, you don't ask. You tell them the amount.

Mistaking the question for the action

Another class mistake: a player says pot, hears the amount and folds, and the student lets it go, as if the player had asked a question. Listen for which one it was.

Saying it before the chip lands

A verbal declaration counts as long as it's said before the chip hits the felt. Even at the exact same time, it's fine, technically. The rule is set up that way because there's not really any way to manipulate it: the player can't get a read off anyone if the word comes out as the chip lands.

  • Said during the motion. A player comes out with a chip and says "raise to 50" while it's still on its way to the felt. I allow that raise. They announced it before the motion was completed, and you're allowed to do that. If another player calls it a string bet, explain that, and if they keep giving you resistance, call the floor.
  • Said after the chip landed. If there's no bet out there and a player drops a green chip and says "10" a full second or two after it lands, generally that chip is supposed to be the full 25. A single chip with nothing said is the full value of the chip. If there was a bet out there, it's the oversized chip rule: a call.
  • A photo finish. In practice, you have a little leeway when it's a split second after. But you have to know your table. When you're in doubt, follow the rule to a T. Either way, take the chance to tell the player to say the amount before they put the chip out, because that was close.

How you say it matters. Dealers have a tendency to use an explaining tone in that moment, and I don't like it. It makes players feel like they're in school. I'd rather make it light: "Woo, that was close." Ask yourself how you'd say it to your own friends, and bring that version to the table.

Cash games and tournaments

In a tournament, enforce it consistently: one chip with nothing said is a call, every time, unless a supervisor says otherwise. Everything in a tournament affects everybody's chips for the rest of the event. In cash games, some rooms and tables are looser about it. In Texas, for example, a lot of tables push back hard enough when the dealer makes a red chip a call that the floor will probably just let them raise. I still want new dealers to enforce it as a call and let the player have you call the floor. If the floor makes it a raise, great: "Okay, great. I couldn't do that on my own."

When the dealer didn't hear it

As a dealer, you need to know every action with 100% certainty. If you're 99.9% sure a player said "call" and the room is loud, you ask. You might feel bad, because the player gives away a little information by repeating themselves. But that's on them. They had the chance to be clear. If you're not 100% sure what they said, someone else at the table might not have heard it either, and the last thing you want is a player saying later in the hand, "I never said call." And if you weren't sure and repeated "call" anyway, the dealer has to take some of the blame.

"I said raise"

This happens a lot: a player throws out a green chip, you announce "call," and they say, "No, I said raise." You didn't hear anything.

  • Ask the table: "Did anyone hear them say anything?" If someone says yes: "Oh, I'm so sorry. I didn't hear it. This was a raise to 25."
  • If it's gone call, call, call around the table before they say something, call the floor.
  • Or stay on their side: "I didn't hear you say it, so I can't let it be a raise. Would you like me to get the floor?" Maybe the floor lets it be a raise.

You can also tell them, "Hey, you need to speak up." But I like to keep it as friendly as possible.

What the supervisor does

When the floor comes over, they gather the facts. The dealer tells them what happened. As soon as the supervisor hears "I thought he said call," they know the dealer isn't 100% sure, so they give the players a chance to speak up. It becomes case by case. A dealer can't make someone call if they said a different word that makes sense there. But if two players heard "call," the dealer repeated "call," the player didn't correct anyone, and only spoke up once the other player tabled their hand, that player had the chance to speak up earlier and may be held to the call. That's the floor's decision, not yours.

That "raise to" rule is the most common, but the floor has the final say. In that hand, I took a regular's "I raise 60" as a raise to 60, and it went to the floor. The floor let him make it 60 more, because that's what he means when he says it, and there was no angle. I learned from it and kept my ego out of it.

Unclear words and gestures

Players get creative, and a lot of what they say isn't a poker term. A player says "going up" and tosses out the amount of the call. Is that a call or a raise? If they've been saying "going up" every time they raise, that sort of makes it a raise. If you have no idea, and especially if you're newer, say what you saw and get a floor: "You said 'going up' and put out a call amount. That could be read as a raise. I'm going to get a floor over here."

In class, when I play the supervisor on that one, I ask the table whether the player has been saying "going up" when they raise. If the dealer just sat down at that table, the players answer. Then the floor might say: "We're going to make this a call right now. Please be careful about your language at the table. Even if you don't say a specific poker term, you may end up being held to an action you don't intend."

The line I give players is the same: whenever you say or do anything at the table that could be interpreted to mean something, you put yourself at risk.

Confirm the action: "Was that your action?"

People check in all kinds of ridiculous ways. The pinky check is the worst, because I miss it so often. When a player's motion is unclear, you don't ignore it, and you don't decide for them. You confirm it: "Was that your action?" They say yes. You say, "Check," and move on.

Don't ask "Was that a check?" or say anything else. "It's your turn" can annoy them if they think they already checked. And once you've confirmed it, the next time they do that motion, you just say "check." If they weren't trying to check, they built the habit, and that's on them. If the floor ever comes, you can tell them exactly that: "In a previous hand, they did this. I asked if that was their action. They said yes."

Most of the time it's innocent. A lot of players aren't taking the game that seriously, and they don't want to be bothered with following rules. But the one thing you don't want is a player gaining an advantage from a strategic lack of clarity: a motion that's a check if the other player bets, and "I hadn't acted yet" if they don't. Confirming takes that away.

When chips come forward

Not every action is spoken. Chips that come far enough forward can count as action, and rooms handle this differently: a betting line, a working-space rule, or the player's cards as the line. The betting rules guide explains all three.

  • Past the working space, then "check." Technically, a player who comes well past their working space with chips and then checks has made a minimum bet. If they're only holding red chips, the minimum is the red chip. Facing a bet, it's a call. If they resist, don't argue. Call the floor. It's their job now.
  • Across a betting line and back. A player picks up a stack, brings it across the line, puts it back and says check. If you know the house rule makes that a bet, a lot of rooms will make it a minimum bet. If you don't know the house rule, call the floor and describe exactly what happened.
  • A big stack forward, then a fold. If it's $5 or $10 to call, I'm not that worried. If it's a few hundred to call and they brought a stack of green out, call the floor for your first six months. A player can bring money out, watch what everyone does, and use that to decide. That's why a lot of places make them put the money in.
[[moment xJBsNUVRVNE 1:18:13 | Chips past the working space, then "check": is it a bet?]]

I'm not a fan of strict betting lines, especially rules that say everything in a player's hand across the line is committed. Those are impossible to enforce. Generally, whatever is dropped or cut out is the bet.

What I correct in class

Letting a contradiction slide

When a player says "call" as the cards go forward, students often assume the toss is a fold. Verbal is binding. Get the call, unless they resist, and then it's a floor call.

Telling the floor what the player meant

When a supervisor comes over for one of these, students want to say "he meant to raise." Don't. Say what was said and what was done, in order. What to say to the floor walks through it.

Common questions about verbal declarations

Is verbal binding in poker?

Yes. In turn, saying fold, check, call, raise, an amount, pot or all in is binding. If the words and the chips contradict each other, the words win.

Can a player take back a verbal call?

No. Once a player says "call" in turn, they owe the call, even if their cards are already in the muck. Only a supervisor can change an action.

Does "raise 20" mean raise to 20 or raise by 20?

In the enormous majority of poker rooms, it means a raise to 20 total.

Can you say "pot" and then fold?

No. Saying "pot" in pot-limit is a bet of the pot, the same way saying "all in" commits you. Asking how much is in the pot is different: that's a question, not an action.

Does a verbal bet count if the chip already landed?

It has to be said before the chip hits the felt, or at the same moment. Said after, the chip plays as it landed: its full value with no bet out there, or a call facing a bet.

What if the dealer didn't hear the player say raise?

If someone else at the table heard it, it can be a raise. If others have already acted, or the player disputes it, it's a floor call.
