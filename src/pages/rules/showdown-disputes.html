The showdown is where the money changes hands, so it's where players argue. The arguments at the showdown tend to come from a handful of situations: a player shows one card, a player mucks a hand that would have won, somebody shows their cards to a neighbor, somebody slow rolls, or the dealer reads a hand wrong.

The basic procedure, tabling, mucking, framing and who shows first, is in the showdown guide, and the two rules underneath all of it are in cards speak and one player per hand. This page is the disputes. In every one of them, your job is the same: make it clear what wins, don't help anybody, and when a player resists, call the floor. The dealer explains the rule. The supervisor makes the ruling.

"It takes two to win": a player shows one card

This one comes up a lot. The river's done, one player tables both cards, and the other player flips up one card and says "I have a flush."

Not every poker room is on the same page about this, but the rule set I teach is that it takes two cards to win at the showdown. Here's how I'd handle it:

  • Read the hand that's properly tabled. Frame it and announce it: "Pair of eights with an ace." That's better than just saying "it takes two to win," because you're doing your job with the hand that's actually tabled.
  • If the player says "I have a flush," you can tell them, "It takes two cards to win," or "This player is the only one who's tabled their hand so far." They'll usually get the message and flip the other card.
  • If they still don't table it, start motioning to muck. Until they put both cards face up, they have nothing.

Tell the player what you need. Don't tell them "table your hand," and don't tell them they're winning. A tabled hand belongs to everybody, so reading it doesn't break one player per hand. Telling someone they can beat it before they've shown does.

If the player gets heated about it, remind them gently: "Hey, I didn't take your cards. I just read the hand that was tabled. You still have the pot." Sometimes, after going to war with you like that, they'll tip you more than usual. Thank them in a way that acknowledges what the two of you just went through together.

When nobody else has shown

"It takes two to win" only comes into it when someone else has also shown. Nowadays, if a player shows one card and everybody else mucks, that one card can win, because nobody else has cards. Some rooms make the last player show their hand to win an uncontested pot. I now prefer that the last player with cards just wins. Both rules are fine. Learn which one your room uses.

And if nobody has properly tabled a hand yet and one player has shown one card, that player has taken the initiative to start the showdown. Don't jump to the showdown order and make someone else show first. Say "it takes two to win" and give them the chance to table their hand. Only if they refuse do you go to the order. The order is for when a reasonable amount of time has gone by and nobody has chosen to show.

A possible tie

When one player has tabled and the other shows one card that might be enough for a chop, I announce the tabled hand, frame it, and slowly reach for the other hand. That gives them every chance to put the second card up. Protect the inexperienced player, and follow your room.

A card flips face down, or flies

Sometimes a player tables their hand and one card lands face down, or a card goes flying. Don't flip it up for them. Go slowly, say "it takes two to win," point at the card, and let the player turn it up. Tabling your hand is the player's responsibility: just put the cards face up on the felt. If a card goes off the table, that's a floor call.

What I correct in class

Turning a player's cards over

You never turn a player's cards face up for them, and you never turn a tabled hand back face down. You don't interpret what a player meant to do. Some dealers make that mistake, and it violates cards speak. As a shift manager, I'd show players how to table a hand gently. Tabling it is on the player.

Unframing too early

When a hand is winning, leave it framed. You don't unframe it until somebody beats it.

When a player mucks a winning hand

A player looks at the board, decides they lost, and tosses their cards in. They had the winner. What do you do?

Nothing. You take the cards and you don't say a word. They chose to muck. You're just facilitating what they chose to do. If you say "are you sure?" or hesitate, you're helping one player against another.

Here's one I talked about on a livestream. A 2/5 all-in: the shown hand was a set of sevens, the river put five diamonds on the board, and the caller mucked without realizing he had the board flush. Nobody at the table said a word. The dealer took the mucked cards and pushed the pot to the set. That dealer did it exactly right.

[[moment gROCpHKuXF0 5:29 | Flush on the board, the caller mucks: why the dealer says nothing]]

The same goes for a player who misreads their own hand out loud. "Oh man, I thought I had nine-five." Let it go. You don't say anything. Read what's tabled.

Read the hand you framed

The other side of staying quiet is that when a hand is tabled, you read it clearly enough that nobody gets confused. Here's a mistake I made. A player tabled 10-8 on an ace-queen board, and I framed the board but didn't announce anything. The caller thought he saw a straight and threw his cards forward. A third player said, "Ten high." I felt like my framing had influenced that muck. What I should have said was "ace, queen, ten high."

If you frame the board, you also read the hand. Read a tabled hand until it's no longer confusing. After that, if a player mucks, it's on them. The supervisor confirmed afterward that dealers read tabled hands.

A straight or flush on the board

When there's a straight on the board and a player tables aces, stay quiet for a few seconds and let the hands get tabled. Never say "playing the board." If someone asks what the player has, say "a straight" and frame all five. That reads the hand correctly without tipping off a player who doesn't realize they're playing the board too. When it's more complicated, like a flush on the board, pause before you announce.

Tabled, then mucked. A player who tables a winner and then picks it back up and mucks it, or tosses their cards forward and grabs them back before you take them, is a floor call. So is a player who mucks after the dealer misread their hand. The details are in the showdown guide and cards speak.

"Show one, show all"

"Show one, show all" is a very confusing statement. Catchy phrases get misinterpreted. What it actually means: if a player shows their cards to someone at the table, the rest of the table can see those cards upon request.

It does not mean that showing one card forces a player to show both. And it doesn't mean you expose the cards on your own. Here's how it works:

  • Who they showed doesn't change it. A spectator, a player who's already folded, the player next to them: all the same. If a player asks you, the dealer, to look at their folded cards, that counts as showing them too.
  • Nobody asks? Muck the cards promptly. You don't automatically show them to the table.
  • Someone asks before the cards reach the muck? Set them aside where you can identify them, and expose them when the hand is over, not during it. If they're already in the muck, they're in the muck.
  • When you expose them, lay them face up, flat on the table, where everyone can see. Don't hold them up, and don't make a production of it. Show them and move on.

Protect your muck the moment somebody asks. The player who showed their buddy and didn't want the cards exposed is going to be annoyed, and sometimes they'll try to get those cards into the muck before you can. Get your hand right on the muck. If it doesn't work out, call the floor.

It gets complicated when a player who hasn't acted yet didn't see the cards and wants to. That's a floor call. And in a tournament, never let a player show you their cards.

Have a clear explanation ready, because the player who wants to see the cards and the player who showed them will both have their own idea of what the phrase means. If the floor rules a different way in your room, go with the floor.

What I correct in class

Showing the cards to everybody right away

When I set this up in class, a player shows their cards to their neighbors, tosses them in, and someone asks to see them. The student who gets it right says: "I'm going to keep these aside, and when this hand is over, I'm going to expose them." Another student told me they would have shown the cards to everybody right then. Wait until the hand is over.

Doing everything at once

When players are mucking, bets are still out and somebody's cards need to be set aside, prioritize. Get the mucked cards first, then the chips, and get the cards you need to expose somewhere they're safe.

Slow rolls

Slow rolling is making an opponent think they've won, then showing the winner. Cards speak, so a slow roll doesn't change who wins. It's an etiquette violation, and the written rules don't cover every gap. Social norms do.

Two I've talked about on livestreams:

  • "You're good." In a PLO hand, one player tabled. The other said "you're good," and then showed his flush. Since the first hand was already tabled, the result stands. If it causes a fight, call a supervisor. If it doesn't, it's something to mention after the hand.
  • Showing two weak cards first. In Omaha, a player deliberately shows two weak cards to draw out the caller's hand. That's poor etiquette. And once another player has tabled, you have to show all four cards to win.
[[moment fieG5mvinQs 1:49:42 | A "you're good" slow roll in PLO: the result stands, and what the dealer does]]

You're not the one who deals with the player. The floor does. A room can ask repeat slow-rollers to leave, and that's a management decision, not yours. What you do is keep the showdown moving and read what's tabled, and if it turns into a fight, call the floor.

Heads-up, a player flips their hand up on the river

A player is heads-up on the river and turns their cards face up before the action is done. Is that a fold?

  • In a lot of cash games, no. Many rooms allow a player to flip their hand up heads-up, though some have stopped allowing it, so know your room's rule. Where it's allowed, leave the cards face up in front of the player. They're live.
  • Only kill them if the player throws them toward you. And tap before you kill them.
  • In a tournament, it's a penalty. Call a supervisor.

Three or more players in the hand is different: a player can't show cards while others are still playing. That's covered in exposed cards.

When the dealer misreads a hand

Everybody misreads a hand eventually. I've done it on camera. In a double-board bomb pot, I was about to chop a pot when a player had a full house on the bottom board that I hadn't seen. Instead of apologizing at length, I said, "Oh, I didn't see it, thanks for pointing that out," and fixed it.

  • Don't push a pot just because the players say "it's a chop." Ask them to say their hands, and read them yourself. In PLO especially, players should announce what they have.
  • If it's caught before the pot is pushed, fix it, keep it short, and move on.
  • If the pot already went to the wrong player, call the floor. Even in an audition, pushing toward the wrong player isn't an automatic fail if you catch it and call the floor. Cards speak explains what the supervisor does next.

And anyone at the table has the right to speak up when a tabled hand is being misread. In a 1-2 bomb pot I dealt, a player got quartered, losing one board and chopping the other. Another player asked why the jack-queen didn't win outright, and the quartered player got on me for explaining it. With tabled hands, cards speak. Anyone can speak up, and the dealer explains what wins and why.

What I correct in class

Reading slowly in hold'em

Players in Omaha will tolerate a dealer taking a moment to read. In hold'em, you can't be slow working out what beats what. At a poker table, if you misread a hand, you'll find out fast: players will let you know, and they won't wait while you figure out what beats what. Practice reading until it's automatic, and when you miss one, understand what you missed. That's what matters.

Where the dealer's job ends

You run the showdown. You read what's tabled, you take what's mucked, you set aside cards someone asked to see, and you explain the rule when a player asks. The moment a player resists, the dispute belongs to the supervisor. Say what happened, in order, and let them handle it. What to say to the floor walks through it.

If you're new in a room and not sure of its showdown policy, go with what the room does, ask about it later, and if anyone resists, call the floor.

How to practice showdown disputes

  • Deal the river and play both players. Table one hand and show one card from the other. Say out loud what you'd say: read the tabled hand, then "it takes two to win."
  • Set up show one, show all. Have a folded hand shown to a neighbor, then have someone ask to see it. Set it aside, finish the hand, and expose it.
  • Practice the silent muck. Put a flush on the board, muck the caller's hand, and push to the shown hand without saying anything.
  • Read every tabled hand aloud, including the ones that aren't obvious, like "ace, queen, ten high." If you have a practice partner, have them argue with you, and practice saying "Would you like me to call the floor?" calmly.

Common questions about showdown disputes

Do you have to show both cards to win in poker?

If another player has tabled their hand, yes: in the rule set I teach, it takes two cards to win. If everyone else has mucked, one card can win nowadays, and some rooms let the last player win without showing at all, while others make them show.

What does "show one, show all" mean?

If a player shows their cards to anyone, the rest of the table can see them upon request. The dealer sets them aside and exposes them when the hand is over. It doesn't mean showing one card forces a player to show both.

What happens if a player mucks the winning hand?

The hand is dead. The dealer takes it and says nothing. If the player had tabled it first, or the dealer misread it, call the floor.

Is slow rolling against the rules?

It's an etiquette violation, not a change to who wins: the cards speak. Rooms can deal with repeat slow-rollers, up to asking them to leave. That's the floor's call.

Can a dealer tell a player they have the winning hand?

Not before it's tabled. That breaks one player per hand. Once a hand is tabled, the dealer reads it out loud.

What if the dealer pushes the pot to the wrong player?

Call the floor. Cards speak, so the supervisor can award the pot to the right player, and if anyone disagrees, it may go to surveillance.
