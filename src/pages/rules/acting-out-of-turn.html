Acting out of turn is when a player acts before the player whose turn it is: a check, call, bet, raise, all-in or fold before the action gets to them. It comes up regularly. Seat one does it a lot, because they can't see past the dealer to the nine seat. People shouldn't be acting out of turn, period. The rules handle it, but it also needs to be discouraged.

Here's the short answer, then what you do about it at the table.

[[toc]]

The rule: does an out-of-turn action stand?

If the amount to call is the same when it actually becomes that player's turn, the action they took stands. If the amount to call has changed, they get a new decision, which can include checking.

The industry phrase is "if the action changes." I think that's confusing terminology. The way I say it is: if the amount to call changes.

  • In no limit, only a raise frees them. If nobody whose turn it was raises, the money stays in the pot. A check or a call in between doesn't change it.
  • If someone raises, the out-of-turn player gets a new decision. You push their out-of-turn chips back, and they're in for whatever they had in before.
  • It works for every kind of action. A player who went all in out of turn: if the players in between fold or call, the all-in stands. If someone raises, the all-in no longer stands and they get a new action.
  • Once it stands, they can't change their mind. They can't fold, call or raise instead. If the big blind raised out of turn and then everyone folds to them and they try to check, they've already made a raise.

Why it works this way

The rule is built so that the player whose turn it was can act the way they would have anyway. It also puts the player who acted out of turn at a disadvantage. If they shove out of turn, the other players can just call. The player in turn who benefits did nothing wrong.

The pot-limit exception

In pot limit, if a player says "pot" out of turn and the player in turn calls, the pot amount has changed. Since what they said was "pot," the way I read the rule, the out-of-turn player gets a new action, though I'm not 100% sure the whole poker world would agree. A raise to a fixed amount out of turn still stands like any other. This one is rare, and it would probably be a floor call. In my whole history, it's come up one time when I was flooring.

What the dealer does

  1. Stop the action first. Don't start explaining. Get your hand out there and say, "Hold on, hold on." Then point: "Action's over here. This is out of turn." Turn toward the player after the one who acted out of turn, because they're the one who'll be confused and keep it going.
  2. Prompt the player in turn, with the amount. "It's your turn first. Check or bet?" If there was a bet before the out-of-turn action, be explicit, because the next player may think the out-of-turn chips are the bet: "It's five to call to you. Pretend this isn't here." You don't need to tell them their options.
  3. Know the amount of the out-of-turn chips. Players often try to take it back. Tell them, "I need that to stay there for right now, and we'll see what happens." Some places teach you to push it back to them. A small, easy-to-remember bet is fine to push back, but if they put a stack right on top of their own chips, now you're stuck. At the very least, know how much it is, and if it's an uncounted stack, find out before they can take it back.
  4. Don't explain what's going to happen. As the dealer, you don't get into those rules in the moment. Explaining the consequences to the player in turn changes their action completely. Just say, "This player bet out of turn, but they weren't allowed to do that yet, and it's over here." If someone asks a hypothetical like "If I raise, do they still have to go all in?", that's a one-player-per-hand issue. Don't answer it. If they push, call the floor.
  5. Call the floor when it's unclear. If several players have acted, or it's mixed up with an unclear bet, just call the floor. It's easier for you. Nobody's expecting you to know the really weird stuff.

You can tell players to stop, but you can't undo what's already happened. That's why getting your hand out fast matters. The more on top of the action you are, prompting players and following it around the table, the less often it happens. When a player acts out of turn, it's a reflection on the dealer a little bit. Just don't prompt a player who's thinking.

For more on why you don't coach one player against another, see cards speak and one player per hand.

What I correct in class

Explaining instead of stopping

A student stops it, but talks to me instead of the table and lets the player take the chips back. Talk to the table like it's a real game: hand out, "Hold on," point to whose turn it is.

Thinking any action in between resets it

In no limit, a skipped player who just calls doesn't free up the out-of-turn raise behind them. Only a raise changes the amount to call.

Skipping a player during a betting round

The other side of this: the dealer moves the action past a player who hasn't acted. Follow the action seat by seat.

When a player gets skipped: the floor's call

Sometimes the out-of-turn action goes on long enough that the skipped player's turn is in question. Whether that player has lost their turn is a floor decision, usually based on substantial action. It differs room to room: there are a lot of poker rooms that let the dealer handle this type of thing, but I think it's better to have the floor handle substantial action calls.

  • If you were with the skipped player the whole time, the other players can't take the action away from them. If you tell the floor you were with that player the whole time, they're going to say it wasn't substantial action about 95% of the time.
  • If nobody realized, including the dealer, the floor might rule substantial action. In that case the floor will probably say the skipped player can only call or fold, because they didn't speak up.
  • The skipped player has to protect their own action. They aren't allowed to let the action go around the table and then say, "All right, it's my turn."
  • Tell the floor the exact order. They need the sequence to make the right ruling: "I was with this player for their action. It went check, check, check, and bet 100, but I was with this player the whole time."

The TDA defines substantial action as any two actions in turn where at least one puts chips in, or any three actions in turn. Posted blinds don't count. Rule sets vary. The definitions are also in misdeals.

Players who keep doing it

The first time, tell them to wait their turn. You can't do anything about that hand. If they do it again, now you've got an issue, and you let a supervisor know. Some rooms are just very punishing to people who act out of turn, and in tournaments, out-of-turn action can come with a penalty. A penalty means much less in a cash game, so it comes down to the room.

Folding out of turn

Facing a bet, a fold is a fold, though some house rules disagree, so know what it's like where you deal. Folding out of turn when nobody has bet yet, or folding to a check, is where rooms are all over the map. A lot of people argue it's bad etiquette, but the game permits it, and in tournaments some places give a penalty for it. If a player folds out of turn, tell them they need to wait their turn. Be clear, but not too pushy. If a player folds to a check, gets their cards back, and someone complains, call the floor and let the floor decide. Unless a player tosses their cards to you, the dealer never decides a hand is dead. That's a supervisor.

Common questions about acting out of turn

Is acting out of turn binding in poker?

Yes, if the amount to call is the same when it becomes that player's turn. If someone in between raises, the player gets a new decision.

Can a player take back a bet made out of turn?

Not if nobody in between raises. The bet stands. The dealer should keep track of the amount so it can't disappear back into the player's stack.

What if a player raises out of turn and the player before them calls?

In no limit, the raise stands. A call doesn't change the amount to call for the out-of-turn player.

What happens to the player who was skipped?

That's a floor decision. If the dealer was with that player the whole time, they almost always keep their action. If nobody noticed and enough action went by, the floor may rule substantial action and limit them to calling or folding.

Is there a penalty for acting out of turn?

In tournaments, there can be. In cash games, it's usually a warning, and a supervisor gets involved if it keeps happening.
