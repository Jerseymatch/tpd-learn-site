A dead hand means one player's cards are killed and the hand plays on without them. That's different from a misdeal, where every card comes back and the hand starts over. People in the industry mix the two terms up all the time, so get this straight first.

The second thing to understand: generally speaking, it's not for a dealer to decide that a hand is dead. There are lots of situations where it seems like you're supposed to kill the hand, and you don't. The dealer takes cards that a player folds. Almost everything else is a floor call. This page covers what kills a hand, what doesn't, and where your job as the dealer stops.

House rules change some of this. When your room has its own rule, listen to them over me.

Folds: the one the dealer handles

When a player is facing a bet and they fold their cards, or they say the word "fold," their hand is dead. If they toss their cards to you, you muck them. Folds, losing hands at the showdown and a few routine spots below are yours to handle. Anything disputed or unusual, the floor decides.

If they say fold but are still holding their cards, you can't ignore it. You also don't snatch the cards out of their hand. Say, "You said fold, you're facing a bet. I need your cards." If they resist and say they didn't mean it, call the floor. You're not the enforcer of killing hands.

Facing a check is different. Folding when you could check for free is bad etiquette, so the house can treat it differently. Most of the time you'll still take the cards. Just don't take them in a way that breaks one player per hand.

The big blind who folds by mistake

This is one of the only times that when a player folds, we don't take their cards. If nobody raised before the flop, the big blind can check and see the flop for free. They've already paid for that betting round. Sometimes the big blind thinks somebody raised when nobody did, or they didn't realize they were the big blind, and they fold. So you push their cards back to them, even if they haven't said it was a mistake.

Some players get upset when you do this. If one does, just say, "We protect the big blind as poker dealers. It's just a thing we do." But if their cards go all the way to the muck, they're dead, and you can't push them back.

The muck

In many rooms, the muck is treated like a sacred dead-hand zone. Face-down cards that touch it are dead. If a player gets their cards into the muck before anybody asked to see them, those cards aren't shown anymore. If a player insists, call the floor.

A few things that are not the muck in most casinos:

  • The dealer's hand. In most casinos, the dealer's hand isn't an extension of the muck. I've seen rooms where it was, but it's not usually the case.
  • The board. The board is not an extension of the muck.
  • A face-up hand on the muck. If a player tables their hand and it lands on the muck face up, it's still considered face up. Some places will kill it. Exposed cards covers the player who tables their hand by mistake.

Never go into the muck

As a dealer, you never go into the muck. Not to check a hand, not to answer a player's question, and not to retrieve cards that got mucked by mistake. In a casino, pulling cards out of the muck is a fireable offense. On an audition, it's a fail. If a player's live cards got mucked, call the floor.

And if your muck slides onto a player's live cards, that's always a floor call. Keep a tight, established muck so it never gets there. Game protection covers how.

What I correct in class

Reaching into the muck

A student mucks a hand, a question comes up about what it was, and their hand goes back into the muck to check. It's one of the most common things I correct. Get out of the habit now. The answer is never in the muck. It's the floor.

Killing a losing hand without signaling it

When a tabled hand loses, don't just pick it up and flip it over. Tap it on the muck first, then turn it. The tap tells the player, and the whole table, that the hand is dead.

Too many cards (or too few)

There's a responsibility on the players to speak up when they don't get the right number of cards. That's why too many cards isn't automatically a misdeal.

  • Before there's been action, an extra card can still be a misdeal. Misdeals covers that.
  • After action has begun, a player who discovers they have too many cards has a dead hand. Say it goes call, call, and then a player says, "Hey, I have three cards." Their hand is dead. It's not a misdeal. Posting a blind or a straddle doesn't count as action.
  • If you see three cards in front of a player, call a supervisor. I think most poker rooms prefer to have the supervisors make that decision, and if the player gives you any resistance, the supervisor is who handles it anyway.

At the showdown

In the rulebook I used in a real casino, a hand with too many cards for that form of poker is dead, and a dead hand can't win any part of the pot. That's true even with a straight on the board that everybody plays. A player with three cards in hold'em isn't entitled to a chop.

A hand with too few cards at the showdown may be ruled live, as long as the player still has at least one card. That's the rulebook I used, and not every room agrees. Either way, it's a floor call. The one-card showdown gets its own section in showdown disputes.

That same rulebook says it's improper for a player to discard any of their cards, even ones they don't need for their five-card hand. A player who does it on purpose is subject to having their hand ruled dead. That's a judgment call by the supervisor or shift manager, not by you.

A card that goes off the table

Players love to toss their cards in the air to show them down. Even a gentle toss can bounce one off the edge and onto the floor. When that happens, you call the floor. Some casinos say that card was never tabled and it's a dead card. Others check whether it's 100% retrievable. There's a good argument for both. Either way, a player tossing their cards carelessly is putting themselves at risk of their cards not speaking.

Address it before anything else at the showdown. If that hand turns out to be dead, one of the other hands can win, so you can't start mucking the rest. When I set this up in class, I want the first instinct to be "floor," then "what would I have done?"

A card that goes off the table on the deal is different. That's an exposed card in most rooms, and it becomes the burn. See exposed cards.

An extra seat, or an absent player

A seat that isn't in the game

In a cash game, if you accidentally deal in a seat that isn't part of the game, maybe a player who's up from the table, it's not a misdeal. You take those cards to the muck and keep the hand going. The cards are random to begin with, so nobody's hand is affected. Most houses play it this way, though it's house to house.

If you notice it after one card, you don't stop and you don't skip the seat. You give that seat its second card too, finish the deal, and then kill that hand. In my opinion, it doesn't make sense to do anything other than throw that one card in the muck, but people feel better when the seat gets a complete hand, and that's what poker rooms will expect you to know. Poker rules don't always make total sense, and we kind of just go with it.

In a tournament, the same mistake is a misdeal. Dealing in a player who wasn't supposed to be dealt in stops the deal there.

Skipping a player who should have been dealt in is not a dead hand either. That's a misdeal. Misdeals has the details.

A player who's away from the table

Dealing in a player who's away from their seat isn't a misdeal. If they're not back by the time it's their turn to act, the hand is killed.

In a cash game you don't have to rush it. If you can see them coming and they're pretty close, you can let the cards sit until they get there. Five seconds is fine. If it's going to be about twelve seconds, that's probably too long, and the table gets into an awkward silence. Some poker rooms want you to take those cards right away, and in a tournament, some rooms have you muck them immediately.

When cards land on someone's hand

A player folds and their cards fly across the table and land on another player's unprotected cards. Technically, the rule is that this can be a dead hand for the player whose cards got landed on. Supervisors hate making that ruling.

As a new dealer, for your first six months to a year, call the floor every single time something like this happens. Even if you're sure which cards are which. Once a card goes on top of other cards, we technically don't know what's what anymore. It's really unlikely, but it's possible a player did it on purpose to help a friend get a better hand. When you're more experienced, you'll understand when it's okay to move on. In a tournament, I'm always going to call the floor for this.

Tell the floor what happened, not your opinion

When the supervisor gets there, say what happened: "This player's cards were here, they were unprotected, and seat three folded and their cards landed here." Don't add, "And I'm 100% sure it's these two." If you say that and the supervisor kills the hand, which is technically the rule, you've made them look like the bad guy. If they ask whether you know which cards are which, answer them honestly. Just don't lead with your opinion. What to say to the floor covers the habit.

This is actually a cards speak rule. The only thing that entitles a player to win a pot is their cards. If you don't protect your cards, you remove the ability for your cards to speak.

Unprotected hands, and when the dealer mucks one

Protecting their hand is the player's job. Dealers aren't just trained, we're programmed to take cards that are sitting face down on the table. I've folded millions of hands over my career, two cards at a time. If a player leaves their cards loose and out in front of them, sooner or later a dealer takes them.

If you accidentally muck somebody's hand, you know what to do: call the floor. The player is going to be annoyed with you most of the time. Don't feel bad about it, and don't argue about it. A player getting their hand mucked happens every day in every casino that has a lot of tables.

What happens to the money is the supervisor's decision, not the dealer's. Since people ask, especially if you run a home game, here's how it often works nowadays:

  • The hand was unprotected. The player isn't entitled to win anything else in the pot. A lot of casinos give them back the bet they had in front of them on that street, and that's it. It didn't used to be like this. Nowadays, rooms give that bet back because the rule is a bit of a booby trap.
  • The hand was protected, with a card cover or a chip on it, and it still got mucked. When the supervisor finds out it was protected, the player gets back every dollar they put into the pot.

What I correct in class

Mucking a live hand while fixing something else

A student is sorting out a floor call or another problem, and their hands come within inches of mucking a live hand that has nothing to do with it. Your instincts about card safety have to be strong all the time, especially when your attention is somewhere else.

Not reminding a player to protect their hand

A player's cards are sitting on top of their chips, or way out in front of them. That's a good moment to remind them, kindly, to keep their cards on the table close to them with a chip on top. It's better for everyone than mucking them.

Rules that don't kill a hand

Old-school rules said that when a player broke a rule, their hand was dead. The casino industry has gotten away from that dramatically, because the penalty is a little too harsh.

  • Acting out of turn. Killing a hand is about the worst thing you can do in a poker room, short of collusion. Out-of-turn action is handled other ways. See acting out of turn.
  • Flipping cards up heads-up in a cash game. Most cash games allow it when it's just two players. Some rooms don't, and they'll tell the player to turn the cards over, not kill the hand. If there's an all-in player and two on the side, it's not heads-up. See exposed cards.
  • Cards that touch the board. The first room I worked in, in Washington State, killed a hand if the cards touched the board when the player tabled it. Every new player had to learn it the hard way. We once killed a hand in a $1,000 pot because of it. It's a spinoff of "cards that touch the muck face down are dead," stretched way too far. Everybody can see the cards. The better rule is to push them back in front of the player and teach them to table their hand properly. Until your room changes a rule like that, though, it's still the rule.

How to practice dead-hand situations

  • Set up the big blind fold. Limp it around, have the big blind fold, and push the cards back with the line: "We protect the big blind."
  • Deal a third card to one seat and let two players act before "noticing" it. Say what you'd say and call the floor.
  • Deal in an empty seat after one card, then keep going. Finish its hand and kill it at the end of the deal.
  • Toss a folded hand onto another seat's cards, then practice telling the floor exactly what happened in one or two sentences, with no opinion in it.
  • At the showdown, knock one card off the table and practice addressing it first, before you kill any other hand.

Common questions about dead hands

What is a dead hand in poker?

A dead hand is one player's cards that are killed while the hand plays on without them. A misdeal is different: every card comes back and the hand is dealt again.

Is a hand dead if it touches the muck?

Face-down cards that touch the muck are dead in many rooms. A hand that lands face up on the muck is still considered face up, though some places will kill it.

What happens if a player has three cards in Texas hold'em?

If action has started, it's a dead hand, not a misdeal. At the showdown, a hand with too many cards can't win any part of the pot. The dealer calls a supervisor.

Can a dealer kill a player's hand?

The dealer handles the routine ones: a player who folds, a losing hand at the showdown, an extra seat dealt in by mistake in a cash game. Anything disputed or unusual, like too many cards, cards landing on a hand, or a card off the table, is the floor's decision.

What happens if the dealer mucks my hand by mistake?

The dealer calls the floor. The supervisor decides what you get back. In many rooms, an unprotected hand gets back only the bet on the current street, and a protected hand gets back everything it put in.

Is your hand dead if you're not at the table?

A player away from their seat can be dealt in, but if they aren't back by the time it's their turn to act, the hand is killed.
