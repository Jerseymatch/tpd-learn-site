Tournament poker is pretty simple to deal. The action is very straightforward, and the poker itself is the same game you deal in a cash room. What's different is everything around it. Every stack gets dealt in whether the player is there or not. There are no missed blinds. An all-in means every hand gets shown. And the rules get enforced to the letter, because in a tournament, everything that happens at your table affects every player still in the event.

One warning before we start. Player tournament knowledge is sometimes vastly different from dealer tournament knowledge. If you play a lot of tournaments, you have a leg up, but don't assume you know everything. These are the details that come up when you're the one in the box.

Why tournaments are stricter

A cash game is a series of separate hands. A player can leave whenever they want. A tournament is one ongoing event. Every chip that moves at your table changes the stacks that every other player in the room is up against. So tournaments should always be strict. Too long, didn't read: follow the rule book to the letter. (I might relax for a tiny $30 tournament.)

That doesn't mean being robotic. There are small common-sense exceptions, like a player who genuinely couldn't see their cards. But you don't clarify for players or let things slide the way an experienced cash dealer sometimes does. And remember who you're protecting. A lot of tournament players want the dealer to say something when someone exposes cards, and it's really annoying to them when dealers just let it go.

The penalty is the other big difference. In a cash game, the room can't do much beyond warning a player and eventually kicking them out. In a tournament, a player who breaks the rules can get a penalty, like sitting out a round, and that actually means something because there's a limited number of hands. Whether anyone gets a penalty is always the floor's decision, never yours. Penalties and ejections covers how that works.

Before the cards go in the air

Most of the setup happens before the tournament starts, and a lot of it runs through the tablet at your table. Every poker room has its own version of the software, and it changes as the technology gets better. They should go over it with you. My one piece of advice: focus on the game first, and the tablet stuff second.

  1. Suit check. Your deck starts suited, usually with another deck in the well. Verify every card on the front, then every card on the back, before you wash it. (Deck changes and setups covers verifying and suiting a deck.)
  2. Verify every player. As players arrive, check their ticket (or whatever the room uses), their table, their seat number and their picture in the system. One of the worst situations you can have is somebody playing at a table and seat they're not supposed to be in. Every World Series, there's a story about it. Don't be the dealer who lets it happen.
  3. Hand out starting stacks. In a cash game, you want the big chips on top and in front. In a tournament, big chips always need to be visible. If you can't tell a player has big chips, the consequence is much more significant, and it might be a floor call. Starting stacks are usually organized in the bank with spacers between them. Hand each one over with the big chips on top.
  4. Set the button where they tell you. They'll tell you where the button starts. If nobody is in that seat, the button just starts dead. That's fine. You don't move it to a different seat. The seats are assigned randomly, and it starts where it starts.
  5. Wait for enough players. Rooms generally want about four players at your table before you start dealing. If you're not sure whether to start or wait, ask. Supervisors are used to that question and will answer you quickly.
  6. Get the deck ready, then wait. When they announce cards in the air, listen to their instructions. Wash a suited deck thoroughly. I like to get it to the point where I can just cut and go, with something on top of the deck to protect it while I wait.

Get the ante before you cut

Most tournaments now use a big blind ante: the big blind pays the ante for the whole table. Before you cut the deck, the ante goes in the pot. Once it's in, you cut and go. That comes from the old way, when every player put in an ante and you went around the table collecting them before the cut, hand after hand. When you're first dealing, it's okay to wait for the blinds to be out too. There's something grounding about it. When you're more experienced, the ante needs to be in before you cut. Not every room enforces that all the time, and it isn't a hard, fast rule everywhere, but it's my rule of thumb.

Making change works differently too. There are no extra chips to make change with, so you involve the players. That's in making change in a tournament. Who gets paid first when a short stack can't cover both the ante and the big blind is in big blind ante rules.

Dealing every stack, even when the player is gone

This is the biggest procedure difference from a cash game, and it's universal in tournaments.

  • Deal every stack, every hand. If there's a stack on the table, that seat gets cards, whether or not the player is there.
  • Take the blinds from absent players. For an absent player, you take the blind from their stack yourself. If you can't reach it, it's fine to have another player take the blinds out for you.
  • The arm's-reach rule. When the last card of the deal hits the button, any player who isn't within an arm's length of their seat has a dead hand. Take their cards immediately.

That last one is the one dealers forget. It's a little hard to remember to grab those cards right away, so train yourself: if a player isn't there, you take their cards the moment you finish pitching. Because here's what happens if you don't. The player slides into the big blind three seconds after the last card. Every other player at the table says, "You weren't here in time." The player has aces and insists. Now it's a floor call, and it's all because you didn't take the cards. Their hand is still dead, but now somebody has to pry it out of their hands. People have had their hands killed this way so many times that you don't want to be nice about it. You follow the rule.

Compare that to a cash game: in many raked rooms, an absent player isn't dealt in at all and gets a missed blind button. Some rooms that charge time deal them in except for the blinds. It's different room to room. In a tournament, it's the same everywhere. Cash game procedures has the cash side.

Absent players on a misdeal

Under the TDA, a redeal is an exact replay of the original deal. A player who wasn't at their seat for the original deal is dealt in again, and their hand is killed again. House rules can differ. Also under the TDA, dealing a hand to a seat that isn't supposed to get one in a tournament is a misdeal. In a cash game, that's just a dead hand. Misdeals has the details.

The button and new players

Tournament button rules are pretty universal, which makes them easier than cash.

  • There are no missed blinds. There's nothing to make up, because absent players are posting every hand.
  • The button always moves forward. If the small blind busts, the next hand has a dead button, and the button goes to the dead small blind seat. It doesn't sit still.
  • A new player gets dealt in, even on the dead button or the small blind. If a player busts and a new player or a transfer from a broken table sits down in that seat, you deal them in. That's a change from years ago, when they had to wait. A player assigned to the button seat can play right away.
  • Except between the button and the blinds. A player who sits down between the button and the blinds sits out that hand. The next hand, the button moves, and they start in the seat behind it. You can't buy the button in a tournament.

In a cash game, a player sitting down in a dead small blind seat can't just play. Straddles, missed blinds and the button has the cash rules, and the reasons behind them.

Running the hand

Pay attention to the action

If you remember one thing about tournament dealing, make it this. These players wait hours for one hand, and if you ruin that hand, it's brutal for them. If a player is counting out chips for a big bet and you bring the next card because you thought they checked, that changes their tournament, and they don't forget it. The fix is just paying attention. Tap the table before every flop, turn and river, and know who's still in. (Premature cards covers what happens when it goes wrong.)

Enforce the small rules

  • One chip with no declaration is a call. In a cash game, an experienced dealer might be lenient. In a tournament, it's a call all the time, unless a supervisor says otherwise. Enforcing it consistently matters, because it affects everybody's chips for the rest of the event. (The technical rules of betting covers the oversized chip rule.)
  • Don't toss back the extra chip. Handing a player back an accidental extra dollar is fine in a cash game. In a tournament, do it to the letter.
  • No showing cards. In a cash game, a player can often let someone behind them look. In a tournament, you say no to that. If a player is looking at their cards in a way where someone else might see, tell them to put their cards down, even if nobody's sitting next to them. If there's a chance somebody saw another player's hand, call the floor.
  • Folding face up is a floor call. In a cash game, you'd just say, "Don't do that." In a tournament, you say, "Floor," because the player might get a penalty. When you're new, call the floor every time a player exposes cards in a tournament, rather than deciding yourself. After your shift, you can ask the supervisors whether they want those calls. (Exposed cards in tournaments has more.)
  • Heads-up players can't show cards or talk about the hand, until there are only two players left in the whole tournament. One player per hand exists because every hand affects everyone still in the event. With two players left, nobody else is affected.
  • No rabbit hunting. If a player asks to see what would have come, the answer in a tournament is no.
  • Nothing bigger than a card on the felt. We don't want phones on the felt in general, but in a tournament especially, you don't want anything that could hide a card underneath it.
  • Count the stub. It's a requirement in tournaments. Rooms usually want it done within your first three hands, ideally once you get to the river. If you sat down to a suited deck on a break, that counts, because you've already verified every card. (Game protection covers counting the stub.)

Phones

Tournaments take phones in a hand seriously, because collusion would affect the whole field. In cash games, rooms are usually more relaxed unless there's a reason to suspect something. The other players police it too.

Stay quiet

Tournament dealers aren't tipped hand by hand, so chatting with the table has almost no benefit. What helps everyone is finishing the tournament. The good tournament dealers are very quiet and do their job very well. And when you talk, this almost always happens: your shuffle gets choppy, you slow down, you make everyone wait, and you miss the action. There's a little time between pushing the pot and getting the next hand out where a word or two is fine, as long as nobody's upset about the last hand and it doesn't slow you down. Talk casually only if you really understand all the dynamics at the table, and don't be the one starting conversations until you're experienced.

[[moment _SrWommPuog 4:13 | Tournament dealers: stay quiet and crank out hands]]

Don't rush

In a raked cash game, speed decides how much the room makes, so the pressure is on to crank out hands. In a tournament, the blinds keep going up, so even slow dealers finish on time. Rushing in a tournament doesn't do you any good if it causes mistakes. In the early levels, nobody's trying to get you to go super fast, and there's a lot of downtime while players register and sit down. Later, they want you a little faster. That doesn't mean we don't care: the quicker players get knocked out, the quicker tables combine. When I'm super comfortable at a tournament, I deal as fast as I can, because it's satisfying. When you're uncomfortable, that's not the time to worry about rushing. How fast a dealer should be goes deeper.

What I correct in class

Getting chatty

If you're used to cash, it's tempting to keep talking in a tournament hand. I'll demonstrate what happens: the shuffle slows down, the dealing gets choppy, and everyone waits. In a tournament, assume it's the wrong moment unless you're sure it isn't.

Stacking chips the cash way

In class I make the point every time: in cash, big chips go on top and in front. In a tournament, big chips always have to be visible.

Saying dollars

Tournament chips aren't money. With tournament denominations, it's a 20K side pot, or 20, not a "$20 side pot."

All-ins and the showdown

All-ins happen constantly in tournaments, and this is where the most important tournament-only rule lives: once a player is all in and the action is complete, every hand gets shown. I haven't heard of any exceptions anywhere in the poker world.

  • Before you bring any more cards, say, "I need to see all the hands." Sometimes players will wait for someone else to show first. Tell them it doesn't matter: all the hands have to be shown.
  • The players turn their own cards. You can't flip them over for the player.
  • It includes players who aren't all in. If two players kept betting into a side pot, they still show when the action's done. And if one player folds to a bet after the all-in, the other players still show before you run it out.
  • Protect the muck. Players who see aces will try to muck. The rule exists to prevent collusion.
  • Award the side pots from the outside in, starting with the last side pot and working back to the main pot. Remember who's eligible for which pot.
  • Chop every pot separately. If there's an odd chip in every side pot, every extra chip goes to the same player. That just is what it is.

Supervisors want every pot built in a tournament. Learn to build them properly first, and only consider shortcuts once you have about a year of experience. How to build side pots walks through it, and the showdown covers reading the hands.

Counting down an all-in

When a player calls an all-in and the other player is thinking, I suggest you start counting out the short stack. It speeds things up. Just remember your number one priority in that moment is watching what the players do. Missing an action is one of the biggest problems you could create.

Count largest denomination first, and cut it out cleanly so you can read it back at a glance. Tournament chips are usually stacked 100s in fives, 500s in fours, 1Ks and 5Ks in fives, and 25Ks in fours. How to count down bets has the full method.

The call button

For me, tournaments are the only time I really use the all-in and call buttons. In a 1-2 cash game, I rarely put out a call button. In a tournament, knowing that a second player is already in can change whether someone risks their tournament life. If one player is all in and another throws in one chip to call with more players to act, a call button tells the next player what's going on. These buttons are tools to communicate. The all-in button is a TDA recommended procedure, not a rule, and as far as I know the TDA doesn't address the call button at all. All-in rules covers using them, and what happens when a player is all in for less than a big blind.

Hand for hand at the bubble

Near the money, the tournament goes hand for hand, and once the bubble bursts, they announce that it's no longer hand for hand. This is one of those times the tournament staff runs the show. Listen to the announcements, and do exactly what the tournament director tells your table. If a player asks you how it works and you aren't 100% sure, call the floor.

That last part goes for the whole tournament. The general rule of thumb is that if there's a weird situation at your table, you call the floor. You don't try to guess. Many rooms give you a floor light on the tablet, a button marked F or S, for things that aren't urgent. Your table number shows up on the TVs, and someone comes over. Say registration has closed and you still have unused starting stacks at your table: that's a floor light, not a "Floor, table 61." Calling the floor covers the rest.

The color-up and the race-off

As the blinds go up, the smallest chips stop being useful, and the tournament takes them out of play, usually on a break. Getting rid of those leftover chips is the race-off. You won't need to know it for an audition, and it's pretty straightforward. Here's how I do it, with green chips being raced off (nowadays it's often the black ones):

  1. Color up the full sets. Each player trades every complete set of the small chips for the next denomination. The small chips usually go into a rack, and the racks hold the right amount, so you can see the full sets quickly.
  2. Verify the leftovers. Each player is left with a few remainder chips. Do a real good job verifying that no extra small chips are buried in anybody's stack. You don't want to deal with that later.
  3. Deal one card per leftover chip. Don't worry about where the button is, unless they tell you to. Start on your left and deal each player one card, face up, for each remainder chip they have. Keep the cards and chips in front of the player's stack so you remember where they go.
  4. Collect all the leftovers and total them. Say there's 225 in leftover chips. Most rooms round up at half a chip and down below it, so 225 becomes two 100 chips.
  5. Race for the new chips. High card wins the first new chip, with ties broken by suit. Suits go reverse alphabetical: spades, hearts, diamonds, clubs. Then the next highest card wins the next chip. Each player can only win once, no matter how many cards they have.
  6. Suit the deck. On a tournament break, the next thing you do is suit the deck. When you leave a table on a break, spread the suited deck on the table so the next dealer knows to use it and verify it, instead of grabbing the used deck out of the well.

The first time you do one, feel free to have a supervisor there with you to make sure. Rooms handle the details a little differently, and they'll tell you how they want it.

Down cards, pushes and the clock

A tournament "down" is your time at a table, and you're paid by the down rather than by tips at the table. Which brings us to what I'd call the most important thing in a tournament, from your perspective: sign the down card.

  • No signature, no pay. Each tournament table has a down card with time slots on it and a pen. If you don't sign it, you don't get paid for that time at the table. The way the industry looks at it, it's on you.
  • Set it beside you. If you sit down mid-hand, the dealer leaving will often hand you the pen and the down card. Don't make everybody wait while you sign. Set it aside right next to you, deal, and sign it when you have a moment. Leaving it there is annoying enough that you'll remember. Then it goes in the well.
  • Check the dealer before you. If you have extra time, look to see that the dealer before you signed. I've saved dealers from missing downs before, and they really appreciate it. If you forgot to sign, don't get depressed about it. Tell a supervisor on your break.
  • Deal one hand, get the down. Where I've dealt tournaments, as long as you deal one hand and sign, you get credit for the full down. Ask your room. If your table breaks, you also do the cleanup, which takes about five minutes or less.
  • Watch the clock. At the series I've dealt, they didn't want a dealer tapping into a table with two minutes or less left in the level. The dealer who's there deals another hand until the level changes. Both dealers should pay attention to that. Rooms handle it differently, so listen to their instructions.
  • Go straight to your next table. Whoever is at the end of that string is waiting for their break. If you have to use the restroom, do it on your break when you can. If you really can't wait, put on your floor light and someone will tap you out.

The TV screens around the room give you most of what you need: the level, the time to the next break, and often where you're going next.

Players busting out

When a player busts, you'll usually log them out of the system. Some rooms keep a set of seat cards in your tray. You put one in the empty seat, and that's how the staff knows where to send the next player. If it's a bounty tournament, you'll pass the bounty chip to the player who knocked them out.

Tips in a tournament

In a tournament, if a player tips you actual cash chips or money, it usually doesn't go in your box. It goes to the tournament pool and gets split among the downs. Some rooms don't start enforcing that until the tournament is in the money.

Think of why. Say there's $80,000 to first. All the dealers in the room have been dealing that tournament for hours. The winner gets lucky at the final table and wants to give the dealer who dealt them those couple of hands $1,000. It would be a little ridiculous for that to go to one dealer, just because it was a coincidence that they dealt the final table, with zero credit for everyone who dealt the hours before. So they pool it. Every room is a little different. Tipping in poker tournaments is the player's side of it.

Your first tournament

In some ways a tournament is easier than cash. There's no rake to take, and experienced players make the game smoother. High-dollar events feel stressful at first, but there's less change to make than you'd expect, and once the players see you know what you're doing, they gain confidence in you. The hardest part early on is multitasking: a new player sits down and needs a stack and a check-in at the same time you're supposed to get the next hand out. Get the game right first.

It's fine to tell the table it's your first day. Players are usually patient. Don't try to go fast. Speed comes with time, and rushing causes mistakes you can't recover from.

How to practice tournament procedures at home

  • Deal to empty seats. Set out stacks for players who "aren't there." Deal them in, take their blinds, and grab their cards the moment the last card hits the button.
  • Get the ante before every cut. Make it automatic, so you never pitch a hand without it.
  • Run all-in hands with three or four stacks. Stop before the next card, say "I need to see all the hands," and build every side pot.
  • Count all-ins in tournament denominations. Read them back out loud, biggest chips first.
  • Do a mock race-off. Give a few stacks some leftover small chips and race them off, start to finish. Then suit the deck.

Common questions about dealing poker tournaments

Is dealing a tournament harder than dealing a cash game?

Not really. Cash is harder to deal because of the rake and the constant change-making. Tournaments have their own procedures and stricter rules, but the action itself is very straightforward. What a tournament demands is attention to the action and enforcing the rules consistently.

Do you deal in a player who isn't at the table in a tournament?

Yes. In a tournament, you deal every stack, every hand, and take the blinds from absent players. If the player isn't within an arm's length of their seat when the last card hits the button, their hand is dead, and you take the cards right away.

Do all players have to show their cards when someone is all in?

In a tournament, yes. Once a player is all in and the action is complete, every hand that's still in gets shown, including players who aren't all in themselves. The players turn their own cards, and you protect the muck.

Can a new player play right away in a tournament?

Usually, yes, even in the small blind or dead button seat. The exception is a player who sits down between the button and the blinds. They wait one hand and start behind the button on the next.

What is a race-off in a poker tournament?

It's how the leftover small chips get removed when a denomination is colored up. Full sets get traded up, each player gets a card for each leftover chip, and the leftovers are totaled and rounded. High card by suit wins each new chip, and each player can only win one.

How do tournament dealers get paid?

Mostly by the down. You sign the down card for every down you deal, and tournament tips usually go into a pool split among the dealers. Every room is different. Poker dealer pay compares cash and tournaments.

Can players talk about a hand heads-up in a tournament?

No, not until there are only two players left in the entire tournament. Every hand affects everyone still in the event, so one player per hand applies even heads-up.
