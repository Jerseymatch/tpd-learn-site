Any time the amount to call changes, you have the responsibility to make sure every single player at the table, even one who's facing away, had a chance to hear it and realize it. You can't be lazy about all-ins, you can't be lazy about raises, and you can't be lazy about the bet that opens a betting round. There can't be mistakes. You have to be preventative and on top of it.

This is also an audition skill. Many auditions will challenge you to count down a complicated bet, because the poker room wants to know you can take whatever gets thrown at you. If you can't break down a bet cleanly, you're going to have a heck of a difficult time passing an audition. Most of what follows uses a cash game as the example, but a lot of it holds no matter what you're dealing.

[[toc]]

Announcing bets and all-ins

When a player goes all in, the first thing you do is announce "all in." If you have an all-in button, put it in front of them. Some players push their chips forward when they say it. Some don't do anything. If they don't, you've made the announcement, but it's still easy for somebody to get confused, so be preventative: "I need you to push your chips out." Never reach into a player's zone to pull their chips out yourself, even though it's convenient.

Do you count it right away?

Some casinos require you to count an all-in right away. Some don't. There's been a big back and forth in the industry about it. The TDA view is that once the dealer counts a bet down, it changes the perception of how much it is. It creates a number the players associate with it, and that might change their action. On the other side, making somebody ask slows the game down. I'm not going to jump on either side. You'll find out wherever you work.

What I teach in class as the general standard: you don't break the bet down until somebody asks when it's their turn, or somebody calls. A big stacked-up all-in can look intimidating, and breaking it down early might show the next player it's almost the same as the raise before it. The poker industry views that as influencing the result. Honestly, I think it's a little silly, but it's the standard.

One more judgment call: if a player has something like $14,000 in front of them and nobody else at the table has more than $1,000, there's no reason to break down their all-in at all. Announce it, and if somebody calls, you break down the caller's stack instead.

How to break down a bet

Your goal is to communicate exactly how much the bet is, two ways: visually and verbally. You're laying the chips out so everybody can tell how much it is just by looking, and so it's perfect for surveillance.

  1. Start with the largest denomination. Largest takes priority at all times, all the way down to the smallest. If the big chips are buried, you can get to them later, but start with them every time you can.
  2. Break each denomination down in its standard increment, and complete it. In a typical cash game, $100 chips break down in fives ($500), $25 chips in fours ($100), and $5 chips in fives (a full stack of 20 is $100). If there are only three black chips, complete the $500 increment with the next denomination and put it with them. That completion makes it a lot easier for everybody to follow.
  3. Count it out loud, touching what you're counting. Don't just announce a number. For a $700 all-in of three black chips plus greens and reds, you say "500, 600, 700." For an all-in of $96, you don't say "96." You say "20, 40, 60, 80, 90, 95, 96." Much clearer to everybody.
  4. Leave it organized. If it's heads up and all that's left is to end the hand, you can leave it broken down. If other players are still in the hand and you need the space, put it back together, but keep it organized. If you need to show it again, it takes a second, because you already counted it down.

You can use the height of a stack you've already counted to count other stacks. Twenty chips is twenty chips. In every casino I've ever been in, all chip heights are exactly the same. That's part of the beauty of how this is designed. (If you have a home game, there's a small chance your chips won't work perfectly like that.)

When you splash a few chips out to show them, hold them between your middle finger and thumb, come at the table at an angle, and let the bottom chip come out first. You don't throw them. You let the contact with the table pull them out.

Doing it with a deck in your hand

There are a lot of situations where you have to do all of this with the deck in your hand, which makes it more difficult. Practice it that way.

  • Never put the deck down unless you absolutely have to. It stays in your hand, and it can't go off the rail. It has to stay at least over the table.
  • If you do have to put it down while cards are in play, you can't pick it back up without a supervisor there. That's game protection. (A deck you shuffled and protected but never dealt from is a different situation, as long as you followed your casino's procedure.)
  • Try not to cross your arms. Sometimes you have to reach over for a far seat. That's just the way it works.
  • Clear your hands before you touch your body. Not every casino requires it, but a lot of them do.
  • It's okay to move a bet away from an obstacle to give yourself room to break it down.

Tournament all-ins

In a tournament, a 500-unit chip isn't anywhere near as important as a $500 chip, so you don't need to be as deliberate. You can go through it more quickly. Two numbers worth learning: a stack of 20 500-unit chips is 10,000, and a stack of 20 100-unit chips is 2,000. So a tournament all-in might be counted like "10,000, 12,000, 13, 14, 14,150."

What I correct in class

Breaking down an all-in nobody asked about

Right after we practice breakdowns, it's common for a student to break down an all-in the second it happens. That's understandable, because we just did it. But at a real table, when a player goes all in, you leave it stacked up until somebody asks on their turn. The other possibility is that somebody calls. Then you know for sure you're going to have to see how much the all-in is, so start counting it down right away, in your downtime, instead of waiting until the end of the betting round.

Verifying every call

Any time you work in any type of poker room, with whatever their rules are, when one person makes a bet and another person calls, you have to validate that those two amounts are correct. So you immediately break down the bet if you haven't already, you immediately confirm the amount of the call, and you get the chips in the pot correctly. That's on us as dealers.

Do it as the action comes. If a player puts out a pile of chips and says call, you can't wait until later to break it down. Say a player puts out an amount without saying anything, intending to call, and you let it sit. It might turn out to be enough to count as a raise. Now you've been treating it as a call, the action has continued, and the rest of the hand suffers because of it. That's an extreme example, but there are many other reasons, too.

And if a player throws out a lot more than the bet (50 into a bet of 30, say), clearly say "call," so the player taking a bite of their pizza looks back up and understands what happened.

The table is how players follow the hand

Players get a lot of their information about the hand by looking at what's on the table. There are plenty of times a player gets interrupted, even by casino staff. Say a player ordered a drink and it shows up 35 minutes later, right in the middle of a hand. The industry discourages waitresses from interacting with players who are in a hand, but I promise you it happens all the time. It's hard to say that's the player's fault. If they turn around and the bets in front of everybody are confusing, that's unfair. All of that needs to be handled as it's happening.

Sizing into a bet

Once a bet is counted, you can size a call into it: set a stack next to it and even out the heights. If an all-in is $125 and you already know that, you can match a stack of reds to it and add the extra chip to make the call. That's fine. But the only reason you can ever size into a bet is that you've already broken it down. If you haven't counted it and the two stacks fall over, you don't know which chips were which anymore. I see dealers take that shortcut all the time, and I've seen the mess that comes from it.

What I correct in class

Pushing two uncounted stacks together

People get confused about this. You're not supposed to put two stacks together that haven't both been counted. A lot of the time a player will initiate it themselves and try to bump their call into the bet, and as the dealer you've got to stop that. If they knock over and get intermingled at all, we're screwed. The only time it's okay is after you've broken the first bet down and know exactly what it is.

What I correct in class

Scooping bets in without verifying them

When I ask a class for the most important parts of this job, the answer I want is taking a rake correctly and verifying every single bet amount you take in. As a general rule, every single time you bring any bet into the pot, you verify it. You're not just scooping it in. It's easy to forget the moment you relax, and I'm glad I keep saying it, because students keep forgetting it.

Making change: the order that prevents mistakes

Whenever you make change for bets that have been made, go in this order:

  1. From the pot, whenever possible.
  2. From other bets in the betting round.
  3. From the bank, only when you have to.

The real reason to wait on the bank is that it prevents theft. The more times a dealer goes into the bank, the more opportunities they have to steal from the casino, and we prevent as much of that as possible. It's also more opportunities to accidentally create a variance. The bank is there because it's needed, but if it's not actually needed, we avoid it.

A basic example

1/2 no-limit, six players to the flop. Four of them called with two $1 chips. Two of them called with a $5 chip. Everyone is committed to $2, so it wouldn't be right to count their $5 toward the betting round. Take in the correct bets, so you have $8 in the middle. Take $3 out of it, give it to one $5 player in exchange for their chip. Do it again for the other one. Now everyone has contributed $2, and you bring the flop.

[[moment i71mRl_J76Y 1:34:16 | When to make change for the blinds and the calls while the action goes around]]

Always leave the chips that need change in front of the player right up until the moment you have the change for them.

Even if a player has $1 chips, if they choose to put a red chip out in a cash game, you deal with it. They might want to hold on to their $1 chips because they want to tip, and throwing a red chip to the dealer and asking "can you chop this?" feels weird to some people. If you really need them to use their change, you can ask, but in general you deal with whatever they want to do.

When the change has to come from the bank

When you're making change out of your well, the player's chip goes into the bank first, and then the change goes out. I can't tell you how many times that red chip has been left on the table, then ended up in the pot, and the bank is $5 short and the dealer gets a variance. Some casinos make dealers pay for that. Be deliberate about it, and clear your hands every time you go into the bank.

And if you can, use a chip from the pot to make the change instead of the player's own bet. You don't have to, but it's a good habit to get into.

What I correct in class

The red chip that ends up in the pot

This one is for everybody. If you make change and you don't put the red chip in your bank first, someone talks to you, something happens, and that red chip has a high chance of ending up in the pot. Now your bank is five short. It's a little counterintuitive, but bring the chip to the incoming side of your bank first, then put the change on the table. Trust me, this is from experience with dealer after dealer, myself included.

What I correct in class

Making change straight off a player's chip

A student took a player's red chip, changed it out and gave them change, and it would have been perfectly fine in an audition. But in class, I want the better habit: always go to the pot first for your red chip, and leave the $5 chip in front of the player if you can. The only time you don't is when there isn't one in the pot. Making change directly off a player's chip is the number one way dealers end up handing change to the wrong person.

Giving change

  • Don't throw the change at players. I know it's fun to throw stuff. They'll say ouch, they'll get upset, they'll go tell on you. It's a guest service thing. Be respectful, but it's a poker table, so you can put a little something behind it.
  • Say what it is. "20 is your change. 20 is your change." The more communication there, the better. And keep your pot tight.
  • You can make change during the action if you can multitask and still watch everybody's betting zone. If a player has a green chip out and they're first to act, you can change it while the others act. You don't need to wait until the end.
  • The action comes first. Whenever you have to choose between making change right now and watching the action, follow the action.
  • Never take chips out of a player's stack. Ever.

What I correct in class

Forgetting to give change

It happens. I promise you there are 100 times I've forgotten to give change over the years. Maybe 100 is the wrong number, maybe it's 40, but it's not zero. There are so many things to keep track of. The time you don't give somebody change is the time your brain is thinking about other stuff. And usually the player says something on the next street, because they were expecting you to do it as that street started.

Shortcuts, and why I don't start you with them

Once you're experienced, there are faster ways. If six players are in for $2, you know it's a $12 pot, so you know at most two red chips can stay in it. You can take all the bets in, go into the bank once, and give everyone their change. I show that so you see it exists. I don't recommend starting out that way. Do it the slow way while you're learning.

Every shortcut that's ever been created to save time is a shortcut because there's risk in it. An experienced dealer who understands the risk can stay away from it. A new dealer who learns the shortcut as part of the job is almost definitely putting themselves at risk. In the very first poker room I worked in, back in 2007, there was an experienced dealer who would get the burn cards and the board ready to go into the shuffle machine before the hand was over, to get more hands out. They only did it when nobody could possibly hit the bad beat jackpot. A new dealer copied them without understanding that, and started doing it when a bad beat was possible. The casino's procedure included verifying the burn cards and the muck, and the house wouldn't pay a jackpot if that wasn't possible. That dealer would have been the one who killed the bad beat. I was a new supervisor then, and I knew enough to stop it before it actually became a problem.

I generally tell dealers: don't take shortcuts until you've been dealing for three to six months. Get the simple, straightforward way into your instincts first. Then if you ever go too fast, you always have the basics in your back pocket.

Players with only big chips, and running out of change

Players who only have green

Every once in a while, a player comes over from table games and sits down with only green chips, and they call with a $25 chip. That's part of why you want to keep some red chips in your bank in these games. (If you have no red at all, you can technically use white. It's kind of crazy.)

In a cash game, you really want the change to come from you. If there's truly no way, you can ask a player: slide the chip over to them, expose it, and say, "Are you willing to make change for this?" If they say no, go to somebody else. Don't force them. If nobody will, you can say, "Look, guys, I need somebody to do this. I can't continue the game without it." You don't have to explain yourself.

There's one exception where it's just easier. In 1/2, when everyone called $6 with two $5 chips and needs $4 back, and a player has a big stack of $1 chips, it's okay to say, "Can you just throw me a $1 chip?" It's a judgment call you can make as the dealer. If you always go to your bank instead, I respect that completely. It's safer.

A new table with no change

Some rooms send everybody to the cashier when they start a game, everybody comes back with only red chips, and the dealer makes a ton of change for the first 30 minutes. There's no flop until all that change is given, so the game is going slower. Players love having chips, too. When I first started playing poker in 2004 and went to Foxwoods, people in the 1/2 and 2/5 games had $1,000 or $2,000 all in red.

If you're going through all your change, see if there's a chip runner who can bring some white chips over. Selling white chips to players who are okay with it is a great way to do it. If not, you'll be making a lot of change at first and you'll probably need a fill pretty quickly (that's covered in working the bank). Ideally, when people buy in, give them some small change whenever possible.

Change and tips: what's allowed

This isn't usually taught in dealer school. As a poker dealer, you are not allowed to trick people, aggressively or indirectly, into giving you more tips. That's called hustling. You're not allowed to bring the flop and then break the whole pot down into $1 chips. Why would a dealer do that? Because when you push a pot full of $1 chips, they look so good to throw to you. It's not okay. You don't need those chips for anything, and if you get caught doing it on purpose, you can get fired. It's totally not worth your job.

But here's what's completely acceptable. You're supposed to keep the rake up to date on every street. In 2/5 with six players in for $5, you make change on the flop and drop $3 into the rake, so that if someone bets and everyone folds, you can push the pot right away without making change later. Following that procedure puts $1 chips in the pot. And when you push a pot with $1 chips in it, the winner is more likely to tip you. Doing the job the right way helps you.

Making change in a tournament

In a tournament, you have to involve the players. There are no other chips available for you to make change with. (I've almost never seen a casino do it differently.)

  • The ante and blinds come first. In a tournament, every stack gets dealt in, and you need the ante squared and the blinds out before you start pitching. Have the players put it out. You don't take it off their stack.
  • Big blind ante: most tournaments now have the big blind pay the ante for the whole table, and you collect the ante before you pitch. (The old way, with an ante from every seat, you go around in order saying "antes" every hand, because they always forget.)
  • Getting change from a player: say it's 100/200 and the blinds only have 500 chips. Before you deal, take the chips you need change for, drag them over toward a player who has smaller chips, and say, "Hey, can you make change for a thousand?" If they say no, it's totally okay to ask someone else. Some players just don't like it, and some don't care.

It's fine for a player to have the wrong denomination chip out for the big blind, but the ante in the middle has to be correct, because that money has already been brought in.

Remember, you're the facilitator of a game people's emotions are tied to. Somebody might think making change for their chips is bad luck. Be polite and preventative when you make change, and don't get pushy about it unless you need to.

Should you color up a messy all-in? In general, I hate coloring up a messy all-in. Players hate feeling like you might make an error while you're doing something you don't need to do. The rare exception is a player using the wrong denomination for the game, like going all in at 1/2 no-limit with a stack of $1 chips. (How color-ups work is in buy-ins, cheque change and color-ups.)

How to practice

  • Cut chips until it's smooth. If you can shuffle and pitch but can't cut chips well, practice cutting chips. Nobody can have you at a poker table going slowly through this. Cut down a 20 stack in fives, then in fours, over and over. (The technique is in how to cut chips.)
  • Use real casino chips. Go to your local casino and buy $100 worth of $1 chips. When you're done practicing, you get all your money back. It costs you the trip, and real casino chips feel just like the ones in the poker room.
  • Challenge yourself. Make messy all-ins with mixed denominations and awkward totals ($949, $14,604), break them down, and count them out loud. Then do it with a deck in your hand.
  • Practice the change order until it's automatic: pot, other bets, bank. Chip in the bank first, then the change out.

Common questions about bets, calls and making change

Should a poker dealer count down an all-in bet?

It depends on the room. Some casinos require you to count it right away. What I teach as the standard is not breaking it down until a player asks on their turn or someone calls. Once someone calls, start counting it down immediately.

How do you count a bet as a dealer?

Start with the largest denomination, break each one into its standard increment (blacks in fives, greens in fours, reds in fives), then count it out loud while touching the chips: "500, 600, 700," not just "700."

Where should a dealer make change from?

From the pot first, then from other bets in the betting round, and from the bank only when you have to. Going into the bank less often prevents theft and variances.

When making change from the bank, what goes first?

The player's chip goes into the bank first, then the change goes out. If you put the change out first, the player's chip can easily end up in the pot, and your bank is short.

Can a dealer ask a player to make change?

In a cash game, avoid it. The change should come from you. If you truly have no way, slide the chip over and ask if they're willing, and go to someone else if they say no. In a tournament, you have to get change from players, because there's no bank.

Is it hustling to make change so there are $1 chips in the pot?

Breaking a pot into $1 chips just to get tipped is hustling, and you can get fired for it. Making change on the flop because you're keeping the rake up to date is just doing your job, even though it puts $1 chips in the pot.
