Mentally prepare before you learn the pitch, because this is the one skill you really want to learn right the first time. I've worked with plenty of dealers who learned it wrong, and once your body has trained itself to do something, unlearning it is a lot of work.

Learning it right pays you back two ways. You deal more hands per hour, which is good for you, good for the house and good for the players. And you protect your body. A pitch with a lot of wrist and arm in it, repeated thousands of times a shift, is how dealers end up with shoulder and wrist problems and things like ganglion cysts.

The setup: elbows, palms and the deck

  • Elbows in by your sides. Not winged out, not pushed forward.
  • Palms face the ceiling. You can tilt them in a little, but the deck should stay basically parallel to the table. Some dealers tilt the deck toward the players, and every card they take off has a chance of flashing. Sometimes a dealer flashes every single card, and the players who can see them don't always say anything, because they like seeing the cards.
  • A smooth, slight angle on the deck makes the pitch easier than holding it dead flat. Just don't angle it so much that anything is exposed.
  • Your index finger protects the front of the deck, but keep it low. If it rides up too high, the card bends as it comes off.
  • Your other fingers sit right down at the edge of the deck. They're what feel that you're taking exactly one card. Grip too hard or too high, and the card bends and exposes when you push it off.

What I correct in class

The pinky behind the deck

Three fingers go on the outside of the deck, and your palm controls the back. Don't tuck your pinky behind or under the deck. With a worn-in deck, that grip makes it hard to peel off one card without grabbing two. A thumb set too low causes a similar problem with sticky cards.

The primary pitch: push and open

This is the pitch I teach first, and the one you'll use for almost every card.

  1. Push the top card off a little, at an angle, with your deck-hand thumb. Not too far.
  2. Grab the upper right corner between your index finger and thumb of your pitching hand. Keep that grip loose.
  3. Find your divot. Your middle finger goes behind the card into a little spot that feels comfortable for you. Everyone's divot is a little different. This finger is what actually sends the card.
  4. Open your hand. Use the deck under the card as a little ramp, and snap your middle finger open while you release with your thumb and index finger.

That's the whole motion: push the card into your hand, and open. No wrist action. No arm motion. The follow-through is just your fingers opening. The timing of the release is the hard part, and if you grab too tight with your thumb and index finger, the card bends up when you push it.

Do not expect to get this right away. If you feel like you got it right away, congratulations, you're one of the few. Missing is part of practicing.

What I correct in class

Swinging the arm like a bat

The most common pitch correction I make is too much motion. Students move the whole arm or snap the wrist, like they're swinging a bat, when the only thing that should move is the finger kicking the card. A related one: turning the wrist sideways, which makes you miss the card with your finger. Turn your wrist up toward the ceiling and you get a flatter, more reliable contact point. It also helps keep cards from flipping.

[[shorts eDiCQgD0NK8]]

Some of my students have pitching motions that would pass an audition but that I still flag, because that much motion, over an eight-hour tournament shift, day after day, is how repetitive strain injuries happen. A lower-effort motion is better for a career.

The second grip: for distance and obstacles

Once the primary pitch is solid, learn a second one. Push the card further off the deck, grab it near the center, a little above the middle, and put your middle finger at the back of the card. Now you can project the card out of your hand without keeping it on the deck.

It's slower than the corner grip, which is why it's not the one you learn first. But you'll need it:

  • When the felt is bad and cards don't glide, so you need more distance.
  • When an obstacle, like a big stack of chips or a player's arms, blocks the straight path to a seat, and you have to take the card off and send it around.

Pitching to every seat

Get a swivel chair if you can. To pitch to the far seats, turn your body in the chair instead of reaching and twisting your arm. You won't be able to do it 100% of the time, but always try to stay as centered as possible.

Pitch to a medium distance, so each card lands in front of its player. Short-pitching, dropping cards close to you and letting players reach, is a common dealer shortcut. It causes confusion and mistakes, because you lose track of whose card is whose.

A few rules that apply to every hand:

  • The pitch starts at the small blind. Dealing the first card to the wrong seat is an automatic misdeal.
  • Don't stop and wait for a player to post their blind. Some schools teach this, and it's a mistake. Keep pitching.
  • Pitch from the short edge of the deck, not off the top or the long edge.
  • Right after the shuffle and cut, the pitch starts. No pause.
  • Never pitch from behind the rail.
  • After the last card, don't cover the top of the deck with your other hand. This habit can get you disqualified. Players should always be able to see the top of the deck.

When things go wrong during the pitch

Here's what to do in the situations that come up all the time.

[[lite zHHLZwj-Dk8]]

Verify every card

Make sure the right player secures the right card. That couldn't be more important. A perfectly good pitch sometimes gets snatched up by someone who isn't paying attention, or, unfortunately, by someone who didn't like the card they got. Verify, verify, verify. Use your peripheral vision if you need to.

A card lands in the wrong spot

If a card lands near a player's stack instead of in front of them, slide it to them. It's a courtesy, and it's part of your job.

Don't helicopter the cards

A helicopter is a card that spins up through the air. Keep your pitch down. A helicopter card is more likely to be exposed at the top of its flight, and more likely to flip when it comes down and hits something or someone's hand. Helicoptering over an obstacle is even riskier.

Something's in the way

If a player's chips or arms are blocking a seat, and you can't ask them to move back a little, bring both arms out, swivel your chair, and pitch around it with your second grip. On your awkward side, you may need to get creative: grab the card in the middle and send it sideways. If a card bounces to the wrong place, stop the pitch and ask that player to grab it. Sometimes stopping is how you keep it right.

A box card

A box card is a card that was face up in the deck. Call your supervisor. That's different from an exposed card, and you don't handle it yourself. If you've pitched a few cards past it, back up the cards so the supervisor can see where it was. Most of the time, the supervisor will kill the box card as if it never happened.

An exposed card

Any card that flashes, or lands face up, during the pitch is an exposed card. That includes a card that hits a chip stack or a player's hand and flips before it's secured.

  1. Leave the exposed card face up in front of that player and announce it. Whenever cards are face up at the table, you always say what they are.
  2. Finish pitching the rest of the cards.
  3. That player gets the next card off the deck, the one that would have been your burn card, as a replacement.
  4. The exposed card goes face up on top of the deck, you announce it, and it becomes your burn card. You'll burn it face down.

If two or more cards are exposed during the deal, that's a misdeal.

One important difference: if a player flips their own card after they've secured it, that card is not replaced. It just gets shown and turned back over.

A card goes off the table

If a card lands on the rail, that's strangely okay. If it goes off the table, it's a supervisor call, and it's treated as an exposed card. Make sure the player doesn't pick it up. That's for a supervisor or another poker room staff member to do.

You're the referee. Every sport has officials who keep the game honest. In poker, the dealer is the only person at the table who represents the poker room at all times, and you have to keep the game's integrity, even against yourself. If a card flashes, don't ignore it. If there was a chance the players saw it, it's an exposed card. That's as much a part of the job as the mechanics.

How to practice the pitch

Go back and watch the mechanics video more than once, and check yourself against it honestly. Are your elbows actually in? Is your wrist actually still? A few things that help at home:

  • Use a soft surface. Some card brands can chip or break if you pitch them onto a hard surface like wood for hours.
  • Keep the cards on the table. Several of my students roll up towels around the edges of their practice table so cards don't fly off, and they've liked it.

Common questions about pitching cards

How do poker dealers pitch cards so smoothly?

Almost all of the motion is in the fingers. The card is pushed off the deck, held loosely by the corner, and sent by the middle finger opening. The wrist and arm barely move.

Why do my cards flip over when I pitch?

Usually it's the wrist. Turn your wrist up toward the ceiling and keep it still, and check that you aren't gripping the card too tightly with your thumb and index finger.

What happens if the dealer exposes a card during the deal?

One exposed card is left face up and announced. The player gets a replacement card from the top of the deck after the pitch, and the exposed card becomes the burn card. Two or more exposed cards during the deal is a misdeal.

What is a box card in poker?

A box card is a card that was face up inside the deck. The dealer calls a supervisor, who usually kills it as if it never happened.

Where does the pitch start?

At the small blind. Starting at the wrong seat is an automatic misdeal.
