Every buy-in has only two parts: what's coming in to the dealer, and what's going out to the player. That's true whether someone hands you a single $100 bill or a wad of cash that would make you want to give them a look for slowing your game down. (Don't. It happens. It's part of the game.)

The same idea runs through cheque change and color-ups. In casinos, chips are also called cheques. Someone hands you chips, and you give them back the same amount in different chips. Learn the buy-in procedure and you've learned most of the rest.

[[toc]]

The working space: incoming left, outgoing right

Picture the area in front of your tray split down the middle of the tray. The left half is incoming: the cash or chips the player gives you. The right half is outgoing: the chips going back to the player.

Start the incoming money up and to the left. That gives you room to work when there are a lot of bills. Don't put it so far away that it isn't protected by you.

Taking the cash

  1. Don't take money out of a player's hand. Ask them to put it down on the table. That way nothing fishy can happen. And never hand cash to players.
  2. Put the cash in order by denomination, all facing the same way. If bills are out of order or facing different directions, take a second to fix it. It makes your life easier.
  3. Spread the bills one at a time. Don't spread five at once. Two bills stuck together won't feel any different until they're further onto the felt. I got lazy once and spread five at a time, an extra bill went unnoticed, and my bank came up wrong. That's why we always take it one at a time.
  4. Angle the bills so surveillance can see three points of each bill, not just two.
  5. Face down is how I teach it, because the number on the back of the bill is bigger. Some rooms want face up. If yours does, do it their way.
  6. Break bills down the way you cut chips. Hundreds, twenties, tens, fives and ones go in groups of five. After five bills, start a new spot even if it's the same denomination. Fifties go in groups of four.
  7. Different denominations never touch or stack on each other. Laying different bills on top of each other might look neat at the table, but on camera, surveillance may not be able to tell what the buy-in was.
  8. Count it out loud, or at least in your head, so everyone can follow along.

For a big buy-in, once a group of bills has been displayed and proven, you can slide it together and keep going in a braid, to save space. You've already shown how much it is.

What I correct in class

Leaving the money where the player can reach it

Keep the bills in front of you, not up by the player. Every once in a while a dealer leaves the bill near the player, does the whole buy-in, hands the chips off, and completely forgets to put the cash in the bank. If it's sitting right in front of you, you can't forget it for long. And bills stay on the incoming side only.

Building the chips going out

On the outgoing side, larger denominations go closer to you. That's casino standard. Beyond that, the goal is an intelligent arrangement: anyone looking at it should be able to read it instantly.

  • Give the largest denomination that's reasonable for the game, and read the table. In a 2/5 game, if there's plenty of red on the table, just give them green. If nobody has white chips in a 1/2 game, work some whites into the buy-in. If a player asks for a specific mix and you have it in your bank, accommodate them.
  • Separate short stacks visually. A $190 buy-in reads best as a full $100 stack close to you, then $75, with the short stack placed up top where it clearly stands apart.
  • Make it provable. When you give two full stacks of the same chip, break one down, then line the other up against it and prove it with the back of your middle fingernail. For bigger buy-ins, do that with the largest stack.
  • Lammer off your bank afterward, during the game, so it's easy for you and the next dealer.

Pushing the buy-in to the player

Pick the chips up in order, with the lower denomination on top or set next to the higher, and verify the amount one last time as you pick it up. Say it to yourself, "175… 190." It prevents all kinds of problems.

  • One or two stacks: middle finger on the outside of the chips, forefinger between them, thumb on the other side. Twist your hand a little to get comfortable, then push. If it's going to the far side of the table, switch hands.
  • A short stack to the near side: the fingernail-down grip.
  • A big row of stacks: lay your hand flat, press down on the bottom chips, and slide them over. For four stacks in a row, put your forefinger and middle finger on the two middle stacks, your thumb and ring finger on the outside stacks, touching the bottom chip of each stack, and turn your whole hand. That's a method I picked up dealing a little roulette.

Then put the cash in the well, largest bills on the inside, and clear your hands. (See working the bank for how the well is organized.)

Avoid doing a buy-in during a hand. With chips out on the table, if anything from the buy-in falls into the pot, you've jammed yourself up. Your room's rules come first, but if you can, wait. And for a huge buy-in, it's usually better to have the player go to the cashier or get a chip runner.

Cheque change and color-ups

Same working space, same principles: incoming on the left, outgoing on the right, and a readable presentation. When it's an even swap, like $275 in reds for $275 in greens, it looks nice to make the two sides mirror each other: full stacks below and closer to you, the short stack up top.

  • Hand the change out first while the player's chips are still displayed on the incoming side. If there's ever a disagreement, everyone can still see what came in. Then put their chips in your tray. When you come out of the tray with the change, come out splashing. It's slightly quicker.
  • Usually go one denomination down or up. Greens for reds, reds for whites. But think about the game: in a 2/4 limit game, a $25 chip probably becomes white chips, not red.
  • Reds go in fives. Some dealers break red chips into fours. You can sort of get away with it, but surveillance prefers a standard, and a lot of places will tell you reds always go in fives.
  • Know your bank. If someone wants to color up more than you have room for, you may have to say no and find another solution. Often you just hand them a rack and let someone else color them up. Not every room lets dealers color up at all, so ask your supervisor.

What I correct in class

Making change out of the pot in the wrong order

When you're changing a red chip into whites for the pot: the red goes to the incoming side, the whites get splashed on the outgoing side, the red goes into the bank, and then the whites go into the pot. Dealers who don't make a habit of getting that chip into the bank first constantly end up confused and accidentally leave it in the pot. Then their bank is short.

Common questions

Why don't poker dealers take money from a player's hand?

So that nothing questionable can happen in the handoff. The player puts the cash on the table, the dealer spreads it out where surveillance can see it, and the amount is clear to everyone.

How should a dealer spread cash for a buy-in?

One bill at a time, in denomination order, angled so surveillance can see three points of each bill, broken into groups of five (fifties in fours), with different denominations never touching.

What is cheque change in poker?

Exchanging a player's chips for the same amount in different denominations. In casinos, chips are also called cheques.

What is a color-up?

Exchanging smaller chips for fewer, larger chips, like reds for greens or greens for blacks. Not every room allows dealers to color up, and you need room in your bank to accept the chips.

Should a dealer do a buy-in during a hand?

It's best to avoid it, because buy-in chips can fall into the pot. Follow your room's rules.
