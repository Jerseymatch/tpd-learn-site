Generally speaking, it's not for a dealer to decide that a hand is dead. There are lots of situations that seem like you're supposed to kill the hand, and you don't. There are also a few standard misdeals that dealers call themselves, and you need to know them cold, because players often won't say anything. If you deal the first card to the wrong seat, the table may just pick up their cards and play.

The Tournament Directors Association (TDA) has effectively set the standard here, and I feel great about their rules. They're publicly available. TDA Rule 35 covers misdeals, and the room rulebooks I've worked with list most of the same things. Cash games follow most of the same ideas, with a few important differences I'll point out. Your room will have its own rules, and when it does, listen to them over me. And when you're not sure, call the floor.

[[toc]]

Misdeals the dealer calls

These happen on the deal, before there's been any real action, and you call them yourself: "Misdeal, everybody," and you bring the cards back.

  • The first card goes to the wrong seat. In hold'em, the pitch starts at the small blind. If the first card goes anywhere else, even if the player picks it up, it's a misdeal.
  • The first card off the deck, or the first card to either blind, is exposed. That includes a card that flashes. If it's possible somebody saw it on its way out of your hand, or the way it landed, we count it as an exposed card even if it's still face down. You don't go around asking whether anyone saw it. Poker players don't want to tell you if they know what somebody's card is.
[[moment -DAL7MATVzQ 1:17:59 | Is it a misdeal when a blind's second card is exposed? No: only the first two cards off the deck]] [[moment fieG5mvinQs 1:18:11 | A card flashes on the deal: say it flashed, flip it up, and call the floor if players push back]]
  • Two exposed cards on the deal. One exposed card later in the deal isn't a misdeal; it's handled the way I explain in how to pitch cards. A second one is a misdeal.
  • Two box cards. A box card is a card that's face up in the deck. More on that below.
  • Skipping a player who's in the hand. If you deal everybody but forget an eligible player, that's a misdeal. You don't fix it by giving them extra cards to catch them up.
  • Dealing out of order. Every player gets the next card off the top of the deck, in order. If a card goes to the wrong spot and you try to fix it by throwing another card over there, that's not okay. That's a misdeal.
  • A down card goes to the wrong player, and they may have looked at it. If you're not sure, it's a misdeal. That's why, as you pitch, you watch whether each player secures the correct card. If a card lands in the wrong place, move it back before you go on, so it's not confusing at all.
  • The deck wasn't properly shuffled and cut. If you realize you forgot a step, or didn't cut because you got distracted, and there hasn't been substantial action, it's a misdeal. The cut is part of the shuffle. Whenever something in the shuffle procedure isn't perfect, we start it all the way over.
  • You keep dealing into the flop. It happens: a dealer spends two hours dealing four cards in Omaha, then goes to hold'em and keeps going after everyone has two. You can't put the flop back on the deck. It's hard to do without exposing a card, and you never put more than the burn back on top of the deck without a supervisor there. That's a misdeal.

Box cards

You're pitching, everything is good, and then there's a card literally face up on the deck. If you see one, you do two things:

  1. Put it directly into the muck. (Some rooms want the supervisor to see it first and kill it themselves. If yours does, back the cards up so they can see where it was.) It is not an exposed card, and it doesn't get a spot in the deal. Treat it like it doesn't exist, like it's a piece of paper.
  2. In most rooms, call a supervisor immediately. I like that. If another box card shows up later in the hand, many rooms rule the hand dead and reconstruct the money, giving back to each player what they bet. You want a supervisor there for that. A supervisor may have you look through the rest of the deck. You never do that on your own. If your room doesn't want you to call for the first one, listen to them.

Sometimes you'll see two right away, back to back or one apart. That happens when there's no shuffle machine and somebody's hand ends up face up in the deck and gets shuffled in. Two box cards is a misdeal, and you don't need a supervisor for it: "Bring them back, there's two box cards."

What I correct in class

The first card to the wrong seat

Students do this a lot: they pitch the first card somewhere other than the small blind. It's a misdeal, and it's one you have to recognize yourself, because players often won't say anything. For perspective, so you don't feel too bad: I have definitely pitched the first card to the wrong location plenty of times. It's a weird thing that happens. Often it's because you got distracted, because someone had you in a conversation. You don't want to do it in an audition, but it's just a misdeal.

Mistakes that aren't misdeals

A lot of dealing mistakes get fixed without starting over. These are the common ones.

You deal one extra card off the top

This happens a lot when you're switching between games. If you deal one extra card off the top and you can keep track of exactly which card it is, put it back on top of the deck. That's the burn card. It's hard to get a card back on top without exposing it, but if it's the burn card and it flashes, it's not a huge deal. If that card gets intermingled with a player's cards, though, it's a misdeal. You can't be sure they'll give you back the right card, and you can't let them choose. Even if they didn't see it, they've seen their other two.

A player is short a card

Say you come all the way around and forget the button's second card, and they say, "I need one more." That's fine. Give them the next card. If you think you already gave it to them, check under the rail first.

You deal in a seat that isn't playing (cash games)

An empty seat, or a player who got up from the table: in a cash game, dealing them in is not a misdeal. Deal a complete hand to that spot and then kill it. Take those cards to the muck and keep the hand going. Most houses allow this, though it's house to house.

If you notice after only one card, you still don't skip them. You give that spot the second card, keep going, and kill the hand. I don't blame people for asking, "What the heck?" In my opinion it doesn't make sense to do anything other than throw that one card in the muck. But from the social side, it keeps people off the ledge. A full hand in that seat is something a player can accept, because theoretically somebody could have been sitting there. One card is the thing they can't let go of. Poker rules don't always make total sense, and we kind of just go with it, because this is what poker rooms are going to expect you to know.

A player who's away from the table and gets dealt in works the same way: if they're not back by the time it's their turn to act, the hand is killed.

Why it's fine to just keep going. Students ask whether an extra hand changes the cards everyone gets. It changes the order, but the whole point of the shuffle is to make everything random, and it's all still random. If extra hands were misdeals, you'd be teaching players to care about something that makes no difference mathematically, and you'd be misdealing hand after hand all year, which costs the poker room real money. We try to get more hands out, not fewer.

In a tournament, it's different

Under the TDA rules, dealing in a seat that isn't supposed to get a hand is a misdeal. A player sits down at your tournament table who shouldn't be there, you deal them in because you're used to dealing people in, and it's a misdeal. In a cash game, it's just a dead hand.

That's a good chance to clear up two terms that get mixed up all the time, even by people in the industry. A dead hand means only those cards are killed and the hand plays on. A misdeal means all the cards come back and the hand is dealt again.

A short ante

In a game with antes, if the pot is short one or more antes, it isn't a misdeal. The player who didn't ante has a live hand and owes the missing ante. If you can't tell who it was, the hand plays out with a short pot.

What I correct in class

Sliding the card over

A student ends up with one too many cards in one spot and one too few in the next, and slides the extra card over. In class, when it doesn't matter, that's something people do. In a real game in a real casino, once the cards have gone into players' hands like that, it's a misdeal, and I'm not going to let students do it in class either.

The standard is this: if nobody has looked at the cards and you know which card is which, you can fix it by moving it back. If you don't know, you don't. And "know" doesn't mean you think. It means you're 100% sure, with zero exceptions.

There's no faking it. If there's even a tiny chance a card was looked at or is out of order, a player at the table is going to tell a supervisor that you did something outside of integrity, whether or not they call you out in the moment. As a supervisor and shift manager, I can't tell you how many times players came up to me, even players I didn't know at all, to tell me what a dealer did at some table. They want the room they play in to be good.

Substantial action

Substantial action is how poker handles situations where the proper ruling no longer has an opportunity to be made. Once there's been substantial action, a misdeal can't be called. A common standard is:

  • three players taking any action, including a fold, or
  • two players acting by putting money in the pot (a bet, call or raise),

before attention is called to the error. That's the standard I've used in a live room; rule sets vary, and some count other things, like the dealer burning a card or bringing the board, as actions. That last part is really important. It's about when the dealer or someone who works in the casino found out. Posting a blind or a straddle doesn't count as an action.

The TDA's own definition (Rule 36) is a little broader: any two actions in turn where at least one of them puts chips in the pot, or any three actions in turn. Posted blinds still don't count.

[[moment W_B8yAuYz90 16:29 | Substantial action: the TDA's definition, and the gray areas]]

This puts responsibility on the players to speak up:

  • A player raises, the next player calls, another raises, another calls, and then someone says, "Hey, I didn't get any cards." In most casinos, they'd be told they should have said something. They let the action continue, so they're no longer entitled to a hand.
  • A player who discovers they have too many cards after action has begun has a dead hand. It's not a misdeal. If they give you any resistance, get a supervisor.

In some rooms the dealer is supposed to make the substantial action ruling, but it comes up rarely and the rule sets are so scattered that I want you to call the floor for it. Call the first time it comes up where you work. Once they explain how they handle it, you'll know. And if you ever see a player with three cards, call a supervisor. I think most rooms prefer the supervisor make that decision.

A bad deck

All of these are floor calls:

  • A joker in the deck, or any reason to believe you're not using a full 52-card deck: cards missing, 50 cards, 54 cards.
  • A defective deck. Say you're pitching and you notice a card is bent at the corner, and it's really obvious. The supervisor will most likely kill the hand if it's really bad, but it's a determination, not automatic. Handle it with sensitivity.
  • A card with a different back.

Once you're deep into a hand, this is basically the only kind of thing that might make you reconstruct the pot and give everybody their money back: two box cards, or finding out the deck isn't right. And you call a floor for it.

[[moment UD_pafOWTT0 1:37:00 | A faulty deck discovered in the middle of a hand]]

For anything the rules don't cover, the TDA added a recommended procedure in 2019 that lets tournament directors use randomness to design a solution. Imagine a dealer on the flop who throws up the deck, and the cards land in the muck and make a huge mess. A supervisor can use that kind of thinking to sort it out. You just call them over.

What you do when it's a misdeal

  1. Just say it. "Misdeal, everybody." Don't draw attention to it or act embarrassed. You're going to make mistakes when you deal, new or experienced, so get used to the feeling and don't stress about it. The question is "What do I do next?", not "Am I perfect?"
  2. Get all the cards back. A misdeal means every card comes back to you.
  3. Watch the muck. When cards are moving around to fix a mistake, it's easy to scoop up a live hand by accident. Keep an established muck and know which cards are dead.
  4. Drop the deck, shuffle up and deal again.

One related detail that's buried in the course: once you've dropped the deck at the end of a hand, if it turns out a player still has cards and you need to pick the deck back up to continue, in any regular poker room you call the floor first. They're there as a witness.

Under the TDA rules, the redeal is an exact replay: the button doesn't move and the limits stay the same.

The line to use

When you inevitably give pot-limit Omaha players two cards, or hold'em players four, the absolute perfect line is: "Sorry, guys, my hold'em brain kicked in." (Or "my Omaha brain.") Once you say that, there's literally nothing they can say. Instead of being upset, they understand. I've had to say it so many times.

What I correct in class

Calling a misdeal to make players happy

When players start giving you a hard time after a couple of mistakes, you can never call a misdeal unless it's actually a misdeal. If they're giving you a hard enough time, call the floor. But the real move is to just move on and not care. If you start saying "my bad, my bad, I'm not trying to do that," it becomes the thing they remember you for. You create a self-fulfilling prophecy of them thinking you're bad, and you go along with it. If you just look at them and move on, it's over.

How to practice misdeals

  • Treat every practice hand like a real game. When something goes wrong, handle it the way you would in a casino. We don't do pretend things in class. If it's a misdeal, start over.
  • Shorten your pitch while your fingers are learning. Get your arms way out there so you don't have to pitch as far. Fewer cards flash or flip, and fewer misdeals happen. How to pitch cards covers the technique.

Common questions about misdeals

What counts as a misdeal in poker?

The common ones: the first card goes to the wrong seat, the first card off the deck or the first card to either blind is exposed, two cards are exposed on the deal, two box cards, an eligible player gets skipped, or the deck wasn't properly shuffled and cut. A bad deck can also be a misdeal, but that's the floor's call.

Is one exposed card a misdeal?

Not usually. One exposed card during the deal is replaced and becomes the burn card. It's a misdeal if it's the first card off the deck or the first card to either blind, or if a second card gets exposed.

What happens if the dealer deals an empty seat?

In a cash game, it's not a misdeal. The dealer deals a full hand to that seat and kills it. In a tournament, dealing in a seat that shouldn't get a hand is a misdeal.

What if a player has too many cards?

If there's already been action, the player's hand is dead. Before any action, the dealer should call a supervisor, who may declare a misdeal.

What is substantial action?

A common standard is three players acting (including folds), or two players putting money in, before the error is brought to the dealer's attention. Rule sets vary. After that, a misdeal can't be called. Blinds and straddles don't count.

What's the difference between a misdeal and a dead hand?

A misdeal means all the cards come back and the hand is redealt. A dead hand means only one player's cards are killed, and the hand plays on without them. Dead hands covers everything that kills a hand.

What does a box card mean?

A card that's face up in the deck. It isn't treated as an exposed card and doesn't get a spot in the deal. One box card is usually a supervisor call. Two is a misdeal.
