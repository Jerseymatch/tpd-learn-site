Most of my students can break a bet down. There's a gap, though, between doing it and presenting it clearly in a live game. A player deciding whether to call 13,400 or 12,800 has to be able to read your count at a glance. And think about the player who loses a $4,900 pot. That already hurts. A dealer they couldn't follow makes it insult to injury.

It matters in your audition too. If an audition video is part of how you're getting hired, the person watching it needs to know how much the bet is, so they can feel comfortable hiring you. The basics, standard increments, saying "500, 600, 700," using stack height, are in bets, calls and making change. This guide goes deeper on the big ones: when to count, how to lay it out so it reads instantly, how to announce it, and what I correct in class.

When to count an all-in, and when not to

The standard in the industry is that you don't count down an all-in unless someone asks, or until someone calls. A stacked-up all-in can look intimidating, and breaking it down can show the next player it's almost the same as the raise before it. Honestly, I think the rule is a little silly, but it's the standard. Some casinos require you to count it right away, so do what your room says. I go into that debate on the making change page.

  • Once someone calls, start counting. When somebody calls the all-in, count down the original right away. If you need a second, say something like, "All right, let's see how much this is." When a player asks "How much is that?", I say, "Okay, let me get a count for you."
  • Watch the action while you count. If there's action behind, stop counting and watch it.
  • Count it if you're not sure it's for less. If you can see it's clearly more than the bet, you don't need to.
  • PLO cash games can be different. In some rooms, in pot-limit Omaha cash games, players prefer you count an all-in, because the next pot-sized bet depends on the exact amount. In a PLO tournament, leave it like any other all-in. See pot-limit Omaha math.

Break it down so it reads at a glance

Breakdowns are for surveillance, for the casino, for you, for verification, and for the players to feel comfortable. I learned to break down bets on table games principles. Poker gets a little lazy sometimes because it's not the house's money. Learn it this way and you'll have an advantage over experienced dealers who do it the lazy way.

  1. Largest denomination first. Finish each denomination before you start the next. Don't start with the red chips and size into it. Finish with the green first.
  2. Complete to round numbers. With a big all-in of black chips, don't just slide the green stack in next to the blacks. If there's $3,700 in black, complete the row to $4,000 with $300 in green, so it reads instantly. The same goes for smaller amounts: 950 isn't a round number. Complete it to 1,000, and put the completing chips right next to it.
  3. Stack a short stack only when it's complete. A complete short stack is five chips for black, red and white, and four for green and purple. When a short stack is incomplete, keep it splashed. Only put a spacer on a complete one. Someone glancing at your table who sees a clear spacer on top will believe it's a complete stack.
  4. With black chips and up, always splash one short stack. We don't take shortcuts there. If a player says "that was only 400 and 400" and you can't prove five and five, that's a $200 problem for the supervisor. With green chips, a mistake is $25 or $50.
  5. Prove it. Prove your stacks with the back of your fingernail, every time. That final prove is what catches a short stack of four sitting among stacks of five. The technique is in how to cut chips.

Keep it clean for surveillance

Break 135 into a stack of 100 with the 35 next to it. Stacking three high with one on top is a craps habit. Craps has a mirror for the box and poker doesn't. Modern surveillance can sometimes see stack height, but I teach the clean version until your room tells you otherwise.

Chip values to know cold

A full stack is 20 chips of one color. Reds go in fives because it's easier to read. Some dealers break reds into fours and you can kind of get away with it, but surveillance prefers a standard, so a lot of places will tell you reds always go in fives. Purple $500 chips go in fours, and some places say twos.

Cutting a 20 stack

Once you've cut out one 20 stack, size the others into it. Some dealers claim they can feel a 20 stack, but even those dealers still count out the first one to prove it, because that's casino procedure. Be certain it's 20. Don't guess.

Space and reach

  • Stand up. You can stand up as a poker dealer and make this much easier for yourself. Once it's counted, sit back down.
  • Keep it separate from the pot, and move a bet away from an obstacle if you need to.
  • On the deck side of the table, use your free hand, turn and reach. After the river you can use either hand. It's completely reasonable to ask a player to push their bet out so you can count it.

What I correct in class

Starting with the small chips

A student starts with the reds and sizes into the bet instead of finishing the greens first. Or finishes the greens at 950 and moves on. 950 isn't a round number. Complete it to 1,000 right next to it, then count up from there.

Short stacks nobody can prove

A stack of 20 reds that's never broken down makes everyone assume. Players shouldn't have to trust that you took the right amount. Prove it for them.

Spacers on incomplete stacks

A spacer tells anyone glancing at the table that the stack under it is complete. Only put one on a complete short stack.

Shortcuts with black chips

Stacking up five blacks and sizing the rest into that stack instead of cutting out a 20 stack, or not splashing one short stack. We don't take shortcuts with black chips.

Announce it so players can follow

  • Announce the groups, not just the total. Say "1,000, 1,450, 1,475, 1,485." The bet has to be communicated clearly as well as displayed clearly.
  • Announce the total, not the difference. By announcing the difference, you're creating liability on the house and yourself for getting it wrong. With a 145 bet and a 672 all-in, you don't want to do that math out loud. If a player asks "how much more?", in a room where I'm allowed to answer that, I work it out slowly with them. You're not a robot. But it's their decision whether to call.
  • Leave it displayed. After you count an all-in, leave it broken down so anyone, including surveillance or a manager, can see the amount. When it's called, bump the caller's chips right up against your counted stacks. Then tell the caller how much more you need, like 2,200 more in a tournament hand. If you need the space, put it back together, but keep it recognizable. There's not one universal answer.
  • Always announce raises and re-raises. Whether you announce ordinary bet amounts depends on the room.
  • Don't volunteer information like the minimum or maximum raise unless a player asks.

Why the display matters

A dealer at the WSOP once broke a bet down correctly and then read the wrong amount. When the breakdown is clean, a player can save themselves when a dealer misreads it. That's the whole point of laying it out properly.

Tournament all-ins

Tournament chips have their own increments: 100s in fives, 500s in fours, 1,000s and 5,000s in fives, and 25,000s in fours. Count it the same way, largest denomination first. Say a player has three 5,000 chips, a pile of 1,000s and some 500s. Splash the three 5,000s: 15. Cut the 1,000s into stacks of five: 30. Three left over: 33. Then the 500s: 34, 34-5. "15, 30, 33, 34-5." Then say it again. Cut it out cleanly so you can read it back at a glance.

Speed vs. verifying

  • Display before you grab. Dealers have a tendency to just start grabbing chips from everywhere. Show it first.
  • Don't kill the ant with a sledgehammer. Check each stack for stray chips as you build it, while your hands and attention are already on it. Don't go back and re-check every stack every time. That slows you down.
  • Don't double-touch. When an all-in amount is matched, move it straight into the pot in one motion, rather than counting it out and setting it aside.
  • Bring the counted bet over. Get in the habit of moving it into the pot area right after you count it.
  • Go at your own pace while you learn. If you do it slowly, you do it slowly. Getting quick usually takes three to six months on the job. It's usually not one month.

How to practice

  • Build awkward all-ins and count them out loud in groups. Try the amounts from my demos: 1,350, 1,359, 1,485, 4,943 and 4,970, and a big one like 14,604.
  • Do it with a deck in your hand, because at the table you often will.
  • Cut 20 stacks in fives and fours until you're certain every time. The drills are on the making change page.
  • Record yourself and rewatch. Could someone watching the video read the bet at a glance? That's the test.

Common questions about counting bets

Do poker dealers count down an all-in?

Usually not until a player asks or someone calls. Some rooms require you to count it right away, and in some PLO cash games players prefer it counted (never in tournaments). Do what your room says.

How do poker dealers count chips so fast?

Standard stacks and memorization. A full stack is always 20 chips, so once you've cut one, you size the rest into it. Four $25 chips are just 100. You don't compute it.

Do I have to be good at math to be a poker dealer?

Not beyond basic math. The math happens while you're learning. On the job it becomes memorization: the tenth time you do 15 × 7, you just know it's 105.

Should the dealer tell a player how much more it is to call?

Announce the total, not the difference. Some rooms let you help a player work out the difference if they ask, slowly and together.

Can a poker dealer stand up to count chips?

Yes. Stand up if you need the reach, then sit back down once it's counted.
