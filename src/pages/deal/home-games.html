Having a home game with friends is one of the coolest things in poker.

This page is how I'd set one up. What you need, how many players, how to deal it like a casino, how to keep it fair when there's no floor supervisor to call, and how to structure a home tournament. One thing I won't cover is charging a rake or a fee. That depends on where you live, so research the law where you are before you take anything out of a pot.

How many players you need

For Hold'em, six players is better, five is acceptable, and eight is great. Stud and draw games work fine four-handed. The higher the stakes, the fewer players you need to keep it interesting. If your Hold'em game gets short-handed, switching to Pot-Limit Omaha keeps people engaged. (How to deal Omaha covers the differences.)

What you need

A table, or felt and padding

You don't need a real poker table. Felt from a fabric store or Amazon, with padding underneath, on a dining table or a couple of folding tables, works. It's the same setup I recommend for practicing. A mouse pad is too thin. Get real padding or shelf liner under the felt.

If you do buy a table, here's what I look for:

  • Gaming suede, not speed cloth. Speed cloth burns your knuckles when you deal.
  • Felt all the way to the rail. I hate wooden racetracks. Cards and chips don't react well on them, so dealers end up floating cards and players end up lifting chips.
  • Dark green. Traditional dark green is the best background for chips and easy on the eyes. On black felt, black chips are hard to see.
  • Folding legs are a plus. And get comfortable chairs.

Cards

Use real plastic poker cards. For a home game I recommend Copag. Bridge size and poker size are both fine: poker size pitches a little smoother, and bridge size takes up less space. The most mark-resistant cards casinos use are only sold to licensed casinos.

Chips

Most of the inexpensive chip sets you'll find online do the job. A few things I've learned:

  • You can't buy real casino chips. High-end casino chip makers don't sell to individuals. That protects casinos from counterfeits and from chips being brought into their games. That's why you can't buy WSOP chips online.
  • Judge a set by a stack, not a sample. I spent good money on an imitation set that started crumbling after a year or two. My cheaper set is still fine. If you can, look at twenty of them stacked, not one chip.
  • If your game gets serious, consider custom chips. With chips anyone can buy, people can bring their own. Custom chips from a manufacturer take a while (get samples first), but nobody can add chips to the game.
  • Check for a lead warning. Casinos moved away from chips with lead years ago. If a home set comes with a lead warning, I'd consider sending it back.

Setting up a home cash game

  • Pick your stakes and buy-in. For a 50 cent and $1 game, something like a $25 to $100 buy-in works.
  • Get enough chips. For that game, about 100 fifty-cent chips and around 200 one-dollar chips, plus some fives and twenty-fives. I'd want 1,000 chips, but 500 works.
  • For a 4-8 limit game, use $2 and $4 blinds.
  • Pick a rulebook. Use the TDA rules, and post a sign that says "house rules, house has final say." More on that below.

Deal it like a casino

Deal for the button

To decide who starts on the button, deal one card face up to each player, starting on your left. The highest card gets the button. If two players tie, it goes by suit, in reverse alphabetical order: spades, then hearts, then diamonds, then clubs. In a home game where you're playing too, don't forget to deal yourself in.

Shuffle so nobody can see the cards

The shuffle you see in most home games, a couple of sloppy riffles with the cards showing, would never be allowed in a casino. Casinos accept a lot of shuffle variations, but every one of them hides the cards from the players and the dealer. If a good player can see where cards go during the shuffle, they can track them in the deal. A proper shuffle mixes the deck so nobody knows what's where, and it's very hard to manipulate. Taking a third off the bottom isn't something I'd do in a casino.

People ask me about cheap shuffle machines for family games. I've never reviewed them. Learn to shuffle instead. It's free: here's the shuffle I teach.

Do everything in the open

The biggest difference between a home game and a casino is procedure. In a casino, everything is done transparently, so that if something goes wrong, surveillance footage can show exactly what happened. That protects the player who doesn't have a single friend at the table. In a home game, friends just sort it out. You'll still run a better game if you handle the cards and chips the way a casino dealer does. Start with the pitch and side pots. Why a casino game is safer than a home game goes further.

What I correct in class

Killing more than one hand at a time

At the showdown, kill one losing hand at a time. Grab two at once and sooner or later you'll pick up the wrong hand and mix it with somebody else's cards.

Running the board before building the pots

When several players are all in and you're going to build the side pots, build them before you bring the board, not after.

Making fair calls when there's no floor

In a casino, when something goes wrong, the dealer calls the floor and the supervisor makes the ruling. In your home game, you're the floor. That's one of the hardest parts of running one, and here's how I'd handle it.

Earn their respect with correct answers

When someone asks a rules question, give them the correct answer. If you don't know, say "I don't know." Jot it down, look it up, and come back with the answer next game. Over time, the players learn that you'll handle every situation with integrity. Print a sign that says "house rules, house has final say" and put it on the wall.

When you don't know the rule

Say so first. Then talk it through, and give the players a chance to weigh in. Here's a trick poker supervisors use in casinos all the time: in the short term, your goal is to find a solution everybody at the table is happy with. If you can, that's the answer for tonight. If you can't, you make the decision. Either way, research it so you know the real answer next time.

A call nobody's sure about

Say a player bets 100, and the next player says something that sounds like "call." You repeat "call," nobody corrects you, the bettor turns over his hand, and now the other player says he never called. In a home game among friends, I'd own my part: "I should have gotten clarification, and I didn't. I thought you said call, but I can't confirm it. Are you willing to put the money in?" If it's a game where you're taking a rake, that's different. You make the ruling. Either way, never pay for a mistake out of your own pocket.

Ease your friends into the rules

Casino players are expected to know the rules. Home-game players aren't, so hold them less accountable. If a friend says "I see your 15 and raise you," I'd call attention to it, playfully, and teach them. If it happens again, I'd remind them. After that, I'd tell them that eventually we're going to start enforcing it. That's how you ease people into the rules. Verbal declarations and the etiquette guide are good pages to send them.

Dealer mistakes are part of the game

When a dealer makes a mistake in a good poker room, one of the first things the supervisor says is, "I understand, but dealers do make mistakes, and it's part of the game." The rules exist to handle it when things go wrong. If you teach your friends that every mistake is the dealer's fault, you're training them to expect a perfect dealer, and that's impossible.

New rules: test them before you commit

If you add something like a shot clock, the rule was known in advance, which counts for a lot. But keep judging whether it's doing what you wanted it to do. The TDA tries out new ideas as recommended procedures before it makes them rules. You can do the same thing.

Running a home tournament

Starting stacks

For eight players, a 10,000 starting stack works: eight 1,000 chips, two 500s and ten 100s. Add 5,000 chips if you want to start at 15,000 or 20,000. Be careful going deep, though. A 20,000 stack with 25-50 blinds is monstrously deep.

Do the blind math

Before you set the structure, walk the blind levels forward. Figure out where everybody will be in an hour, and set your level length to match how long you want the tournament to last. If you use a big blind ante, it replaces everyone anteing: the big blind puts in the ante for the whole table, and it's the same money in the pot.

Re-entry, rebuy or add-on

  • Re-entry treats a busted player as a brand-new entrant, with a new random seat. It's what casinos do now, and it's my favorite because it's simple.
  • A rebuy usually goes 100% into the prize pool, with whatever structure you announced.
  • An add-on is a one-time purchase, often at the end of registration.

Whatever you choose, the key is to announce it in advance.

Payouts

Search for poker tournament payout charts, and pick one that fits the number of players you have. Some charts make no sense for a small field, so check that yours does.

If you run a home tournament, all-in rules and side pots are the two pages worth knowing cold. All-ins are constant in tournaments.

Dealing home games as practice

If you're learning to deal, home games are phenomenally helpful practice. You get used to dealing to real people. The catch is that nobody corrects you, so it's easy to build bad habits, and dealing home games alone usually isn't enough to pass a casino audition. How to practice dealing at home covers how to use them alongside correct practice.

Common questions about home poker games

How many players do you need for a home poker game?

For Hold'em, six is better, five works and eight is great. Stud and draw games are fine with four.

How many chips do you need for a home game?

For a 50 cent and $1 cash game, about 100 fifty-cent chips and 200 one-dollar chips, plus some fives and twenty-fives. I'd want 1,000 chips total, but a 500-chip set works.

What's a good starting stack for a home tournament?

10,000 chips works for eight players: eight 1,000s, two 500s and ten 100s. Then walk the blind levels forward and set your level length to match.

Who deals first in a home poker game?

Deal one card face up to each player. Highest card gets the button. Ties go by suit: spades, hearts, diamonds, clubs.

Can you buy casino poker chips for a home game?

Not really. The companies that make casino chips only sell to casinos, to prevent counterfeits. Buy a good home set, or order custom chips.

Can you rake a home poker game?

It depends on where you live. I won't advise on the law. Research the rules where you are before you take anything out of a pot.

Should a casino dealer host home games?

That's up to you, but think about your license. When I was licensed in Florida, I didn't host home games. Florida is strict about them, and I didn't want to risk my gaming license.
