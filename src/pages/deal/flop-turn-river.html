Every hand of hold'em that goes to the river, you bring cards to the board three times: the flop, the turn and the river. Each time it's the same routine. Bring the bets in, tap the table, burn a card, bring the card. It sounds simple, and it is. But a clean board with a little felt between every card looks professional, and a burn card that flashes its corner to seat 9 is a game protection problem.

There's more than one correct way to burn and to bring a flop. I teach the way I do because I think presentation matters. If your supervisor tells you to do it a different way, do exactly what you're told.

Step 1: Bring the bets in and tap the table

Once all the action on a betting round is complete and you've brought the bets to the middle, tap the table before you touch the next card. Don't slap it, don't hammer it. Tap it just loud enough that everybody hears that cards are coming. I teach a quick two taps, with an open palm.

The reason is that somebody may not have acted, and the rest of the table might not realize it. Think about how often the big blind is last to act preflop and all they have to do is check. Sometimes you think they checked, or you look up and realize they didn't. The tap gets everyone's attention for a second: if you haven't acted yet, cards are coming.

  • Volume: loud enough to hear in a noisy poker room, not so loud it wakes anybody up. Smack the table and someone is going to spill coffee on themselves. The World Series especially hates a loud tap.
  • Tap right where you're going to burn. If your hand goes all the way out to the side of the bank to tap and then has to come all the way back to burn, that's wasted motion. Tap next to where the burn card is going.
  • Say the number of players, not "flop." As you tap and burn, announce how many players are in the hand: "Four players." Don't say "here we go to the flop." Everyone knows it's the flop. Act like you've been there before.

Tap even when the players are all in. It isn't required, but it gives anyone still in the hand a chance to stop you before the next card comes.

What I correct in class

Skipping the tap

Forgetting to tap is one of the corrections I give most: students go straight to burning the card, or tap before the flop and forget before the turn and river. The tap tells everybody the action on this round is over. It has to become a habit you don't think about, so practice the whole sequence (tap, burn, bring) as one motion until it is one.

Step 2: Burn a card nobody can see

Before every street, the top card of the deck goes away face down. That's the burn card. We burn because we don't want anybody to be able to know what the top card of the deck is. If a player had managed to put a tiny mark on a card, say a barely visible line on the ace of spades, they could tell when it was sitting on top of the deck. Burning a card takes that chance away.

That means the burn is only worth anything if nobody sees the card. So we go to great lengths to protect it:

  1. With your deck hand, push the top card out a little with your thumb.
  2. Bring your other hand over the deck so your index finger goes underneath the card and your thumb is on top, with your hand open against the card. Grab it at the top corner. When you grab it there, nobody can see through your hand.
  3. Get the deck down close to the felt and pull the card down. Your hand protects it the whole way.
  4. Slide it under the pot. It stays face down and covered from that point on.

The common mistake is to grab the burn card the same way you'll grab the flop cards, from the middle of the edge or the bottom corner. Then the card bends a little on the way down and the corner shows. The players on that side of the table can see it, and they aren't going to say anything.

And never leave a card half pulled off the deck while you stop to think. If you're not ready to burn yet, keep the card on the deck.

What I correct in class

Flashing the burn card (I've done it too)

Exposing the burn card is a correction I give a lot. I'll admit something: a player once told me, "I can see your burn cards." Embarrassing. I teach this correctly, but habits creep in, so I had to ask myself what I was working on, and I fixed my burns. You can be quick with it. You can't be lazy with it. Burn from up high on the corner, with your hand covering the card, and it can't be seen.

Where the burn cards go

  • Under the pot, under at least one chip. Say a player goes to show down their hand and one card lands face down on the table. If the burn cards are lying loose, you might not be able to tell which card is which. With a chip on top of the burns, it's always clear. Watch for this while you're making change: dealers pull every chip off the burn cards to make change and leave them uncovered. Always leave at least one chip on them.
  • Stair-stepped. Each new burn goes under the previous one, stepping down toward you, not on top of it and not squared up in a pile. Stair-stepped, anyone can see at a glance how many burn cards there are.
  • Separate from the board and the muck. Board cards should never touch the pot or the burn cards, and the burns should never drift into the muck.
[[moment h4oNTRkLXho 1:27:21 | Burn cards vs the muck: burns under the pot and stair-stepped, the muck in its own pile]]

A shortcut I use when I think a pot is going to get complicated: when I bring the bets in, I put the odd remainder of the pot on the burn card. If the pot is $530, the $30 goes on the burn and the $500 is the round part I have to remember. You don't have to do it, but if you do, keep updating it each street.

Step 3: Bring the flop

  1. Take the top card with your index finger underneath and your thumb on top, and bring it down to the felt. Do it again, landing the second card roughly on top of the first. If it's a little up or down, that's okay. Same thing with the third card.
  2. Square the three cards up: thumb on one side, index finger on top, other fingers on the outside. You can bring your thumb to the bottom for a moment.
  3. Put your thumb right on the edge of the three cards and pull them out. They come out in a straight line.

That's slightly slower than the fastest possible way to bring a flop. I teach it because the presentation is clean, and once you have it, it's plenty fast.

Always leave a little felt between each card. Sometimes you see an inexperienced dealer bring a flop where the cards are touching or overlapping. That makes it harder for everyone at the table to see what's there. In the dealer world, we take pride in having the board in a neat line with a little felt between every card. If a card comes out a little crooked, bring them out and then fix them.

The turn and river get the same gap as the flop cards. Every card on the board is spaced evenly, about an eighth to a quarter of an inch apart, even on felts that print separate boxes.

[[moment 4N3G8b5pvgc 45:00 | Board spacing: the turn and river get the same gap as the flop]]

Where to put the flop

Leave yourself room for the turn and river. Keep the pot, with the burn card under it, slightly to your left, and start the flop next to the burn card. If the flop starts out on the right, the board ends up lopsided once the turn and river come. I've seen dealers start the flop so far over that the turn and river land on the shuffle machine. It looks terrible. When the river comes, the board should look centered for everyone.

What I correct in class

A sloppy board when the hand gets busy

I see it a lot: the board looks great early in the hand, then there's an all-in to deal with, or the dealer gets distracted, and by the river the cards are bunched together, touching each other, or touching the burn cards. Keeping the board looking clean all the time is as fundamental as the shuffle. It has to be instinctual, so it happens even when your head is somewhere else.

Right after the flop

The flop is out, and now your eyes go to the first player to act. Follow the action first. While you're doing that, take the rake if there's rake to take: bring the flop, then take the rake, and keep updating it on every street so you're never stuck figuring it out at the end of the hand. (The full sequence is in how to deal a hand of Texas hold'em.)

The same idea applies on later streets. If you want to tidy up the pot, bring the next card first and fix the pot after, so the action keeps moving. More hands out is more money for everyone, you included.

Step 4: The turn and the river

When the action on the flop is complete, bring the bets in and do it all again: tap the table gently, burn a card the same protected way (stair-stepped under the last burn), and bring the turn.

To bring the turn, push the card out and turn your hand upside down, so your index finger is on top and your thumb is on the top corner, underneath. Flip it up, then drag it over into place next to the flop. Plenty of dealers will tell you to bring the card right to its spot. That's fine. I like getting the card visible as quickly as possible and then sliding it into a neat position, because I like the way it looks. Both are right, unless your supervisor says to do it one specific way. Just keep in mind that every extra slide costs a little time, over and over, every hand. If you slide, bring the card down close to where it's going to end up.

The river is the same again: bets in, tap, burn, bring.

And never peek at the turn or river before you put it out. Players first, dealers second. Bring it out in one motion, with no pause.

[[moment Sf3BKrj43Gc 1:53:28 | Never look at the turn or river before you put it out]]

Preflop is the exception to any shortcut. Some experienced dealers, in rooms that allow it, bring the turn or river first and then collect the bets. Never do that preflop. Preflop, you always bring every bet in before you tap and burn. And until you've been dealing a while, just bring the bets in first every time.

And make sure the action really is complete before you bring the next card. Honestly, the most annoying thing in the world is when a dealer brings a premature river because they missed that someone still had $10 left in seat 4.

After the river: drop the deck and protect the stub

Once the river is out, you don't need the deck anymore, so you drop it:

  • Grab the deck with your pitching hand without covering the top too much, slide the cut card off while keeping everything low, and set it down. Then protect it.
  • The other common way, which I believe the World Series made popular, is to switch hands, pull the cut card out, spread the stub, and cover it.

Either way, the stub stays intact and covered. If a player's cards hit it on the way to the muck, cards can spin off and get mixed up, and you won't know which card is which. Keep it intact in case something went wrong and you need it. Maybe you think you just brought the river and it was actually the turn. That happens.

What happens to the burn cards

Once the river is out, it's standard to take the burn cards out and put them in the muck. A lot of high-end procedure manuals say to do it as soon as the river comes, and that's true in tournaments too. Some rooms do it differently. My preference is that you always pull them before you push the pot, so you can push the whole pot in one motion.

The exception is mixed games. In stud and draw games, the burn cards also tell everyone what betting round you're on, so you don't muck them.

Don't clean up the board itself until after you've pushed the pot.

Counting the stub

If there's no shuffle machine counting the deck for you, at least once a down you need to verify that all 52 cards are there. You count the stub, the cards left after the hand is dealt. Do it at the end of a hand that goes to the river, usually while players are betting on the river, and do it in one of your first few hands at the table. You don't want to find out you're short a card later in your down. Once per down is the norm in most places. The official policy at the WSOP and Texas Card House is twice: once in the first few hands and once in the last few.

[[lite hWr3Dtr2ryw]]

The math for a 10-handed hand that went to the river:

  • 10 players × 2 cards = 20
  • 3 burn cards + 5 board cards = 8
  • 52 − 28 = 24 cards in the stub

Drop the stub off the deck in counts of eight. 24 is three eight-counts, and counting in eights makes it easy to keep your place if you get distracted. Nine players dealt in means three eight-counts with two left over. Eight players, four left over. If the hand ended on the turn and you need to count anyway, add the burn and the river that never came out: two extra.

Count silently. Players don't want to hear you counting out loud while there's action going on. And if your count comes up wrong, don't announce it to the table.

What I correct in class

"I'll count it next time"

I once asked a student whether they count the stub, and the answer was "sometimes." Here's why I care: a card can go missing in ways you'd never expect, like getting mixed in with cut cards left standing in the well. I count the stub every single time. Weird stuff happens, and counting the stub is how you catch it.

When something goes wrong

  • You forgot to burn. You don't take the cards back. You always burn before you put cards out, so if you didn't, that's a floor call.
  • You dealt one extra card off the top. This happens, especially when you're switching between games. If it's exactly one card and you know 100% which one it is, put it back on top. That's the burn card. If you're not sure which card was supposed to be the burn, don't guess: call a misdeal.
  • A card flips face up when you go to burn it. Treat it like a normal part of the game. Announce it ("Jack of spades is the burn"), turn it face down, burn it, and keep going. If you drop your head and make a big deal of it, the table will too. Rooms handle this a little differently, so learn yours.
  • You bring a card before the betting round is complete. That's a premature card. Call the floor, even if it seems obvious, and even if a player objects. The supervisor works out whether there was substantial action and handles it from there.
  • Draw and stud games. The hardest habit to break in mixed games is the instinct to bring a flop after the bets come in, because you've done tap, burn, flop so many times. The second most common mistake there is forgetting to burn.
[[moment W_B8yAuYz90 19:46 | A premature card: the dealer calls the floor, and the supervisor handles it]]

How to practice

  • Burn 10 cards in a row from the top corner. When a student in my class was flashing the burn, I had them burn 10 in a row right then.
  • Bring the flop over and over until it's smooth. Then do the whole street: bets in, tap, "three players," burn, flop. Then turn, then river, then drop the deck.
  • Practice board position with a pot and burn card in place, until the river always lands centered without you thinking about it.
  • Count stubs. Pitch out 8, 9 or 10 hands, run the board, and count the stub in eights.
  • Record yourself dealing a hand and watch it back. Every time you see a habit you don't want, stop the video and fix it with reps until it's in your instincts. That's how my students get better quickly.

Common questions about the flop, turn and river

Why do poker dealers burn a card?

So nobody can know what the next card is. If someone had marked a card, they could spot it sitting on top of the deck. Burning the top card before every street takes that chance away, as long as nobody sees the burn card.

How many burn cards are there in Texas hold'em?

Three in a hand that goes to the river: one before the flop, one before the turn and one before the river.

Why does the dealer tap the table before the flop?

To let everyone know cards are coming. If someone hasn't acted yet, like a big blind who still needs to check, the tap gives them a chance to speak up before the flop comes out.

Where does the burn card go?

Face down under the pot, under at least one chip, with each new burn stair-stepped under the last. At the river, it's standard to take them out and muck them before you push the pot, except in mixed games.

What happens if the dealer forgets to burn a card?

The dealer doesn't take the cards back. It's a floor call, and the floor decides how to fix it.

How often should a dealer count the stub?

At least once per down, in one of your first few hands, whenever there's no shuffle machine counting the deck for you. Some rooms, including the WSOP and Texas Card House, officially want it twice: in the first few hands and the last few.
