A poker table doesn't use one deck. It uses two: one in play and one in the well. Every so often a setup gets swapped for a new one, a suited deck shows up at your table, a game opens or a game breaks. Each of those moments has a small procedure, and doing it cleanly is part of looking like a professional.

The number one reason to learn to spread a deck cleanly: when a table is about to open, you have to spread a suited deck face up so everyone can see it. That's what this video shows.

The short answer

  • A setup is two decks with different-colored backs, one dark and one light. Each poker table uses two decks, so they come in pairs.
  • Poker cards are plastic. Table games use paper cards and change them about every four hours. Plastic poker decks last much longer, and rooms rotate them between tables.
  • A suited deck gets checked before it's used. Front first (every card is there), then the back (no card is marked). Then you do a full wash.
  • Decks get suited on tournament breaks and when a game closes, so the next dealer can verify them.
  • A dead spread is a dealer sitting at an empty table with a bank, ready to open a game the moment there are players.

What a setup is (and why poker uses two decks)

Cards come in a two-deck "setup," with one dark back and one light back, because each poker table uses two decks. While you deal with one, the other is sitting in the well. When you tap into a table, you take the deck out of the well and use that one, and the other one goes back in.

Poker size versus bridge size is personal preference, and rooms use both.

How often poker rooms change decks

Table games use paper cards and change them about every four hours. Poker uses plastic. At the 29-table room I helped open in Florida, we kept 45 to 60 setups in circulation, for up to 90 days. A setup starts to feel worse after roughly 20 to 60 hours of play, so you rotate them, and the same deck isn't always on the same table.

Old cards cost the room money, too. Worn, sticky cards slow the dealer down. Slower dealers mean fewer hands, and in a raked game that means less rake. Big casinos' budget processes don't always see that. Smaller card rooms tend to fix it faster.

"The cards suck." What to tell the supervisor

Nothing. A professional dealer figures out how to shuffle and pitch whatever cards they're given. I've dealt great decks and terrible decks in the same rooms. You can ask for better equipment, but blaming management only hurts the relationship. The supervisors already know, and new cards take time.

When a player asks for a new deck

Players ask for new setups to change their luck all the time, and rooms often change the deck after a quads-over-quads hand. That comes from table games, where managers would superstitiously change the cards or the dice after a hot streak. It carried over into poker, but it isn't real. A new deck doesn't change anyone's luck.

It isn't free, either. Every request means a supervisor going to get a new setup, and the dealer verifying it before it can go into play.

Checking a suited deck

A suited deck is one in order, suit by suit. When people in a poker room say "new deck," that usually just means a suited one. Any time you have a suited deck, you can't just start dealing. Here's the procedure:

  1. Check whether the deck is suited. Every time you sit down at a table, look.
  2. Spread it face up. Verify that every single card is there. I usually start with the ace of spades and go the other way, but technically it doesn't matter. What matters is that every card is visible. You verify by looking, not by touching each card, so spread it in a way where you can see them all.
  3. Scoop it up, turn it over and check the backs. Spread it face down and look at every card's back, in the glare of the light, to make sure none is marked.
  4. Do the full wash, then go into your shuffle. (The shuffle guide has the full wash and the mini wash.)

Old shuffle machines sometimes left a half-moon mark on the cards that looked like a fingernail mark. If every card has it, it's no concern. What you're looking for is a card you could pick out from the back. If you find one, take that deck out of play and call the floor. Brand-new decks, fresh out of the box, need extra checks, because misprints happen.

This is a habit dealers get lazy about when their supervisors don't care. Don't. More on marked cards is in game protection and surveillance.

Even a deck that isn't suited gets a quick look

When you tap into a table and the deck isn't suited, still give it a quick spread and do your wash on the first hand. Every once in a while a card from the other deck gets mixed in, and then it can't be a valid deck. The first hand is when you find out.

Leaving a suited deck for the next dealer

If you're the dealer leaving and there's a suited deck in the well, take the suited deck out and spread it on the table, so the incoming dealer knows to use it. Otherwise they might pull it out and start shuffling or washing it without verifying it first.

How to spread a deck

Put your thumb on the back side of the deck, with your index and ring fingers on the other side for control. How far the cards spread comes from how much pressure you put with your thumb. You get the feel for it quickly. To flip a spread face up, keep it tight, flip it with a little "oomph," and have your other hand waiting at the far end to catch it, so the last cards don't fly.

To pick a spread back up, don't gather it card by card. Slide your other hand under the end, pick up the bottom card, and scoop the whole thing at once.

Honestly, it doesn't matter what your technique is. If you can spread a deck, you can spread a deck, and nobody cares how you did it. But it has to look clean, because the first time you open a table, everyone's watching you do it.

What I correct in class

  • A messy or backwards spread. I've had students who couldn't spread a deck for two weeks, and students who could deal pot-limit Omaha but couldn't spread a deck. I teach the grip step by step: center your thumb on the side of the deck, come up higher with your whole hand, and keep the deck on the correct side.
  • Spreading the wrong way with the left hand. If you spread with your left hand, the spread has to go the opposite direction.
  • Touching every card to verify it (I've seen it more than once). You verify a suited deck by looking. Spread it so every card shows.
  • Skipping the first-hand wash. Your first hand when you tap into a table is when you do the full wash. Not your second hand, not your third. A brand-new suited deck gets one too.
  • A first-hand wash that's too short. Wash longer, with some energy, as if the deck started out suited.
  • Spreading for show in a video for a poker room. Personality is welcome in some settings. In a video meant to impress a poker room, a cleaner, more standard spread is better.

How to suit a deck

Suiting a deck means putting it back in order, suit by suit. You do it on tournament breaks and when a table closes, so the next dealer can verify it.

There are faster ways, but here's how I do it. Throw the hearts and the spades into the middle, and keep the clubs and the diamonds in your hand. Then pull each suit from the king down: king of diamonds, queen, jack, ten, all the way down to the ace, then the clubs the same way, then the hearts and the spades. That gives you diamonds, clubs, hearts, spades: red, black, red, black.

The fastest method is to spread the deck and pull each suit in order by eye. Under a minute is respectable. Once you start suiting decks as a dealer, it gets so ingrained that you just keep going.

Tournament breaks

In a tournament, decks get suited every break. On a color-up break, you race off the chips first, and then the next thing you do is suit the deck. So when you come back from a break, you'll often find a suited deck at your table. And if the clock says there's a break in 15 minutes, you're going to suit the deck anyway, so don't stress about counting the stub on that last hand. It's still a good habit to train.

Opening a table

When you open a new table, the deck will already be suited. Verify every card on the front, scoop them up, turn the deck over and verify every back. Each poker room also has its own little opening details, and some will have you do a couple of other things. That's room-specific, and they'll go over it with you.

Then you deal for the button. In a cash game, only count the players who are actually there. Deal a card face up to each player, starting on your left, and the high card gets the button. Suits don't rank in poker, except for the button and in stud. For a tie, it goes reverse alphabetical: spades, hearts, diamonds, clubs. The full button procedure is in straddles, missed blinds and the button.

Closing a table

When a game breaks, the dealer typically does three things:

  1. Suit the deck, so it's ready to be verified the next time it's used.
  2. Take care of the bank. Some rooms count down the bank and slip it. Others bring it back to its starting stacks, its "impress," which can mean getting a fill. (More in working the poker bank.)
  3. Clean up, cup holders included.

What a dead spread is

A dead spread is a dealer sitting at an empty table with a bank, ready to open a game the moment it's ready. It's especially useful when the break room is far from the poker room.

New dealers tend to resent dead spreads, and I get it. Your string of tables is broken up by a table with no players, and without perspective from a harder job, being told to sit there feels like a waste. As a shift manager, I saw them as necessary. But a director who wants one open at all times is just going to anger the dealers.

Poker has far fewer dead spreads than table games. Part of that is that poker dealers complain about them. Part of it is that every poker table is the same, so you can open whatever game is next on one table as players arrive.

A dead spread is also the one place I like a fancy card spread, like the heart spread. Some supervisors don't like it. I'd never do it during a game.

How to practice

  • Spread a deck face up, then face down, until every card shows and you can scoop it up in one motion.
  • Suit a deck and time it. Aim for under a minute.
  • Run the opening procedure from start to finish: suited deck, verify the front, verify the back, full wash, shuffle, deal for the button. Record it and watch it back. (More drills are in how to practice dealing at home.)
