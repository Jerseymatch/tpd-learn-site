In a lot of poker rooms now, the first hand you deal when you tap into a table isn't a regular hand. It's a double-board bomb pot. Everyone who's playing puts out an ante, there's no preflop action, and you bring two boards instead of one. Each board is worth half the pot. In a 1/2 no-limit hold'em game, that's usually a $5 hold'em bomb pot. In a 2/5 no-limit game or a pot-limit Omaha game, you'll often see a PLO double-board bomb pot, and in the games I deal it in, that's a $15 ante. Lower-stakes PLO games might use a smaller one, and the bigger the game, the bigger the ante.

There's no universal way to deal these. Some of the top people in the industry have asked me whether I've made any content on bomb pots, because there's no real standard. What I'm teaching here is my recommended procedure, and most of it is what we do at Texas Card House. If the poker room you work at does it differently, follow what they do. That's totally fine.

Step 1: Sit down and collect the antes

When you sit down, the previous hand was whatever the regular game is. The players are in the middle of conversations and they've forgotten it's a new dealer. So I say hi to everybody and I say, "Bomb pot, everyone." I'm creating some small talk around the idea of "put your five bucks out," so I'm not waiting on them.

  1. Swap the deck and shuffle up to the cut. I go riffle, riffle, box, riffle, and then stop before the cut. That's the old-school tournament way, from when we used to collect an ante from every single player. It isn't a universal procedure. It's just what I like.
  2. Collect the antes going around in one direction. It doesn't matter which direction. Going one way is what makes sure I've gotten the ante from everybody. When I get to someone who hasn't paid, there's no confusion: I've taken from these three players, and I can show the chips if they say they already put it out.
  3. Ask, casually, if a player hasn't put an ante out. In the room I work at, the bomb pot isn't a required hand. We give players the option. "Would you like to play the bomb pot?" If they're not at the table, we assume they're out. I once had a player tell me, "Don't ever ask me again. I never play bomb pots." I said I'd never ask him again. You don't need to make a big deal of it.
  4. Stack the antes as you collect them. This is a split-pot hand, because each board gets half, so I stack the pot as I go.
  5. Finish the shuffle and cut. If I'm not sure whether I did my riffles, I do them again. Even if it's 1% that I didn't, you don't want to deal out an unshuffled deck.

In a PLO bomb pot, you also have to keep track of the pot from the start. Fifteen times six sounds like math. After a short time dealing these games, it isn't. It's memory, just like 10 times 8 is 80. Nine times 15 is 135, and I don't have to think about it anymore.

When only some of the table is playing

Say there are nine players at the table and six are in the bomb pot. With antes already collected, it can be hard to remember who's in. So sometimes I break procedure in the interest of game protection: I deal the cards first, counting the players in my head, then go around and collect the antes in that same order. If one player's chip isn't there anymore when I reach for it, I just say, "Oh, I need that red chip back." You don't need to accuse anybody of anything. That's according to my rules, and I don't work at your casino, so check with your room.

Where the button goes

A bomb pot is a separate hand within the same game. In a lot of rooms, and the way I like to do it in a 1/2 game to keep it simple, the button stays exactly where it was for the regular game. You don't move it for the bomb pot. The next hand goes back to regular no-limit with the button in the same place.

Some rooms use a separate bomb pot button that gets passed around the table one hand at a time, in the opposite direction from the way the regular button travels.

This is where dealer-to-dealer communication really matters. Say a player busted on the last hand of the previous dealer's down, the button moved to that empty seat, and a new player sat down. You'd need to remember, not on the bomb pot but on the hand after it, that the new player can't be the button. A bomb pot with a lot of action can take five minutes. The previous dealer is long gone, and the players probably won't remember either. You need to keep track of it. (There's more on this in cash game procedures.)

Can a player who sits down at a dead button play the bomb pot? Some rooms don't let them. That's based on the traditional logic of poker: you have to earn the button to get the button. I think nowadays it's probably slightly more common not to let them play, and some places do let them. Personally, I think it's harmless in a 1/2 no-limit game, maybe not in every game. It's better for everyone when people can play more hands, and the bomb pot isn't really part of the regular hand. That's one reason a bomb pot button is nice: with one going around, this never comes up.

Step 2: Deal, then bring two flops with one burn

There's no preflop action. You go right to the cut and pitch the cards: two to everybody in hold'em, four in PLO. Then you go right to the flops.

  • Burn once for both flops. That's what they do at Texas Card House, and I totally agree with it. The burn card's job is to protect the top card when there's action happening, so nobody can see what's coming next. You're bringing both flops at once, both turns at once and both rivers at once, so one burn per street does the job.
  • It matters more in PLO. A lot of the time you'll get nine players in a PLO bomb pot. If you burn twice on every street, you end up using every card in the deck, including the card touching the cut card on the bottom. There are reasons we prefer not to use that card. One burn per street also lets more players play.
  • Keep the two boards neat, one in front of the other, with a little felt between each card. Players have to read their cards against two boards, so give them a board they can read.
  • The action starts to the left of the button, same as any flop.

The basics of tapping, burning and spreading a board are in how to bring the flop, turn and river.

What I correct in class

Board cards jammed together

A student squeezes the cards of the two boards together. I want some felt between the cards. Otherwise it's too hard for the players to read.

Tapping for every card

You tap the table once for the burn, not once for each card you bring.

Forgetting the chip on the burn cards

Keep at least one chip on the burn cards at all times. In a bomb pot, the simplest way is to keep one chip on the burns and do everything else separately, rather than sliding the burns under your stacks.

Step 3: The betting, and keeping the pot stacked

Standard procedure says you bring the bets in and then you bring the next cards. Some rooms let you bring the turns first and collect the chips after. That's an advanced move. More hands get out that way, and more mistakes happen. The rooms that make you bring the bets in first have a good reason: it prevents mistakes. Do what your room says.

Either way, stack the pot as it builds, because this ends up being a split pot. I don't know whether that's written procedure at the room I work at. It's just common sense. But do it mindfully:

  • Don't block anyone's view of the boards. If I'm reaching across and spending time on the chips, players on that side can't see the boards. In a double-board PLO bomb pot, my arm in the way is brutal for their experience. These players already have a hard time reading four cards against all the cards on the table.
  • Work on the pot when it's someone else's turn. I pay attention, and I wait for a player to be done with whatever they're doing before I reach in front of them. That might mean waiting a second, or going in a different order than I normally would.
  • Don't stack the chips too high. Tall stacks get in the way of the boards too.

What I correct in class

Stacking in somebody's way

A student stacks the pot while a player still needs to see the boards and act. You have to be mindful of who's in. You don't stack in somebody's way. You just wait.

All-ins and bet amounts

  • Loose change. In most poker rooms, loose change doesn't play in a PLO bomb pot. In a $5 hold'em bomb pot, some places don't let all of an all-in player's chips play, because a $5 ante doesn't need small chips. Personally, in a 1/2 bomb pot, I like the rule that if a player goes all in, all of their chips play.
  • Get the bets right quickly. On the river, players are going to want to show down almost immediately. If two players call an all-in with a stack of black chips, I don't make change for everyone. I count down the all-in, toss a chip back and say, "Give me 11 more." Players generally want things to go smoothly.
  • Counting an all-in in PLO. The technical rule in most poker rooms is that you don't count a player's all-in until somebody asks. You can absolutely do that. In a PLO bomb pot, if I can see it's less than the pot, I'll admit I think it's fine to just count it so it's done, because if someone says "pot" right away, figuring out the amount takes a long time. If your poker room is strict about stuff like that, do what they say.
  • Don't let go of the pot size. Once a PLO pot gets big enough, a dealer might relax, because anyone betting that much is going to be all in anyway. But a player facing an all-in can ask how much is in the pot to figure their odds. You want to be able to figure it out.
  • Betting caps. In a very big PLO game, the players sometimes still want a bomb pot but limit everybody to, say, 1,000, 3,000 or 5,000 for that hand. You just keep track of it. It sounds stressful, but it really isn't that bad.
  • Leave the final all-ins in front of the players. With several all-ins, I build the main and side pots, and I leave the last all-in amounts out in front of the players they belong to. It's easier than bringing it all in, and it helps me remember who shows down first. Building those pots is covered in how to build side pots.

Folding on a check

Folding on a check is something players hate in some places, and some tournaments even give penalties for it, because it changes the hand for the players still to act. I used to think, "Come on, just check." Now that I've dealt nine-handed PLO double-board bomb pots, I completely think differently. The last thing I want is to make somebody sit with a hand they'd have folded for five or ten minutes while everyone checks and thinks about it. In PLO bomb pots, at least, I believe folding on a check should be encouraged. Let the other players battle it out.

Counting the stub

Bomb pots usually give you time to count the stub, but two boards change your numbers. In PLO, if you burn once per street, you'll have three cards left over, plus four for every seat out of nine that you didn't deal. Six players means 15 cards. If a big hand doesn't give you the time, it's okay to count it on the next hand. How to count it is in the flop, turn and river guide.

Step 4: Read both boards

Dealers probably make more mistakes reading hands in double-board bomb pots than in any other situation, especially the PLO ones. When I started dealing double-board PLO bomb pots, it took me a few months to really be on it. A lot of the players are on it themselves, and it's tough to do everything you have to do and stay on it. So give yourself permission to learn as you go. But don't rush yourself either.

  1. Read each board before the hands. Look at what's possible. Are there any pairs on the board, so a full house is possible? Three of a suit? Any straights? If there's no flush possible on top and no pair, you know what you're looking for.
  2. Frame each hand against each board. In poker, we generally say to frame up. In a double-board bomb pot, I think it makes sense to frame down the boards. Again, that's what I do, not a universal procedure. Bomb pots are a relatively new part of poker.
  3. Say what you see, and give players a chance to speak up. "Three aces on top. Tens and threes on the bottom." Verifying with everybody as you go gives players a chance to say, "Whoa, wait."
  4. Kill the hands that don't win either board.
  5. Remember that one player can win the board they weren't playing. A player might think they're playing the top board and end up winning the bottom one. It happens.

Reading PLO hands fast is a skill of its own. Remember that in Omaha you have to use exactly two cards from your hand and three from the board. A player can look like they've made a royal flush and not have it, because they can't play the cards that way.

Something that happens a lot in these showdowns: another player brings a hurry-up energy and starts saying, "They chop, they chop." Your response is simple: "You need to tell me what the hands are so I can see them." Don't point at the cards. Players who want their hand to win will usually frame it themselves, which helps. But ultimately, it's your responsibility to see it.

Step 5: Award each half

Each board gets exactly half the pot. If you've kept the pot stacked, you've been cutting it in half along the way whenever you had the time, so by the showdown it's often already split. When one player wins one board and another player wins the other, some procedures say to push one side at a time so you don't mix them up. I think it's fine to push both halves at once.

  • Talk your game. Say what you're doing as you go. The more you do that, the more confidence everyone has in you.
  • Who gets the odd chip. In the room I deal at, the worst position gets the extra chip: the player who's going to get the button next. I've also seen rooms where the top board gets it. Whatever your poker room does is fine with me.
  • Stand up if you need to. On a really big pot at a big table, standing up gives you the reach to chop it cleanly. Start with the largest denomination, check the sides of the stacks for dirty stacks, and don't pick up a tall stack if you can slide it.
  • Pay the pots in order. With side pots, work from the outside in, and keep each player's hand in front of them so you know who's eligible for what.

When someone gets quartered

Say one player wins the top board outright, and that same player ties another player on the bottom board. The first player gets half plus a quarter. The other gets a quarter: they got quartered. The procedure you can always fall back on, even if the players don't love it, is to bring everything in, award half to the outright winner, then split what's left. When two players put the same amount in on the river, there's a shortcut players expect: the three-quarter player takes their bet back, and you split the quarter player's bet in half between them. When there's an odd amount, have both players throw an equal amount into the main pot so the bets cut evenly. If one player wins a board and two others chop the other board, I bring it all in. That's half, a quarter and a quarter, and there's no shortcut I'd use. The full walkthrough, with the math, is in split pots and quartering.

Variations you'll run into

  • Rake. I've never dealt a bomb pot in a game that takes a rake. The rooms where I've dealt PLO bomb pots charge time per player, so nothing comes out of the pot. If you deal in a rake room, ask them what they do and do whatever they say.
  • Single-board bomb pots. Depending on what your state allows, some games use a single-board bomb pot.
  • More than one, or nothing but. Mostly you're looking at one bomb pot when you first get to a table. I've also come across games that are only bomb pots, over and over, and games that do two in a row.
  • Five-card PLO. I've dealt five-card PLO double-board bomb pots that they only play eight-handed because of how many cards there are. To keep from running out, they don't burn before the flops. That actually makes sense, because there's no action between pitching the cards and bringing the flops, so that burn card wouldn't be protecting anything. They also use the last card touching the cut card, which is normally a no-no. Do whatever your poker room says.
  • Players who skip bomb pots in bad position. In some rooms, players don't want to play the bomb pot in bad position and do want to in good position. The only solution I've heard of is the bomb pot button going the opposite direction. In the rooms I've worked in, I haven't seen it be an issue.

How to practice bomb pots

  • Run the whole hand at home. Antes in one direction, one burn, two flops with felt between the cards, both turns, both rivers, then read and award each board.
  • Run the hands from my two videos. Six players, two boards. Read the boards first, then the hands, and say what you see out loud.
  • Practice the PLO numbers. 15 times 6 is 90, 15 times 9 is 135. Get the common ante totals into memory.
  • Set up the messy ones I demonstrate. A player who wins one board and chops the other, a multiway all-in with side pots, and an odd amount you can't cut in half.

Common questions about bomb pots

What is a bomb pot in poker?

A hand where everyone who's playing puts in an ante, there's no preflop betting, and the dealer goes straight to the flop. In a double-board bomb pot, the dealer brings two boards, and each board is worth half the pot.

Does the button move in a bomb pot?

Usually not. In most rooms the bomb pot is a separate hand and the button stays where it was, so the next regular hand has the same button. Some rooms use a separate bomb pot button that moves the opposite way.

How many burn cards are there in a double-board bomb pot?

The way I deal it, one burn per street: one before both flops, one before both turns, one before both rivers. Some rooms burn more. In eight-handed five-card PLO bomb pots, some rooms don't burn before the flops at all.

How is a double-board bomb pot split?

Half the pot to the best hand on each board. If one player wins both boards, they scoop it. If two players tie on a board, they each get a quarter.

Do you have to play the bomb pot?

It depends on the room. Where I work, it's optional, and a player who's away from the table is assumed to be out.

Is a bomb pot raked?

It depends on the room. The rooms where I've dealt PLO bomb pots charge time, so there's no rake from the pot. In a rake room, ask what they do.

Dealing bomb pots in a real room

New dealers who start at the summer WSOP usually deal hold'em tournaments all day. When I took eight brand-new dealers from Remote Dealer School to deal the WSOP Circuit at Texas Card House Austin, they had to handle cash games, PLO, high-low and double-board bomb pots. That's why class uses hard hands.
