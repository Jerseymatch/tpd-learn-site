Dealing is all about reps. You don't learn it by osmosis, and you don't learn it by watching videos alone. You learn what to do, then you repeat it until your hands do it without you thinking about it. Most of those repetitions happen at home, by yourself, at a dining room table.

That's good news, because it means you can get most of the way there with a cheap setup and a plan. This page is the plan: what to set up, how long to practice, what to drill, and how to check yourself when nobody is there to correct you.

Set up a practice space (you don't need a poker table)

Every dealer should have somewhere to practice, and it doesn't cost much. You don't need a poker table. Lots of my students practice on their dining room table, and one even had a setup in a van.

  • A felt. A 36" x 72" hold'em layout is plenty. You only need enough room for six or seven chip stacks. You don't need a rail.
  • Padding under the felt. Do not skip the padding. You'll be miserable without it. Felt on a hard surface isn't enough, because you can't get your thumbs under the cards to pick them up. A rug pad or shelf liner underneath gives the felt enough give. A hard table topper by itself makes shuffling hard too, so put padding under it or use a felt.
  • Something around the edge to pitch into. Towels or a blanket around the edge of the table work as a rail.
  • Chips, cards and the small stuff. A basic chip set, two decks of Copag cards, cut cards, a dealer tray, spacers and a dealer button.
  • A phone mount or tripod, so you can record yourself. More on that below.

When I went over this on a livestream in early 2026, the whole setup without a table came to under about $150. A practice table is optional. The one I have cost about $100 and took a couple of hours to put together.

No room for any of that? For shuffling alone, a large gaming mouse pad works if it has some padding. Mine is too thin to get under the cards, so put shelf liner or something padded underneath it.

How long to practice each day

For the core skills (the shuffle, the pitch and chip cutting), I ask students for about two hours a day, split up however feels right. Forty minutes on each is fine. In my school, people usually practice about four hours a day between class and practicing on their own, about five days a week.

More isn't always better. My own dealer school was four hours a day, and that let me absorb it, rest and come back focused. Eight hours of concentration a day is too much. You can't cram this. It takes time for it to sink in, which is why learning to deal takes months, not a few weeks.

Practice one thing at a time, slowly

When you're practicing alone, the goal is to train your instincts, not to go fast. Here's the method:

  1. Find the one thing to change. Watch yourself and figure out what's actually causing the problem.
  2. Do it slowly and correctly. When I say correctly, I mean it. For most corrections, speed comes later (the wash is the exception: train that one quickly from the start). Right now you're training the habit.
  3. Do it 20 times. My rule of thumb is that if you do something 20 times, it will be in your instincts. That's not as much as it sounds: shuffling a deck 20 times takes about 20 minutes.
  4. Test it. Try it without thinking about it. If your hands go back to the old way, start over and do it again until they don't.
  5. Then move on to the next thing. One change per session until it's a habit.

When you practice full hands, the same idea applies. If you lose track of the pot or forget the math, don't count the pot to catch up. You'll teach yourself to do that at work. Start the hand over and force yourself to get it right.

Drills that work

  • Shuffle, then pitch four cards to everybody. That's my favorite. It hits two skills at once: you get a shuffle in every time and all your pitches in every time.
  • Pitch into a target. Set a hat or a basket on the floor and pitch into it. Don't pitch Copags into a wooden crate or anything hard. The cards will chip, and I've had Copags snap little pieces off.
  • Race yourself around the table. Pitch around the table and time yourself, then see if you can beat your time.
  • Cut a 20 stack. For chip cutting, having a 20 stack in front of you is a solid way to drill.
  • Mindless reps, once your hands are trained. Shuffling or pitching over and over while you watch something you enjoy is a fine way to get a little faster. Don't do it while you watch the lessons themselves. Those details need your full attention.
  • Deal complicated hands. Once the mechanics are there, watching a lesson isn't enough. After the side pots lesson, for example, I tell students to deal out ten complicated multi-way all-ins.
  • Talk out loud like there's a full table. Announce every all-in, every raise and every bet, even with nobody there. If you practice silently, you'll deal silently.
  • Practice the habits around the hand, too. If you don't have a rake slide at home, take the rake at the end of each hand and put it in a cup holder or always in seat one, so the habit is there when you sit down in a real box. Rake, promo drop and tips covers it.

Each guide on this site ends with a practice section for that skill. Start with the shuffle, the pitch and chip cutting, then work through dealing a full hand.

Record yourself and watch it back

Watching a recording of yourself dealing is incredibly valuable. One person told me they were horrified by the little habits they saw on video that they had no idea about. Poker rooms won't hand you their camera footage, so the only way you get to see yourself is to film it.

This is the core of how I teach. I watch your video, find every correction, and you work each one into your instincts. Between classes, the number one priority for every student is to watch the video of themselves and make those corrections. Only after that do they go on to the new material. You can do the same thing at home: deal a hand, watch it back, write down what you'd fix, and drill those things.

It's also the best way to test yourself. If you can deal a complicated hand on video and it still looks right when you watch it back, that's a big deal.

What I correct in class

Not watching the video

I can tell when a student didn't watch the video of themselves between classes. The correction from last time is still there. It doesn't mean they're in trouble. We just spend their class time on it, instead of on something new.

Practicing fast instead of correctly

Students want to practice a correction at speed. It's not about doing it quickly yet. Go slow, do it the right way until it's in your instincts, then let the speed come.

Practicing silently

With no real players at the table, students stop announcing the action. Say "all in" anyway, and say every raise. You're building the habit, not just the hand.

Sleep on it

I teach my students how important sleep is, because of the kind of learning dealing is. After you practice something like your shuffle, get a good night's sleep and let yourself wake up naturally. A lot of the smoothing out of fine motor skills happens while you sleep. You're often better the next day, and it can save you hours of practice.

Getting live reps: home games, charity events and practice sessions

Practicing alone builds your mechanics. Live games add real players and real pressure. When you can't get into a casino game yet, there are a few ways to get reps with people:

  • Charity casino events. Search "casino events" in your city. They pay a little and they're great reps. Get your shuffle, pitch and chip cutting fluent first, though. They won't have a shuffle machine.
  • Home games and bar poker. Good for getting used to dealing to people.
  • Ask local poker rooms. Some rooms let you practice when it's slow, and some have dealers who run practice sessions.
  • Set up scenarios at your practice table. Set up the situations you want to practice and deal them.

Here's the catch: nobody corrects you in a home game or a casino night, so bad habits can form. Dealing play-money events won't get you through a real audition by itself. People who've only dealt recreationally often audition and fall short, because a poker room expects you to actually know the job. Live reps are a supplement to correct practice, not a replacement for it.

Keep practicing until you're hired (and after)

How many reps is enough? Way more than people think. Someone once told me they'd practiced 200 hands and still pitched two cards at a time now and then. Two hundred hands is roughly one day of dealing. Keep going.

And don't stop once your class or course is done. I tell every graduate to keep practicing until they're hired. It takes about two months on the job for the instincts to be deep enough to stay. If you get lazy after training, they can fade. When you're ready to test yourself, how to pass a poker dealer audition covers how to practice for that specifically.

Common questions about practicing dealing at home

How can I practice dealing poker without a poker table?

Put a felt over a rug pad or shelf liner on your dining room table, with towels or a blanket around the edge to pitch into. That's all you need. Felt on a bare hard table isn't enough, because you can't get under the cards.

How many hours a day should I practice dealing?

About two hours a day on the core skills is a good target when you're starting out. My school students usually put in about four hours a day between class and practice. Eight hours a day is too much to stay focused.

Can I learn to deal poker by practicing alone?

You can build most of the mechanics alone, but you need some way to see your own mistakes. Record yourself and watch it back. Even better is someone who knows the job watching you and correcting you.

What do I need to practice dealing at home?

A felt, padding to go under it, a chip set, two decks of plastic poker cards, cut cards, a tray, spacers and a dealer button. Add a phone mount or tripod to record yourself. The gear page has what I recommend.

Is dealing home games good practice for a casino audition?

It's good for getting used to people, but nobody corrects you, so bad habits form. Dealing home games or charity events alone usually isn't enough to pass an audition at a real poker room.

How long does it take to get good at shuffling?

For each correction, about 20 good repetitions to get it into your instincts, and a night's sleep after. Your mechanics keep improving long after that. My own pitch has evolved over years.
