Side pots and multi-way all-ins are probably the most requested part of dealing I've ever been asked about, and I get why: there's huge pressure to get the money exactly right, do it quickly, and make the players confident you know what you're doing.

Here's why it matters so much in an audition. If a poker room hired dealers who weren't good at this, the floor calls they'd have to deal with would be insane. Not knowing who is eligible to win what is the worst floor call a supervisor can get. If there's one thing you get absolutely right before your audition, make it this.

[[toc]]

Why side pots exist

Go back to one of the fundamental rules of poker: cards speak. Your cards entitle you, at the showdown, to win a pot. But only an amount equal to what you put in.

When poker was first played, a player who ran out of money could be forced to forfeit the whole hand. We don't want that. So there's going all in: once your last chips go into the pot, that's all you're eligible to win from each of the other players. Everything bet beyond that goes into a side pot that you can't win.

The main pot is the pot every player in the hand has put an equal amount into. Each side pot is the next layer up, for only the players who put in more.

The rule that prevents almost every mistake: start with the short stack

In any multi-way all-in, always start with the shortest stack. That player's all-in amount, taken from every player, is the main pot. Then find the next shortest stack, take that amount from every remaining player, and that's the first side pot. Keep going outward.

This comes up in class over and over. When students start somewhere other than the shortest stack, that's when the money goes wrong.

A simple example

Three players are all in on the flop. The shortest is all in for 150. The next player has 70 more than that, and the third covers them.

  1. "Short stack's over here, looks like 150. I'm taking 150 from every player." Take 150 from each and put it in the middle. That's the main pot, and all three are eligible.
  2. The next shortest stack has 70 left. "This side pot's 70." Take 70 from both remaining players. That's the side pot, and only those two are eligible for it.

Taking the matching amount from each player's chips like this is called cherry picking. Go one player at a time, take the amount once, and move it straight into its pot.

One audition note: in a simple all-in for less with a couple of callers, auditioners usually want you to do the math instead, because it's quicker. Multiply the all-in amount by the number of players in the pot, move that to the main pot, and everything else goes on the side. They don't want to watch you take 15 from every single stack.

Step by step at a live table

  1. Build the pots before the board runs out. When multiple players are all in, make the pots first, then bring the remaining cards. There's an argument for the other way, but things can happen. I've seen enough that I want everything squared away before anyone finds out whether they won.
  2. Verify the calls before you build anything. When someone goes all in and there are calls, first make sure each player has put in the correct amount for the bet they made. Players want to see that the bet they actually made was respected. Give change at this point too. Then start building pots.
  3. Build from the short stack outward, one pot at a time, saying each amount out loud: "645 from every stack." When it gets complicated, it's okay to stand up to reach and count.
  4. Count each player's all-in separately. Don't mix stacks together before you've verified them. That way you catch a mistake before it's combined into a pot.
  5. Leave room for the board. No pot goes where the board goes. Keep pots from mixing with anyone's stack or with each other. Leave space for the river.
  6. Leave each all-in player's cards in front of them, not lined up at the top of the table like you see on TV. With several all-ins, it's much easier and more professional.
  7. At showdown, award the outside pot first, the last side pot, between only the players eligible for it, then work inward to the main pot.
[[moment gppYw2y6LwM 1:15:38 | Building the pots during the all-in action vs running the board out first]]

Exception: when everyone's all in on the river and already showing. Building every pot the normal way can be a pain when it all happens at once. Here you can start with the winning hand: frame it, count their amount, take that amount from everybody, and award it. Then compare what's left for the rest. It feels backwards to push the main pot first, but clearing it away makes a huge difference.

Talk your game

Your words are half of handling a side pot. The players are thinking two things: "I really hope I win," and "I'm worried the dealer is going to mess up." Clear communication is what fixes the second one.

  • Announce who's all in for what: "This player's all in for the main pot."
  • Announce that further betting goes on the side. "Action on the side, everybody. Please don't show your hands." Not clearly saying this is one of the most common mistakes dealers make, because it leads you to bring side-pot bets into the main pot.
  • Say each pot's amount as you build it.
  • At showdown, say which pot you're on: "This is the first outside pot, between you three."
  • Remind the all-in player to show for the main pot. They may forget while everyone focuses on the side pot.
  • Stay neutral. No "sorry," no "nice hand." You never want to look like you're picking a side. And never call attention to what's on the board. That's up to the players.
  • Announce the total to call, not the difference. Doing math out loud for a player creates liability if you get it wrong.

What I correct in class

Rushing because the players are rushing you

Take your time, especially when you're new. Don't feel the pressure players put on you to speed up. If you do, you won't know who's eligible to win what. A huge multi-way all-in could take a long time, the next dealer may be standing behind you waiting to tap in, and it doesn't matter. I'd estimate roughly 70% of working dealers don't have an efficient, smooth method for complex side pots, so getting this right sets you apart.

What I correct in class

Counting a whole stack to compare it

When building a side pot, don't count down every player's entire stack to compare them. Just pull the amount you need and stop once you have it. And touch each chip only once. Don't count out a matching amount, set it aside, and then move it again. Move it straight into the pot. Dealers who don't hold themselves to touching each chip once build a habit that's hard to break.

What I correct in class

Losing track of the main pot amount

Never get so focused on neat stacks that you forget how much is actually in the main pot. And when you move a side pot, move it carefully so it can't mix with the main pot or a player's chips. If a stray chip ends up in the wrong place, call attention to it and call the floor.

All-in basics that come up every time

  • Don't count down an all-in bet until it's called. That's the rule across poker. Once someone calls, start counting it down right away. If you're not sure whether it's all in for less, count it. (In PLO cash games it's common to count it right away, because the next player's pot-sized bet depends on the amount.)
  • A verbal "all in" still needs chips in the pot. Require at least one chip to actually go in, and use the all-in button to keep the action clear.
  • Loose change doesn't play. In a game that plays in $5 increments, chips that aren't a multiple of five come back to the player.
  • An all-in for less doesn't automatically reopen the betting. In no-limit, it only reopens the action if it's a full raise. This one confuses even experienced dealers. When in doubt, call the floor.

How to practice side pots

  • Pretend you're at a real table. Practice talking your game out loud, exactly as you would at work.
  • Record yourself. Watch it back and check whether you got every pot right. If you can do a big multi-way all-in correctly three times, that's huge.
  • Learn the basic method flawlessly first. There are shortcuts, like working inside out so you don't have to build every pot when the big stack wins. They're only for people who already do the basic way perfectly. Whatever you learn first is the hardest thing to unlearn.

The side-pot hands we deal in class are some of the hardest you'll ever see. My grads keep telling me those spots only come up once in a long while on the job, and that it's better to be ready for them.

Common questions about side pots

How do you calculate a side pot in poker?

Start with the shortest all-in stack. Take that amount from every player, and that's the main pot. Then take the next shortest remaining amount from each player still in, and that's the first side pot. Repeat until every chip is in a pot or returned.

Who is eligible to win the main pot?

Every player still in the hand. The main pot only holds an amount each of them contributed equally.

Which pot is awarded first?

Normally the outside pot, the last side pot created, between only the players eligible for it. Then you work inward to the main pot.

What does "all in for less" mean?

A player going all in for less than the current bet or a full raise. In no-limit, an all-in for less than a full raise doesn't reopen the betting.

Should the dealer count an all-in bet right away?

No. The standard is to count it down once it's called, or when you aren't sure it's all in for less. PLO cash games are a common exception.
