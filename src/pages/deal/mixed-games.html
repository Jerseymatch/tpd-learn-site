My guess is that about 70 to 75% of dealers out there don't really know mixed games and stud. You can work for years dealing hold'em and Omaha and never see them. But they come up. The WSOP auditions dealers on them. When I made my lessons, its published audition included a hand of no-limit hold'em, a "flop game," a seven-card game and a mixed game, and they picked which ones however they felt like it. There are still plenty of rooms that require seven card stud in the audition, even rooms that hardly ever spread it. And in Texas, mixed games run as cash games, and 2-7 single draw shows up on tournament schedules.

There are three families of games. Flop games are hold'em and Omaha (I cover Omaha in how to deal Omaha). Stud games give each player two cards down and then cards face up. And what most people mean by "mixed games" are the draw games: deuce to seven, ace to five, and the badugi family. In a mixed game, the games rotate. A plaque in front of the dealer shows which game you're on, and it changes each time the button gets back around to seat one. Some games have 16 games in the mix.

One thing to know up front: my lessons on these games were taught from the WSOP perspective, using the WSOP dealer guide as it was when I made them. Since then, the industry hasn't been as solid on some of this stuff, and room rules vary. Throughout this guide I'll tell you the WSOP way and where rooms do it differently. When your room says otherwise, do it their way.

The two mistakes hold'em dealers make in every one of these games

Bringing a flop. The biggest mistake hold'em and Omaha dealers make at stud is that they bring a flop. It happens in the draw games too. Your hold'em instincts take over. It frustrates players, and it's a floor call.

Forgetting to burn. For hold'em dealers, remembering to burn in stud is really hard for some reason. You do have to do it, every street. In the draw games, you burn before every draw, even if everyone stands pat. Once a hand starts, you always burn before you deliver cards. Period.

Both come from going too fast. Do not try to go too fast in these games. If you're not fully comfortable, deal slowly.

What I correct in class

Bringing a flop

It's the number one mistake I see in stud and draw. A student once came up with a trick I think is genius: put the pot where the flop would go. If the chips are sitting there, you can't bring a flop.

Skipping the burn before the draw

It's the number two mistake. Build one habit and repeat it every time: tap the table, burn a card, then bring the cards.

Seven card stud

Stud used to be more popular. Regular stud, a lot of players find boring now, and it's sort of a dying game. If you're not going to deal at the WSOP, it's possible you'll rarely see it. But it's the base for razz and stud hi-lo, so learn it well.

The structure

  • Eight players at most. Eight times seven is 56, and there are only 52 cards.
  • No dealer button. In a mixed rotation, keep the button behind the players' stacks and move it each hand anyway, just to count the hands.
  • Antes. Most stud games have one, and tournaments do, though not every cash game. Only players who are dealt in pay it. Collect antes going around in one direction so you don't lose track. The WSOP-preferred timing is: riffle, riffle, box, riffle, wait for the cut, collect the antes, then cut and pitch right away. If you find yourself in a mixed game, find out what they do. Don't assume.
  • Limit betting, five rounds. In a 4/8 game with a $1 ante and a $1 bring-in, third and fourth street are bet at $4 and fifth, sixth and seventh at $8: two betting rounds at the lower limit and three at the higher limit. There's one more betting round than in hold'em. The ante and bring-in amounts vary by casino, so don't memorize them.

Dealing the cards

  1. The first card always goes to seat one, the player on your left. Every round, every single time. There's no button to deal from.
  2. Two down, then one up. That's third street. After that, fourth, fifth and sixth street are dealt face up, and seventh street goes down. Just remember: four cards up, last card down.
  3. Pitch up cards in one motion. Push the card off, flip it in your hand, give yourself room to kick it, and pitch. There's a tendency to hold the card and look at it before you pitch it. Don't. Get it right out there and use your eyes later.
  4. Leave the cards where they land unless it isn't clear whose card it is or it lands in someone's hole cards. Then stop and pull it out. Players can arrange their own cards, and some don't like the dealer moving them. Go with the flow.
  5. Burn every street, and keep the burns visible. The burns show what street you're on. Some dealers bury the burns under the pot and they disappear.

The bring-in on third street

The player with the lowest up card has to bring it in. If two players have the same rank, the suit decides. Suits rank in reverse alphabetical order: spades, hearts, diamonds, clubs. So the deuce of clubs is always going to bring it in. The ace is high in regular stud.

  • Announce the rank and the suit: "Three of spades brings it in." Saying the suit flags a mistake if there's a lower card on the table.
  • The bring-in player can't simply fold. They're required to bring it in for the bring-in amount, or they can complete to the small bet.
  • Odd amounts. I treat the bring-in almost like a blind. With a $1 bring-in and a $4 complete, a player who puts out $2 hasn't put out half a complete, so push the extra back. With $3 out, that's more than halfway, so they have to complete.
  • A complete is not a raise. Going from one to four doesn't count as a raise, the way I learned it. After it, the bet and three raises go 4, 8, 12, 16. Announce it: "Complete to four." I deal stud infrequently, so if your room counts it differently, go with your room.

After the bring-in, the action goes to the left in normal order.

Fourth street on: the high hand acts first

Starting on fourth street, the player whose up cards make the best high hand acts first, and they can check or bet. It's not a consistent place where the action starts every round, so look at the board every street. If two up-card hands tie, the WSOP (when I made my lesson) gave it to the player closest to the dealer's left, and the TDA recommends going by high card and suit. Rooms differ, so be ready to say, "Oh, okay, I learned it this way, but I can do that."

The open pair. In regular stud only, when a player shows a pair on fourth street, any player may bet the bigger increment. It's a choice, not automatic, and once someone does, the bets stay at the bigger increment for that round. It isn't used in stud eight or better or razz. The TDA recommends dropping this rule and it's old school, but the WSOP still did it when I made my lessons, and some rooms still do.

Folding

A player folds by turning their up cards face down. That is an automatic fold in seven card stud. If they try to take it back, you get a supervisor. Rooms differ on this, and in a friendly player-run mixed game, the players may not hold someone to it. Either way, you don't argue it: any resistance from a player, you call the floor. Count the cards when they fold.

Running out of cards on seventh street

With eight players, stud can run out of cards. During the sixth-street betting, discreetly count the stub. The number you need is the number of players still in, plus two: one burn, plus a card that stays on the stub. So five players needs seven cards, and eight players needs ten. You never want to be sure you had enough and be wrong.

  1. Enough cards: burn, deal a down card to each player, and you're done.
  2. Not enough, but enough once you add the burn cards: mix the burns into the stub without exposing anything, give it a quick shuffle, cut, burn and deal. Rooms differ on the order. Another way I teach it is to deal as many as you can first without using the bottom card, then mix what's left with the burns, cut and continue. In my lesson I mix the burns in before dealing, which is most likely the WSOP procedure. Either way, you need to know before you start dealing whether you can make it all the way around.
  3. Not enough even with the burns: burn and deal one community card face up in the middle. Every player uses it, and there's a betting round.

The first time it happens in a new room, ask for a floor.

Exposed cards

  • A down card exposed on the deal. When I made my lessons, the WSOP called it a misdeal. The TDA recommends dealing a down card in its place and letting the exposed card be the player's up card. You have to kind of learn both. In an audition, confirm which way they want it.
  • Seventh street dealt face up. Dealing everyone's seventh-street card face up is one of the common mistakes. This is a floor call a hundred percent of the time.

Never handle a premature card on your own. Call the floor. For more on exposed cards in general, see misdeals.

The showdown

If the river was bet, the last aggressor shows first. If it was checked, the high board shows first in stud and stud eight or better, and the low board shows first in razz. Tap the cards when you muck them.

What I correct in class

The first card to the wrong seat

It's tempting to start with whoever acts first, like the high hand. In stud, the first card goes to seat one every round.

Two up cards to one player

You only give one to everybody, not two. One card per player per round.

Seventh street face up

Four cards up, last card down. Seventh street goes face down.

Razz

Razz is seven card stud for the low hand. The low wins the whole pot, with no qualifier.

  • The ace is always low. Straights and flushes don't count against you, so 8-7-6-5-4 is an eight-seven low. Pairs do count against you. The best hand is A-2-3-4-5.
  • Read from the highest card down: "ten-five low."
  • The bring-in is the highest card, by rank and then suit. The king of spades is the highest card, and since the ace counts low, it never brings it in.
  • After third street, the lowest hand showing acts first. A pair showing ranks worse than unpaired cards.
  • No open-pair rule, and since the pot isn't split, there's no need to stack it.

Stud hi-lo

Stud eight or better splits the pot between the best high hand and the best qualifying low. The low has to be five different ranks, eight or lower. Straights and flushes don't hurt the low, and you read the low from the highest card down, so a 6-5 low beats a 7-4. If there's no qualifying low, the high takes it all. The bring-in is the low card, the same as regular stud, the high hand acts first on later streets, and there's no open-pair rule.

  • Stacking. The WSOP wants you to stack the pot after the last card. A lot of casinos teach you to stack it as you go.
  • Odd chip goes to the high. Push the high half first, then the low.
  • Read the high first, then the low, for every hand. That's the same as in Omaha hi-lo.

Stud hi-lo "regular" (no qualifier) splits the pot between the best high and the best razz hand. It follows the razz rules: the bring-in is the high card, and on later streets the low hand acts first.

For the odd chip in a split between two players, the WSOP goes by high card and suit, even in the low games. Some tournament directors use low card by suit. It just needs to be fair. The full chopping procedure is in split pots and quartering.

The draw games: 2-7 and A-5 triple draw, five card draw

Draw games are the least commonly known games for a dealer, less common even than stud. But the WSOP runs bracelet events in them every year, and they run in cash games in Texas. Deuce to seven and ace to five are probably the most common mixed games.

Deuce to seven (2-7)

  • The lowest hand wins, and the ace is high. Straights and flushes count against you. The nuts is 7-5-4-3-2, not all one suit.
  • 2-7 triple draw is limit, with a button and blinds, six players at most, and four betting rounds. In a 4/8 game, the blinds are $2 and $4, and there are two rounds at $4 and two at $8.
  • 2-7 single draw is no limit, seven players at most, with two betting rounds.
  • Five cards each, face down. The first card exposed on the deal is replaced and becomes the burn. A second exposed card is a misdeal, just like in hold'em.

How to run a draw

  1. Players discard face down, in turn, starting left of the button. The same players who bet first discard first. A player who stands pat taps or says "pat."
  2. Wait until everyone has discarded.
  3. Announce every count: "Pat, two, two, one, three." That locks in the numbers.
  4. Burn, even if everyone stands pat.
  5. Deliver in order. In a cash game, you can pitch the draw cards. In tournaments, the draw cards are never pitched: you push them out. Dealing five in a row to a player drawing five is fine. Give the new cards separately; don't push them onto the player's hand.
  6. Discards go to their own pile, off to the side by the tray. Slide them into the muck only after all the draws have been delivered.

That's the whole rhythm: say the number of cards each player wants, tap the table, burn, and deliver. And talk your game: "Okay, I'm going to burn now, and then bring two to you, one to you."

The burn cards are sacred. They tell you which draw you're on, so keep them under the chips until the pot is pushed, and don't pull them in with the discards.

Running out of cards in a draw

With six players drawing three times, you'll run out. Deal as many cards as you can without dealing the last card of the stub. Then combine that last card with the muck, not with this round's discards, wash it, shuffle, cut and continue without another burn. Never shuffle the cards face up. The way I read the WSOP text, a player you couldn't finish gets a partial draw, and you remember that you still owe them. Every single poker room has a slightly different way to do this, some mix the stub with the muck, some with the burns, so you just ask them.

Ace to five (A-5) triple draw

It's dealt almost exactly the same as deuce to seven. The difference is the hand: the ace is low, and straights and flushes don't count. The winning hand is the same as the low in Omaha eight or better or stud eight, but there's no qualifier, so a ten low can win. The WSOP plays it limit, with blinds and no ante, six players at most.

Five card draw (high)

The WSOP plays it no limit, with a button and blinds, one draw and two betting rounds, seven players at most. If a card is exposed on the deal, leave it, finish the deal, and it becomes the burn. Two exposed cards is a misdeal.

What I correct in class

Dealing to everyone at a short-handed game

When seven players sit down to a six-max draw game, one player sits out. The player under the gun, next to the big blind, gets a sit-out button and isn't dealt in.

Delivering before the counts are announced

Wait for every discard, announce every number, burn, then deliver. Announcing the counts is what locks the numbers in.

Badugi, badacy and badeucy

Badugi is a four-card, limit triple-draw game, eight players at most, with no ante. The best hand has four different suits and four different ranks. Every four-card badugi beats every three-card hand, and so on down. Compare hands from the highest card down. Straights don't matter. The nuts is 4-3-2-A in four different suits. When you read hands in these games, you need the suits.

Badacy is five cards, with half the pot to the best badugi and half to the best ace-to-five low, no qualifier. It's triple draw limit, six players at most, no ante. Since it's a split pot, stack the pot.

Badeucy is half badugi and half deuce to seven. The way I teach it, the ace is high in both halves, so the best badugi becomes 5-4-3-2. I'll be honest: I couldn't find my source for that again when I checked, so if your supervisor or shift manager says to do it a certain way, you just do it.

The draws work exactly like the draw procedure above: announce, burn, deliver, discards to their own pile.

Sitting down at a game you've never dealt

This is the part that matters most. Mixed-game rules are less universal than hold'em rules. Niche games are played by small groups, and their customs vary. In some rooms, the players organized the game and set the customs, as if they were running a home game inside a casino. You don't need to know every mixed game before you sit down. When you deal in a new room, find out what they do, often just by being cool with the players.

There are two mindsets, depending on where you are:

  • A room that spreads stud every day: you need to know every rule.
  • A rotation game with plaques: you just ask them how they want you to do it. Sit down and say something like, "Hey, guys. I'm a dealer. I know how to work my way around the table, but I'm going to need you to cue me for what the games are." That attitude right there is life-changing for the players. There's usually a player who'll explain.

Bring patient, non-confrontational energy. Poker players are competitive, and they won't be gentle if you fight them. Follow what the room and the players do, unless it's against your room's rules or the state's regulations. And in an audition it's different: there, know everything you can, and call the floor if you miss a detail.

How to practice

  • Learn hold'em mechanics first. Everything in this guide assumes you can already deal a hand of Texas hold'em.
  • Deal the hands, don't just read about them. If you have cards, a felt and chips, you have to practice this, because this stuff is hard. You don't want to show up just kind of understanding it. Get friends to play the stacks if you can.
  • Set up the situations from my lessons: the stud run-outs on seventh street, the bring-in ties, and a draw game that runs out of cards.
  • The burn habit: tap, burn, bring the cards. Every time.

Common questions about dealing stud and mixed games

Who brings it in in seven card stud?

The lowest up card, with ties broken by suit: clubs is the lowest, then diamonds, hearts and spades. In razz, it's the highest up card.

Who acts first in stud after third street?

The best hand showing. In razz and stud hi-lo regular, it's the lowest hand showing.

What happens if you run out of cards in seven card stud?

If adding the burn cards gives you enough, mix them into the stub, cut and deal. If there still aren't enough, deal one community card face up that every player uses.

How many players can play stud, triple draw or badugi?

Stud games: eight. 2-7 and A-5 triple draw: six. 2-7 single draw and five card draw: seven. Badugi: eight. Badacy: six.

Do you burn a card in draw games?

Yes, before every draw, even when every player stands pat.

Do I need to know every mixed game to get hired?

Not for most rooms. Many rooms never spread stud or draw. If you're auditioning for the WSOP or a room that spreads mixed games, you need to know them. On the job, a dealer who can deal can ask the players how a game they've never seen works.
