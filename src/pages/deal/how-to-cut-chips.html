Cutting chips means breaking a stack down into smaller, equal stacks so that anyone looking can see exactly how much is there. You do it for surveillance, for verification, for yourself, and so the players feel comfortable with how many chips are moving in every transaction.

It's one of the first things an audition will test, and one of the easiest places to spot a dealer who taught themselves. Here's how to do it right, including the detail I didn't include in my original lesson, which turns out to be the thing that makes it work.

Fives and fours: how chips are broken down

In casinos, a full stack is 20 chips of one denomination. How you break it down depends on the chip:

  • $1, $2, $5 and $100 chips break down in fives. Four stacks of five. A full stack of $5 chips is four stacks of $25, which is $100.
  • 50¢, $25 and $500 chips break down in fours. Five stacks of four. A full stack of $25 chips is five stacks of $100, which is $500.

It's done this way because those groupings make the math as easy as possible: every small stack is a round number. That's industry standard, and it's what everyone at the table will expect to see.

The grip

Pick the stack up in your fingertips, never your palm. (More on why in How to Handle Chips.) For cutting:

  • Pinky down behind the stack. It protects the back of the chips. When you push the stack forward and try to prove it, chips will slide back toward your hand and out of place if your pinky isn't there.
  • Thumb on one side, middle finger on the other, both touching the felt.
  • Index finger on top. This is the finger that cuts.

Index finger, not thumb

Some people will tell you that in poker you can cut with your thumb. You can get the job done that way, but cutting with your index finger is considered the most professional way, and both your speed and your flexibility top out much higher. With your index finger, you can cut toward your arm and anywhere in that whole range of motion. With your thumb, you're stuck going perpendicular to your arm. Thumb cutting is never allowed in table games, so learning with your index finger also keeps that door open for your career. Thumb cutting has its occasional uses in poker, like for sticky chips or a bet that's far away, but it shouldn't be how you learned.

Step by step: cutting a stack

  1. Pick up all but five. Lift the whole stack except the bottom five chips (or four, for chips that break down in fours). If you grab one too many or one too few, that's fine. Drop one, or pick one up, and fix it. No big deal.
  2. Set your next stack down toward your arm. The small stacks should run in a line from your index finger toward your elbow. When you're learning, set the stack you're holding a little bit behind the first one so you can see what you're doing. Later you'll put it down right next to it.
  3. Bump. Push the stack you're holding into the stack on the felt.
  4. Prove and cut. Bring your index finger flat across, toward the stack on the felt. It meets the stack in your hand at the same height as the stack that's already down, and you lift everything above that line up into your hand. You've cut. Pull away and release the chips that stay.
  5. Repeat: bump, prove, release, all the way down.
  6. Prove the last stack too. Even though there's nothing left to lift, bump and prove the final stack. That's what shows it's the same height as all the others. Skip it and you could end up with 5, 5, 5 and a 4 that nobody checked.

The detail that makes it work: cut at the moment of impact

This is the step I forgot in my original video, and I ended up teaching it in person over and over, so I made a follow-up lesson just for it. It's something table games teach a lot, and it helps in poker too.

When you're learning slowly, you can have every finger in the right place and still pick up the wrong number of chips. The fix is timing: the moment of contact is the exact moment you cut. When your stack bumps into the one on the felt, that contact is what lines the chips up at the right height. Cut at that instant, every time.

When you're new, exaggerate the bump so you can feel that moment. As you get fast, it shrinks down to a tiny motion. You might not even see the bump anymore, but it's there, I promise you. It matters even more in poker rooms where the chips get played a lot and turn a little sticky.

The splash

Once the stack is broken down, splash it so everyone can see the count.

  • Take the small stack at an angle between your thumb and middle finger and push it off the felt. The chips push themselves into position. Let the table pull the chips out of your fingers instead of dropping them, and the splash stays tight.
  • Don't spin them, and don't drop them. With sticky chips, both of those send chips flying.
  • Clear your hands. Surveillance and everyone else need to see how many chips are there, and that nothing's in your hand.
  • Scoop and prove. Thumb on one side, middle finger on the other, ring finger and pinky behind, and scoop the stack back up, proving it as you go. That last prove makes sure no chip ends up in your palm. It doesn't take any extra time.

Then hand it off using the grip from chip handling: index finger fingernail-down on top, thumb and middle finger on the sides.

When to splash, and when not to. New amounts get splashed, like a stack you just broke down or chips you just took. Stacks that have already been proven don't need to be splashed again. In limit games, don't splash bets into the pot, because the bet amounts need to stay clean and countable. And always splash short stacks of large chips. If you can't prove a short stack of $100 chips, a $200 disagreement with a player is a serious problem for your supervisor.

The mistakes I correct most in class

Chip cutting is one of the categories I correct most often in my live classes. Over four cohorts this year, I logged more than 100 separate moments of fixing someone's chip cutting. These come up again and again:

What I correct in class

Cutting at an angle to your arm

Your stacks should run in a straight line from your index finger toward your elbow. When students cut across their arm at an angle, or sideways, it's slower and harder, and cutting sideways is something I correct the moment I see it.

What I correct in class

Skipping the last prove

That final prove is what tells you a stack is really 100 and not 95 or 105. It's especially important if you're someone who tends to miscount. Don't skip it to save half a second.

What I correct in class

Pinky and thumb off the felt

Keep your pinky and thumb grounded on the felt while you cut. They're your control and your guide. When they lift up, the cut gets loose.

What I correct in class

Doing it the way that feels comfortable

When you're brand new, nothing about cutting chips is going to feel comfortable, so learn the correct, universal method from the start instead of what feels natural. I also see dealers bring in drop cutting from craps. Picking up all but five is simpler and standard in poker. And in class, use every single bet as a rep. If you rush through breaking down a bet without cutting it properly, you're practicing the wrong thing.

How to practice

  • Learn both hands. A professional dealer has to be able to cut with both hands. It comes up constantly. The left hand is just the mirror image.
  • Practice with fives and fours. Switch between $5-style stacks (fives) and $25-style stacks (fours) so both become automatic.
  • Compare yourself to the video. If it doesn't look or feel like mine, go back, figure out what's different, and fix it. Getting this right over time makes it much easier to get hired and much easier to be fast once you're working.

Common questions about cutting chips

How many chips are in a stack in a casino?

A full stack is 20 chips of one denomination.

Why do dealers cut $25 chips in fours?

Because four $25 chips make $100. 50¢, $25 and $500 chips break down in fours, and $1, $2, $5 and $100 chips break down in fives, so every small stack is a round number.

Should you cut chips with your thumb or index finger?

Index finger. It's the most professional method, it's faster once you're good, and it gives you a much bigger range of motion. Thumb cutting is never allowed in table games.

What does "bump and prove" mean?

The bump is pushing the stack in your hand into the stack on the felt. The prove is bringing your index finger across at that same height and lifting off everything above it, which shows the two stacks are equal.

Why do my chip counts come out wrong?

Usually it's timing. Cut at the exact moment your stack makes contact with the stack on the felt. That's what keeps every stack the same height.
