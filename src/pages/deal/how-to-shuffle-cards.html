The shuffle is the first thing anyone watching you deal will see. I've run more than 1,000 live auditions as a shift manager, with people's jobs on the line, and the shuffle sets the tone for all of them. A clean, quiet, confident shuffle tells the person hiring that you've put the reps in. A shaky one tells them you haven't.

The professional poker shuffle has one sequence, and once your hands learn it, it never changes:

  1. Wash
  2. Collect & square
  3. Riffle
  4. Riffle
  5. Box
  6. Riffle
  7. Cut
  8. Into the hand

Below I'll go through each step the same way I teach it in Remote Dealer School. Watch the video first so you can see what the whole thing should look like at full speed. Then read through the details, because the details are where people fail auditions.

Why poker dealers shuffle this way

Everything in the shuffle has one of two jobs. It either mixes the cards or keeps anyone from seeing or following a card, including you. That's why the deck turns away from you, why the cards stay flat to the table, why the cut card's dull side faces up, and why the cut takes a third of the deck. Once you understand that, most of the rules stop feeling arbitrary. If something you do with the deck could let somebody see or track a card, it's wrong.

There's a second test I use when I'm watching a student: does it feel like poker? Poker room managers have watched thousands of dealers. They know what the real thing looks like. A shuffle can technically work and still look nothing like what a real dealer does, and that's enough to fail you.

Step 1: The wash

Spread the cards face down and mix them on the felt with both hands. Every card face should touch the felt at least once. A full wash takes about seven seconds.

You don't do a full wash every hand. There are two kinds:

  • The full wash (about 7 seconds) happens on your first hand when you tap into a table. Not your second hand, not your third. You also do a full wash when you've got a brand-new suited deck. Even when a deck isn't brand new, that first-hand wash matters, because every so often a stray card from another deck ends up mixed in, and spreading the deck is how you'd catch it.
  • The mini wash (2 to 3 seconds) happens between every other hand. Its job is protection. If a player wins and their cards go back on top of the deck, picking them straight up could let someone see what they had. A quick little wash before you collect takes care of that.

What I correct in class

A wash that barely moves the cards

The most common wash problem I see is a student gently patting the cards around instead of actually mixing them. Move them. Make full circles, not half circles. And make it brisk. This is the one part of the shuffle I want you to train fast from day one. With the riffle and the pitch, I'll happily have you go slow and get the form right. But if you practice the wash slowly now, you'll keep washing slowly forever, and a slow wash makes the whole table wait on you.

Step 2: Collect and square the deck

Scoop the cards together until they're tight, either by pushing in from the outsides or by closing your fingers around them and pulling them in. Take one card off the top and put it on the bottom. That gives you something to grip so you can pick the whole pile up. It works with more than one deck, too.

Now turn the deck away from you, so the card faces point away from you. That's "poker away." The dealer should never be able to see the cards or work the deck. Drop the cards lightly on the felt to get a flat edge, then use one hand to push the deck while the thumb of the other hand spins it square. It takes a few tries. Don't worry if it's awkward at first.

What I correct in class

Setting the deck down between steps

Nearly every student does this at some point: they put the deck down on the felt and pick it back up between parts of the shuffle. Keep the cards in your hands the whole way through. It's faster and it looks like you know what you're doing.

Step 3: The riffle

This is the hard part. It's the step that feels least comfortable in your fingers, and it's the one people spend the most time on. Here's the grip, piece by piece.

  1. Grip. On each hand, your middle and ring fingers go on the top edge of the deck with your thumb opposite them, against the side. Your hands mirror each other. Your pinkies go on the outside edges of the deck. Your index fingers just float for now.
  2. Split. Split the deck into two roughly even halves. It doesn't matter which half ends up on top.
  3. Index fingers to the center. Bring your index fingers to the middle until they touch. Your grip should now be strong enough that you could lift your thumbs off the cards. You need that much grip for what comes next.
  4. Thumbs to the corners. Bring the two halves together and slide your thumbs up to the inside corners and underneath the cards, until your thumbs are basically touching.
  5. Flatten your fingers. This is the uncomfortable part. Flatten all your fingers on top of the cards and leave your thumbs underneath. Pinkies stay flat against the sides.
  6. Let the cards fall. Release with your thumbs and let the cards fall onto each other. Don't force it.
  7. Push it together. Keep light pressure on top so the deck stays put, keep your riffle position, and use your pinkies on the outside to slide the halves together.

Three things make a riffle look professional:

  • It's quiet. A loud, snappy riffle damages the cards and it's completely unnecessary. Just let the cards fall onto themselves.
  • It laces. What you want at the end of the riffle is cards interleaving one or two at a time, not big clumps landing together. Some poker rooms specifically watch the lacing.
  • It stays flat. The cards should always be close to parallel to the table, never tipped up where someone could see a card. Flattening your fingers is part of what hides the cards. When it's done right, nobody at the sides of the table can see a thing.

You don't need to angle the deck halves on purpose so the corners meet. When your thumbs, pinkies and elbows are in the right position, that angle happens on its own.

What I correct in class

The pinkies. Almost everyone.

If I had to name the single most common shuffle mistake I see, it's the pinkies. They curl in, or tuck, or drift off the deck, when they need to be flat and pressed against the outside edges. Pinkies on the outside are what give your thumbs control. Right behind that is index fingers that curl in during the riffle instead of staying flat. Let your thumbs and pinkies do the work.

[[shorts sxHz1Iq037o]]

Two more I see all the time: shuffling too close to your body, which keeps your fingers from relaxing flat (move it out toward where the chip tray will sit), and pressing hard on the sides of the cards when you push the halves together. That's tension, and over a career, tension in your hands and wrists turns into real injuries. The less pressure you use, the easier the deck slides together.

Step 4: The box

The box is just moving packets of cards from the top of the deck to the bottom. Cards from the top go to the felt, the next packet goes on top of those, and so on until the whole deck has been moved. My preference is to keep the deck in my deck hand, in the riffle grip, and pull packets off the top with the thumb and middle finger of my other hand, wrist flat on the table. You can also use the other hand to place each packet onto the felt. Technically, either way is fine.

People ask me how many packets you're supposed to pull. There's no magic number. Anywhere from three to six is normal. Poker procedure isn't as rigidly identical everywhere as people think. What matters is that you never lift the cards up while you box. Keep everything parallel to the felt so nobody sees the bottom card.

Step 5: Riffle, riffle, box, riffle

Put it together: riffle, riffle, box, riffle. That's the professional casino poker shuffle. The step people skip most often is that last riffle before the cut. Don't skip it.

Timing tip: After your final riffle and before you cut is the moment to take care of anything that needs doing before the hand: collecting antes in a tournament, handling buy-ins in a cash game. Once you cut, finish the cut and get the cards in the air whenever you can.

This moment matters to players, too. In tournaments, players sometimes rush their blinds out as the clock runs down, thinking that locks in the old level. Pretty universally, the new level starts when the dealer riffles. It isn't about when the blinds go in.

Step 6: The cut

Clear your hands so everyone can see they're empty. Put the cut card right in front of the deck, dull side up. The shiny side can reflect a card. Then, with one hand, either take about a third off the top or leave about a third on the bottom, and put that packet on the cut card. Put the rest of the deck on top of it, and clear your hands again.

The reason you take a third, not a few cards, is so nobody can follow a card through the whole shuffle.

What I correct in class

Cutting with two hands

I stopped a student mid-hand once because, while chatting with the table, he cut the deck with two hands. In an audition, that's an instant fail. Talking is exactly when this happens. Your hands go on autopilot and the bad habit comes out. That's why I want the right habit trained so deep it shows up even when you're distracted.

Related: if you start a cut, stop halfway, and lose your place, don't try to patch it. And if you lose track of where you are in the shuffle at all, just restart the shuffle calmly and keep going. In an audition, you don't need to rewash. Nobody fails for a calm restart. People fail for panicking.

Step 7: Into the hand, and pitch

Pick the deck up into your deck hand. Your index finger protects the front of the deck so the top card can't come up and flash. Your other three fingers protect the side. Your thumb stays flexible. If the deck isn't perfectly square, brush it square with your thumb and forefinger, or push it square with the front knuckle of a finger.

What you never do is cover the top of the deck with your fingers. Players should always be able to see the top of the deck, because that's what tells them the dealer isn't doing anything they shouldn't. And don't tuck your pinky behind the deck. With a worn-in deck, that makes it hard to peel off one card without grabbing two.

Then pitch. The very next thing after the shuffle and cut is the cards going out. No pause. One of the things I correct a lot in later classes is a dealer who cuts, then just sits there. The table is waiting for you.

Practice it the right way

Here's the honest truth about the riffle: if you just go through the motions to get it done, you'll have to retrain yourself later. Go slowly at first and get every finger in the exact right place. Speed comes on its own. Struggling with the riffle grip early on is completely normal. Nearly every student in my classes goes through a stage where it feels impossible, and then one day it doesn't.

A couple of other things that help:

  • Practice on a felt surface. Cards slide and riffle differently on felt than on a bare table, and you want to train on the surface you'll actually work on.
  • Don't be afraid of the cards. Students worry about cards flying during the wash or riffle. Control comes with practice, and auditions are looking for someone who's comfortable with the cards.
  • Practice while talking. Once the mechanics are solid, practice the whole shuffle while holding a conversation. Talking is what makes dealers choppy, and real tables talk to you constantly.

If you have smaller hands or long nails, you may need to adjust the grip a little, and that's fine. I've had students with smaller hands use a slightly different method that works for them. The goal is the result: mixed cards, nothing exposed, and a shuffle that feels like poker.

Common questions about the poker shuffle

What is the casino poker shuffle sequence?

Wash the cards, collect and square the deck, then riffle, riffle, box, riffle, and cut. After the cut, the deck goes into your hand and the cards go out.

How long should you wash the cards?

A full wash is about seven seconds, and every card face should touch the felt at least once. You do it on your first hand at a table and with a brand-new suited deck. Between all other hands, a two-to-three-second mini wash is enough.

Why do dealers use a cut card?

The cut card protects the bottom card of the deck so nobody can see it. That's also why you use the dull side. A shiny cut card can reflect the card face.

How many times do you box the cards?

There's no fixed number. Three to six packets is normal. What matters is that the cards stay flat to the table so the bottom card is never exposed.

Can you shuffle with long nails or small hands?

Yes. You may need to adjust your grip, and that's fine as long as the cards stay protected and the shuffle still looks and feels like poker.
