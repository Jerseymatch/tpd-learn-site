When I tell people what I do, a lot of them react like it isn't a real job. It is. Dealing poker is a skill-based career, and most people overlook it because they don't know it exists. You can go from making nothing to making $30 to $50 an hour in about two months, without a college degree. I've watched my own students do it. One of my students finished my last class at 4:00 in the afternoon, drove to a poker room, and was hired by 5:00.

I found this job by accident. I grew up in Massachusetts, got an electrical engineering degree I didn't want to use, and at about 25 I moved across the country to Washington State with no plan for money at all. A casino there offered a $75 blackjack class, cheap because they had such a hard time finding dealers. I took it, and a few months into dealing table games I learned poker at that same casino. That was 2007. Since then I've dealt, worked the floor, run a poker room shift, run more than 1,000 live auditions and done the hiring, and I still pick up dealing shifts in Texas. This is how I'd tell anyone to get in.

What the job really is

You're not just pitching cards. You're running the game. There's real money on the table, sometimes a lot of it, and people are trying to leave with more than they came with. If someone crosses a line, or someone isn't following the rules, you're the one who's in tune with that and handles it.

I compare it to a referee in a basketball game. If nobody fouls and the ball never goes out of bounds, the referee barely has to do anything. Poker is similar, except you also shuffle and deal every hand. And you're on camera the whole time. Surveillance is watching you and every player, which keeps everyone protected, and it also means you have to do the job right. It's a performance job and a precision job at the same time. That's why poker rooms make you audition.

A few other things I love about it:

  • You don't take the job home. As a supervisor I'd think about the room after my shift. As a dealer, I'm maybe once a month thinking about something that happened at a table. It's like going to school with zero homework.
  • You never have to fight with a player. You state what happened, and if there's any resistance, you call the floor and let them handle it.
  • It's social. You meet every kind of person. If you don't want to sit in front of a computer all day, this is the opposite of that.
  • You can move. I've worked in casinos in Washington, Florida and Texas. Once you've had the job, you can get a job wherever there's a poker room.

And you need to know the other side going in: you'll work holidays, and you're sitting in one place for long stretches. It's sedentary work, and how much pain you go home with depends a lot on how you do the job.

Who it's for, and who it isn't

People ask me if they need to be good at math. You don't. You need to be willing to put the work in. Some dexterity helps (a guitarist I talked to on a pathway call had a good shuffle and pitch right away), and so does being able to handle a little pressure. Liking poker helps too, though I know dealers with ten years in who don't care about the game and make good money.

It's a great fit if you:

  • Play poker. You're already at the table all the time. Why not take the one seat that's guaranteed to win? Poker players make really good dealers because they already understand the game.
  • Are tired of traditional jobs and want a skill you'll have for life once you get hired the first time.
  • Want to earn in months, not years, without four years of college and the debt that comes with it.
  • Are looking for a second career. A friend of mine spent a career as a photographer, started dealing in his 60s, and did it for about eight years. He loved it. One of my students in his 60s went to the WSOP, and I was blown away by how good he was compared to the other dealers there. This isn't just for young people.

I'll be just as blunt about who it isn't for:

  • People who won't practice. Joining my school doesn't get you the job. You have to put the work in.
  • People who want easy money. You're responsible for other people's money in a competitive game. You have to stay engaged.
  • People who can't take feedback. I make a lot of corrections, respectfully. In the industry you'll get corrected too, even as an experienced dealer at a new room.
  • People who fall apart under pressure every single time. A little nervousness is normal, and it can be coached. And if you think you're a bad multitasker, so am I. The way you learn this job, your instincts handle five things and your brain handles one.

What you need to get hired

This is one of the best parts of this job. The casino doesn't care if you have a college degree. They don't care about your grades. You don't take any test except their audition.

  • No certificate required. People ask if they need a dealer certification. They don't. A poker room cares whether you can do the job, and a good auditioner can just tell. One of the first questions in their head is: does it feel like poker?
  • The audition is everything. They're looking at accuracy, control, your presence, and how you talk to people. You're not selling yourself. You're demonstrating.
  • A license, depending on the state. Every state has its own requirements, and tribal casinos can run their own checks. I paid for licenses and fingerprint background checks in Florida and Washington. In Texas right now there's no gaming license for poker, so you get hired and start.
  • A clean record. With felonies, it's harder to get licensed, and in some places impossible. If you have felonies, I'll be honest: this job probably isn't for you. If you have something old on your record, like an arrest from when you were young, tell the truth and bring the paperwork that explains it. Never lie on the application. I haven't seen many dealers lose a job over an old issue they were honest about.
  • Your finances can come up. I know of a Florida tribal casino that turned down someone who'd passed a table games audition because they had over $20,000 in debt that had gone unpaid. Debt you're making payments on is different. Other places would have hired that person, so this varies a lot.
  • Age. You have to be 21 to deal the WSOP. For anywhere else, check your state and the room.

The ways to learn

One of my students laid out the four ways to learn this, and it's a good list: an in-person dealer school, a remote school like mine, a casino's free in-house class, or teaching yourself. I learned in a casino's own class myself ($75 back then). My student's take on the free in-house classes some casinos run was that they're more of a weed-out, with little individual instruction. Here's what I'd tell you about the rest.

  • Teaching yourself works for some people. If you're a poker player who already knows the rules well, my self-paced Complete Course can be enough.
  • Free videos and dealing home games usually aren't enough on their own. I hear from people who watched some free videos, dealt at home, went to an audition, and found out it wasn't close. A few have faked their way through an audition at a room that was opening and needed dealers, and some of them got fired.
  • Dealing a casino party isn't casino poker. Play-money event dealing is fun, but it doesn't prepare you for a real audition. I talked to someone on a pathway call who thought it would. He went to an audition in Texas, and it didn't work.
  • Practice at home matters more than people think. When I took my blackjack class in 2007, I had nothing to practice on at home, so I could only practice in class. I would have gotten better faster with my own setup. (Here's what I recommend to practice at home.)

How long does it take? Learning to deal from scratch takes about 120 hours. My Remote Dealer School is 8 weeks, and the last stretch is feedback sessions that take you the rest of the way, as long as you put the work in. If you want to see where to start, the first skill I teach is the shuffle, and the whole order is on the How to Deal page.

How people actually get hired

Getting hired is supply and demand. If a room has 25 spots and 50 people apply, a well-trained dealer almost always gets one. If there are two spots in a place everybody wants to work, it can take patience. So be smart about where you go.

  1. Find out what's on the audition. Call the poker rooms near you. Rooms will tell you what they test. Then practice exactly that. In my school we do this for every student, for the rooms where they want to work.
  2. Go where they're hiring a lot of dealers at once. A room hires the best of whoever shows up. Mass hiring, like the summer WSOP (it hires in waves every year) or a WSOP Circuit event, gives you the best odds, and you walk away with experience. The summer WSOP is about seven weeks long, and new dealers mostly deal hold'em tournaments, so it's one of the easiest places to start.
  3. Watch for new rooms. Anytime you can get an audition at a poker room that's just opening, they need dealers.
  4. Treat the waiting as part of the interview. At a group audition, one person deals and everybody else plays. You find out a lot about someone at a poker table. Be good to the people around you while you wait.
  5. Know the permanent jobs can be competitive. Some rooms open one to three spots and get 50 to 100 experienced dealers. Others hire many at once. Temporary work at a series can be your foot in the door.

Sometimes it's easier than you'd think. That student who got hired an hour after class called the room first, the manager was there, and he drove over. He shuffled, pitched, brought a flop, and they stopped him right there. They needed dealers, and they could see he could do it. It's not always like that. But there's been a need for dealers my entire time in this industry.

Your first months on the job

Here's what I tell every class: I'm teaching you to pass the audition. You pass the audition, you get the job. Then it takes about six months to get good. After maybe two months it's in your instincts and it's not going anywhere. Around six months, sometimes a year, you get to that flow state.

  • Your first day will feel intense. It's real people and their real money, with all the procedures coming at you at once, plus things nobody taught you, like the room's tablet system. The first hand is the hardest. Then it settles down.
  • Every room is different. The button, the blinds, where things go on the table. When I came back to dealing in 2021, it took me about a month to make a new room's details instinct. Take notes.
  • Do it their way. Never tell a supervisor "that's not how I was taught." I learned this the hard way as a new blackjack dealer: I told a supervisor my teacher said otherwise, and I ended up off the table and in the office. Ask to learn how they want it.
  • Be your own teacher. There's usually a 90-day evaluation period after you're hired, and nobody is going to teach you everything you're expected to know. From years as a supervisor and shift manager, I can tell you the worst thing a new dealer can say is "you didn't tell me this."
  • Keep practicing until your first shift. If there's a gap between training and starting, practice every day. Skills fade fast for a brand new dealer.
  • Show up. The number one reason dealers get fired isn't mistakes or stealing. It's attendance. Rooms usually run on a points system, often eight to 10 points: a call-out is a point and being late is half a point. A lot of rooms now let you offer your shift to other dealers instead, so use that.

What I tell every class

Don't burn bridges

The skill plus your relationships with the poker rooms is your value. When I was a shift manager in South Florida, I hired a chip runner named Victor. He wasn't even a dealer yet. Today he's the COO of Texas Card House, over nine poker rooms. The people in your class and at your first job could be anywhere in a few years. Be respectful to every single one of them.

A skill you have for life

This is the part I can't say enough. Once you've gotten the job one time and worked it a little, it's yours. I mostly supervised from 2009 to 2016 and barely dealt. When I went back to dealing, it took about an hour to get it back. I've left and come back to dealing several times over my career, and it was there every time.

People lose jobs all the time, not because they were bad at them, but because the company shut down. After a business of mine failed, I paid off tens of thousands of dollars of debt dealing poker, while paying for my family's life. That's what having this in your back pocket means.

How you get paid: an hourly base plus tips, and the tips are most of the money. I tell my students $30 to $50 an hour, and I mean that number. New dealers start toward the low end and speed up from there. The full breakdown is in poker dealer pay and tips.

Common questions about becoming a poker dealer

How long does it take to become a poker dealer?

Learning from scratch takes about 120 hours of work. My Remote Dealer School is 8 weeks. Getting hired can be the same day or take a while, depending on how many rooms near you are hiring.

Do you need a license to be a poker dealer?

It depends on the state. In Florida and Washington I had to get licensed, with fingerprints and a background check. Texas currently doesn't require one for poker.

Do you need a certification or a degree?

No. Poker rooms don't ask for a degree or a dealer certificate. They make you audition, and the audition is what gets you hired.

Can you become a poker dealer with no experience?

Yes. Plenty of my students had none. You need to be trained well enough to pass the audition, and the summer WSOP and new poker rooms are good places to get your first job.

Do you need to know how to play poker?

It helps, and poker players make good dealers. But you can learn the rules as part of your training. I know long-time dealers who don't care much about the game.

Is it hard to become a poker dealer?

The barrier to entry is low, but the job has a lot of details. If you practice, take feedback and treat it seriously, it's very learnable. If you won't practice, it isn't for you.

Can you learn to deal poker online?

Yes. You need your own practice setup at home, which is actually an advantage: you can practice between classes. That's how my Remote Dealer School works.
