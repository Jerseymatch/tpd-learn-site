Being a poker dealer is a great career, but there's very little support for the physical side of it. You sit for hours, you make the same small motions thousands of times, and nobody shows you how to take care of yourself. I think of dealers as their own entrepreneurs. The base pay is small and the rest depends on you, and so does how much pain you go home with.

I've dealt 70- and 80-hour weeks back to back, and my fingers can get a little tired. That's when I notice tension creeping into my mechanics. So in class I spend a lot of time on tension: how I deal so my hands don't hurt, and how to find and fix your own. I also had an occupational therapist who works with dealers on my show to walk through posture, the dealer chair and stretches. That conversation is the video below.

Your technique is your health

Here's the thing most people don't realize: some pitches will pass an audition and still hurt you. When I'm working with a student, I care about two things. Number one is passing the audition. Number two is limiting your motion and reducing the tension in your arm, because until you do, you may be limited in the number of hours you can work.

The injury I warn about most is a ganglion cyst: a buildup of fluid under the skin of the wrist from all the friction of a high-motion pitch, repeated over and over. I know multiple dealers who've had one. People who've gotten one say it hurts like hell. Flicking your wrist on the pitch is also a carpal tunnel risk.

And if there's a lot of tension in your fingers, for example squeezing the deck really hard, you can develop trigger finger too.

A low-tension pitch

My pitch has almost no motion. There's barely any movement in my wrist and none in my arm. The card comes off my finger, not my wrist. Some things to check:

  • No swinging. If you're moving your whole arm or wrist like you're swinging a bat, your shoulder and wrists get tired. If you have to swing your wrist to get more distance, you're doing it wrong.
  • Grab the card lower. Grabbing the card too high up strains the wrist. Grab it so your middle finger is lower on the card, and release at the exact moment you kick.
  • Don't squeeze. Hold the deck without a lot of pressure. When I pitch, it feels very gentle, not intense.
  • Trust the card. People tend to add wrist motion when they're afraid the card won't get there safely. Stay with the finger.

If you're already working and your pitch has too much motion, here's how I'd fix it without hurting your bottom line: practice the low-motion pitch at a tournament table, where a little slowness won't cost you, until your fingers get used to it. The full technique is in how to pitch cards.

What I correct in class

"Your pitch is good enough to pass an audition, but..."

I say some version of that sentence to a lot of students. Their pitch works, the cards get where they're going, and it would pass. But there's too much wrist in it, and over 30-plus hours a week that adds up to fatigue, and possibly a ganglion cyst. I'd rather fix it in class than have you find out a year into the job.

Tension in the shuffle

The shuffle hides a lot of tension. One place I see a lot of it is when you push the two halves of the deck together after the riffle. So many dealers, and I had to learn this one too, put pressure on the deck in several spots when they push it together. That just adds tension. If you relax everything and use your fingers as light guides, with your pinkies bringing the halves in, the cards push together easily. With zero pressure on the deck, it's a lot easier.

  • Keep your index fingers flat on top instead of bent. There's much less tension in your hands that way.
  • Drop your wrists. Push your arms out a little so your wrists sit lower to the table.
  • Expect it to feel unnatural at first. Shuffling, pitching and cutting chips all do, and when you're new there can be a weird tension or cramping in your hands. After five or six weeks it starts to feel natural, and it gets better every time you sleep after practicing.

Step by step, the shuffle is in how to shuffle cards like a dealer.

Chips and reaching

Reaching can add to wrist pain. I once watched a student who'd mentioned wrist trouble reach across the table with a really, really bent wrist to pull in chips. The fix is to put your whole hand down and scoop. Your hand can be relaxed and sideways.

When you cut chips, keep your wrist relaxed and low, close to the table, with your arm flatter. A high wrist makes cutting harder. Visible tension in your hand causes mistakes in your cutting, too, so if it's going wrong, stop and relax. More in how to cut chips.

For far-away pots, I've seen Texas dealers keep a chip rake by the well to pull in chips. It saves your body over a career, especially if you have a short reach or a bad back. I don't teach it in school, but it's worth knowing about.

Find your own tension: "What am I working on today?"

I can't watch you deal every shift, so I teach a method you can use for your whole career. Every table you sit down at, ask yourself: what am I working on today?

  1. Identify where your greatest tension is. It could be physical, in your hands, your back or your body. Or it could be mental, like the stress of getting the pot right. Do you feel tension when you're holding the deck? When you shuffle? When you pitch? It's hard to identify, but being able to is very powerful.
  2. Come up with a solution, between tables, not during the game. In the break room, or that evening at home, think about what you could do to make it better. You can ask another dealer how they do it, too, but decide for yourself whether their solution is right for you.
  3. Work on that one thing, with real intention, the next time you deal.

So many dealers never do this, and because of that, they don't get better, and a lot of them don't know what they should be working on. That's why I say you're your own teacher.

Your chair, your posture and your neck

You sit for nearly your whole shift, so your chair matters. Some of this section is from an occupational therapist who came on my show. She offers ergonomics workshops to poker rooms.

  • Learn what the knobs on your chair do. This is step one of her workshop. Many dealer chairs let the seat slide forward and back and tilt. Dealers never learn this because players are waiting, so play with it when you're not dealing. Good dealers adjust the chair every time they sit down.
  • Aim for 90-90-90. Her target is feet flat (or on the chair's base bar if you're short), knees and hips at about 90 degrees with your shoulders stacked over your hips, and elbows relaxed at about 90. Don't lean to one side or cross your legs. It's a target, not all or nothing: shorter dealers may not be able to reach the pot from that position. A new posture can feel worse for a while before it feels better.
  • Turn with the chair, not your neck. Every dealer chair swivels. I teach going with the chair on the pitch. After weeks of 80-hour dealing, my neck gets sore, and it's likely from turning my neck instead.
  • For lower back pain, lower the chair and sit back against it. We've talked about this on my show. It puts more strain on your shoulders and you reach further, so it takes about a week to get used to.

Where your wrists rest

When she explained how resting your wrist on a hard edge compresses the nerve that's involved in carpal tunnel, I realized I rest my non-deck-hand wrist right on the table edge near the tray. The fix is to rest your arm further up, toward the forearm, while still keeping your hands on the table.

Stretches

A student once asked me in class if there were stretches for the hands. At the time I told them the truth: I'd never talked to a professional about it. Intuitively, I sometimes stretch my wrist at the table. If you do, keep your hand above the felt, never below it. Since then, the occupational therapist showed two on my show:

  • A neck stretch: open your chest and look up, the opposite of the dealing posture. Do it at a fixed cue before and after every shift so it becomes a habit.
  • A wrist stretch for the muscles on both sides of the forearm, plus thumb circles for stiffness.

Small habits that add up

  • Lose the watch. I stopped wearing watches when I became a poker dealer. After about three months, my wrist hurt because it was tired from the extra weight.
  • Bring a seat pad. I cut a cheap yoga mat into circles and carry one from table to table. It gives more grip on the chair, and you're not sitting in someone else's sweat. (Don't leave them in a car in a Texas summer. They melt.)
  • Think about your shoes. Where the room allows sneakers, I wear inexpensive solid-black barefoot shoes. Since dealers sit most of the shift, my feet feel free. My co-host prefers a cushioned sneaker with a thick sole. And if you move up to supervisor, you'll be on your feet 8 to 10 hours, so it matters even more.
  • Wash your hands first on every break. I don't touch my face until I've washed my hands, every time, and I get noticeably less sick.
  • Plan your food and water. I eat a healthy breakfast, eat little at work (always a green apple), drink water, and have my coffee at least an hour before my shift. Being stuck at the table needing the bathroom is the worst.
  • Sleep. Fine motor skills smooth out while you sleep. You're often better the next day than you were at the end of practice.
  • Consider learning both hands. I suggested this to a left-handed student. I've sprained my wrist playing basketball, and having the other hand available would have been great.

The occupational therapist also covered grip aids for dry fingertips, which get more common as dealers get older. Check with your room first: some rooms ban finger moisturizers because they gum up the card machines. Clear, textured thumb covers are another option.

It can be a long career

None of this should scare you off. One thing I love about dealing poker is that it has a long shelf life. I know people doing it in their mid-70s, sometimes older. And it might be counterintuitive, since you're sitting, but it keeps you in a little bit of shape. I hurt my lower back years ago, and the one thing that's helped strengthen it more than anything else is dealing poker.

Comfort means longevity. In rooms that allow overtime, being able to work more hours without hurting is a lot of money. For what a shift and a long tournament series actually look like, read a day in the life of a poker dealer.

Common questions about poker dealer health

Is being a poker dealer hard on your body?

It can be. You sit for hours and repeat the same motions thousands of times. But it has a long shelf life: I know people dealing in their mid-70s, sometimes older. Low-tension technique and a good chair setup help you last.

Can dealing poker cause carpal tunnel?

Repetitive wrist motion is the risk. Flicking your wrist when you pitch, or resting your wrist on the table's hard edge, puts strain where carpal tunnel happens. Pitch with your finger, not your wrist.

What is a ganglion cyst, and why do dealers get them?

It's a buildup of fluid under the skin, often at the wrist. In dealers, a high-motion pitch repeated over and over creates friction that can lead to one. A low-motion pitch lowers the risk.

Why does my wrist hurt when I pitch cards?

Too much wrist motion and grabbing the card too high are the two things I see most. Squeezing the deck adds tension in your fingers, too. Reaching across the table with a bent wrist to pull in chips can add to it.

How should a poker dealer sit?

Adjust the chair every time you sit down. Aim for feet flat, knees and hips at about 90 degrees, shoulders over your hips and elbows relaxed. Turn with the chair, not your neck.

Do poker dealers stand or sit?

Poker dealers sit for nearly the whole shift, which is why chair setup and posture matter so much. Supervisors are the ones on their feet.

Learn it right the first time

It's much easier to learn a low-tension pitch and shuffle from the start than to fix a habit after a year of dealing. When it's your turn in Remote Dealer School, I'm watching every detail on camera, and part of what I'm watching for is exactly this.
