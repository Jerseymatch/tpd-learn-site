I was a shift manager in a 29-table poker room and ran more than 1,000 live dealer auditions, so I've seen hiring from the side that makes the decisions. Here's the thing most people miss: a room doesn't hire the best dealer in the world. It hires the best of whoever shows up for the spots it needs to fill. If a room has 25 spots and 50 people apply, one of my Remote School graduates almost always gets one. If there are two spots in a place everyone wants to work, it can take patience.

So once you can deal, where and when you apply matter almost as much as how good you are. This page is about that part. What rooms look for once you're in front of them is in what poker rooms look for, and the audition itself is in how to pass a poker dealer audition.

Where rooms hire the most dealers

Your odds are best wherever a lot of dealers are being hired at once. There are four places that happens.

A room that's opening

This is the easiest way to get your first dealing job. A new room hires in bulk, and it's less strict. It needs dealers who can do the job well enough to open, not five years of perfect mechanics. An established room only hires a few at a time and expects more. Get in at an opening, and you'll improve on the job.

[[moment bFYqqjU9OwY 3:52 | Easiest way to get your first dealing job: find a room that's opening]]

A tournament series that's in town

If one room has one opening and 30 applicants, only the best one gets it. But when a room is running a series with 80 tables, or the WSOP is hiring 1,500 to 1,700 dealers, they take nearly everyone who can deal. (That's also why WSOP dealers aren't always the best dealers.) The summer WSOP hires in waves every year, and the WSOP Circuit stops hire too. You get great odds, and you walk away with experience on your resume. How that hiring works is in dealing the WSOP and the circuit.

[[moment EDme-XjMLRk 42:43 | The best time to get hired: when a tournament series is in town]]

A room that's reopening

When a casino reopens after a long shutdown, many of its former dealers have moved on, so it's a very good time to get in. And if games run short-handed when they reopen, the extra players open more tables. A room can end up needing as many dealers as before, or more, even with fewer players.

[[moment 6fgwvqV1HKc 5:28 | Why a room reopening after a shutdown needs more dealers]]

A big room on its normal turnover

Even a big room that looks full hires regularly. Big rooms always have turnover, so they tend to hire roughly every three months, or every four to six months if they overhired last time. If you didn't get in this round, keep practicing. And don't burn bridges.

Start where you can get hired, then move up

The most desirable rooms usually pick experienced dealers. New dealers usually start at a less desirable room that still pays decently, then move after six to twelve months. That's normal, and it's a good plan. Be willing to relocate if that's where the opportunity is.

Expect the gap to be real. Even after you're hired somewhere, it can take another two to six months on the job before you could pass the audition at a tough room. Some of the toughest rooms audition on unusual games they spread at high stakes, like badugi and ace-to-five. For a room like that, I'd tell most people to get about a year in another room first. And even once you're hired, rooms keep newer dealers off their high-stakes games. When high-stakes players are happy, they gamble elsewhere in the casino too, so rooms send their experienced dealers there.

If you want a high-end room, there are two paths I'd suggest:

  1. Pass an audition at an easier room, deal there six to twelve months, then audition.
  2. Train mixed games and work the WSOP or a circuit series to sharpen them. (Stud, draw and mixed games covers the games.)

Some rooms also start new dealers in tournaments or low-limit games, where a new dealer can do the least damage while learning. If a room does that, it can be a sign it needs dealers.

Card room or casino?

Both are good places to work, and the honest first answer is: start wherever you can get hired. But they feel different.

  • A casino poker room usually means free food in employee dining, more people and more stability. The procedures are stricter, though, and you often can't play there. In a casino, poker makes far less money than slots and table games, so table-games procedure bleeds into the poker room. Table games are the house against the player, so everything is tight, and surveillance follows procedure closely. Sometimes that's killing an ant with a sledgehammer. And in a casino, an executive might walk by while you're chatting with a supervisor and tell you to look busy. That's part of why many dealers like night and overnight shifts: the executives aren't there.
  • A card room is all about poker. It's player against player, and it's less corporate. You have more freedom to be yourself, and the room can be a little more relaxed so players have a good experience.

I slightly prefer card rooms. But both are good places to deal.

Which markets are best for new dealers?

I won't give pay numbers for specific rooms here, but I can tell you the pattern I've seen over and over. New poker markets tip more. For roughly the first six to eighteen months after poker arrives somewhere new, players tip a lot more. I've seen it in Florida, Texas, Maryland and Virginia. Later the market settles down: tips get steadier and a little lower, and the money is more about how many hands you deal per hour. New rooms are also easier to get hired at, so a new market is good news twice over.

Right now, I think Texas is the best overall answer when you weigh the money against the cost of living. And once you have experience, you're not stuck anywhere. Dealers pass auditions in one state after another and work series in other states. Pay in general is covered in poker dealer pay and tips.

Send a video of yourself dealing first

A recorded video audition is a huge advantage. It's easier for the room: a manager can watch it in their downtime, like the overnight manager, instead of scheduling you to come in. And it's easier for you, because an in-person audition at a single room puts a lot of pressure on you.

A clip of yourself dealing a clean hand is worth more than any certificate. Courses usually hand you a certificate of completion, but the casino industry doesn't care about certification. Rooms just want to see if you can do the job. Send them a video and you might get hired across the country without going there first. The WSOP has moved toward video, too.

In my school, the first thing we do for hiring is send rooms the video audition a student has already passed with me. Some rooms are so short on dealers they'll even train you for free.

When a room says you need experience

Plenty of job postings ask for experience you don't have yet. That doesn't have to stop you.

  • Never misrepresent your experience. They'll know the moment you deal. Instead, call or visit and ask a supervisor honestly what they're hiring for and how to get an audition. Be honest and confident.
  • Show up when they're busy. A poker pro and dealer I coach players with gave this advice on one of my streams: go in on a busy Saturday night, find the manager who's running around, and offer to audition right then. If you can pass, the experience doesn't matter. Whatever you do, keep it short and don't tie up the supervisors. How to visit a room the right way is in what poker rooms look for.
  • Get in the door another way. Apply as a chip runner or brush, or take a cashier or host job. You'll be close to the people who hire dealers. That path is in from chip runner to dealer.
  • Get some live practice first. Auditions are intense. Dealing for real people before your audition helps a lot.

Turning a temporary job into a permanent one

A temporary series job is a great foot in the door, but know what you're up against afterward. Some rooms open a permanent job and get 50 to 100 experienced dealers fighting for one to three spots. Others hire a lot of dealers at once, and your odds are much better.

If you're on a temporary contract and want to stay, ask. Nobody brings someone in for months without at least considering them for a full-time spot, and turnover is high. Here's roughly how I'd put it to a supervisor: tell them you're loving it, that you're a little stressed not knowing what happens after the 90 days, and ask whether this could lead to a permanent position. Then let them advise you.

Before you apply: a short checklist

  1. Find out what's on the audition at each room you want. Call and ask. Then practice exactly that.
  2. Look for the volume: openings, reopenings and series coming to your area.
  3. Record yourself dealing a clean hand so you have a video to send.
  4. Be ready to start smaller and move up after six to twelve months.
  5. Get some reps with real people before your first audition.

Common questions about getting a poker dealer job

How do I get a poker dealer job with no experience?

Get trained, then apply where a lot of dealers are being hired at once: a room that's opening, a tournament series or a room reopening. If a posting asks for experience, call or visit and ask a supervisor honestly how to get an audition. A chip runner job is another way in.

When is the best time to apply for a poker dealer job?

When a room is opening, when a tournament series is coming to town, or when a casino is reopening. Big established rooms also hire every few months because of turnover.

Is it easier to get hired at a new poker room?

Yes. New rooms hire in bulk and are less strict. They need dealers who can do the job well enough to open.

Is it better to work at a card room or a casino?

Both are good. Casinos usually mean more stability and perks like employee dining, with stricter procedure. Card rooms are all about poker and more relaxed. I slightly prefer card rooms, but start wherever you can get hired.

How often do poker rooms hire dealers?

Big rooms tend to hire roughly every three months because there's always turnover, or every four to six months if they overhired last time.

Can I get a poker dealer job by sending a video?

It can. A recorded audition is easier for a busy manager to watch, and a clean video of yourself dealing can get you hired somewhere you've never been. It's worth more than a certificate.

Should I move to get a poker dealer job?

If the opportunity is somewhere else, being willing to relocate helps. New markets tip more, and once you have experience you can deal almost anywhere there's a poker room.

Do I need a certificate to get hired as a dealer?

Usually not. Most rooms just audition you and see if you can do the job. More on that in poker dealer school: do you need one?
