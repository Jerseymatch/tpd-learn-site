Is it safe to be a woman poker dealer?

In my opinion, dealing is probably the easiest way for a woman to spend time in a poker room. A casino has heavy camera coverage. Security will walk staff to their cars, at any hour, and they don't complain about it.

What the room is like

I'll be straight with you: poker rooms weren't designed with women in mind. There's constant cursing, distasteful talk and disrespect. You'll hear lines like "Are there any aces in this deck?", which every dealer hears. The 2019 TDA Summit even had a women-in-poker panel that talked about exactly this. Figures quoted there said only about 10 to 15% of casino poker players are women, with far more women playing in bar leagues, online and at home.

Personally, I let most of that talk bounce off me. People who are more sensitive to it feel it all the time. The pros and cons of being a poker dealer covers the rest of what to weigh.

Do players bully women dealers?

Here's the honest answer: bad dealers get bullied. Players pick on the dealer who's making mistakes, so have a plan before you sit down.

The best plan is to give players nothing to bully you about. A dealer I trained, who's in the video below, put it this way: she's presentable, she knows the game and she barely makes mistakes. That doesn't mean you have to be a great poker player. She wasn't one, and I've known plenty of good dealers who barely played before or after.

Men get a hard time from players too. Whoever you are, you need a little fight in you to make players back off. The next section is how I teach doing that without an argument.

When a player challenges you

Sooner or later a player snaps at you. You announce a bet and he says, "I wasn't asking you." You might feel attacked. If you're a woman, you might feel that he isn't respecting you because you're a woman, and you might be right. Either way, here's what I teach: it's not about whether you win the situation.

Tie what you do to the two fundamental rules of poker, cards speak and one player per hand. If a player asks about the bet, you don't have to say a word. Spread the chips out so everyone at the table can see them equally. You've done your job, you haven't engaged, and you haven't helped anyone get information they shouldn't have.

A dealer I trained told me players have doubted her pot-limit Omaha math, then counted it themselves and found out she was right. When a player goes off on her, she says nothing and calls the floor, because she knows the dealer is the one who could lose a job over a blowup, not the player. Talking to players has more on handling the human side.

What I teach

Our instincts go to how we'd act in regular life. If you're the type who doesn't take anything from anybody, the urge is to snap back. At the table you can't. Learn the rules well enough that the rules do the talking: spread the bet so the action is clear to everyone, without a word.

Harassment and players who cross the line

There's a difference between a rude table and a player who crosses the line. If a player puts his hands on you, or anything like that, please don't think of it as part of the job. That goes for any dealer, man or woman.

There was a player in a Florida room who kept putting his hand on dealers' legs. He got kicked out for a week, then a month, then six months. When I was a floor supervisor, I handled complaints about players who thought they could do anything to a dealer. That's not OK.

Here's how it works in practice:

  • Call the floor. Many rooms let dealers give a player a warning, but the nuance is hard, and dealers are in a tough spot because they rely on tips. Whether a player is warned again, moved or asked to leave is the supervisor's decision, not yours. When to call the floor explains how.
  • Expect the floor to stay. After a call about abuse, a supervisor should stay at the table for a while. It's one of the things I want every supervisor trained on.
  • Know that rooms differ. A ban is usually casino-wide, so rooms inside a big casino can be reluctant to kick people out.

Getting hit on at the table

It happens, and the hard part is that you're stuck at the table. You can't walk away from your game.

A dealer I interviewed said it's awkward because the whole table is watching and she doesn't want to embarrass anyone, but the answer is always no. She also keeps a "work personality" at the table to protect herself. That's a trade-off every dealer makes in their own way, and just doing your job well is never wrong.

It also helps to remember an old line I like: shut up and deal. Conversation belongs between hands, never during one.

Bring your own personality

There's no single right way to be at the table. Bring the parts of your personality people like you for, as long as it never gets in the way of the game. I've talked with women dealers about this. One uses a playful style that gets her through situations smoothly. Another is a motherly figure at the table, and there are women dealers in their fifties who have absolute mastery of that "oh, honey" way of correcting a player without making a big deal of it. My style is different: I'm the attentive one who knows the right answer and can give someone a little grief back when they need it.

This is where you get to be yourself, as long as it doesn't get in the way of the game.

Getting started

The path in is the same for everyone. One dealer I trained started as a poker brush (the person who seats players and runs chips) with no poker knowledge, then moved into dealing. You can read about that route in from chip runner to dealer, or start with how to become a poker dealer.

Common questions from women about dealing poker

Is being a female poker dealer safe?

In my opinion, yes. Casinos have heavy camera coverage, and security will walk staff to their cars at any hour. Dealing is probably the easiest way for a woman to spend time in a poker room.

Do female poker dealers get harassed?

It happens. Poker rooms have a lot of cursing and disrespectful talk, and some players cross the line. When they do, call the floor. It isn't part of the job.

What should a dealer do if a player harasses her?

Don't argue. Call the floor and tell the supervisor what happened. The supervisor decides what happens to the player.

Can a poker dealer kick a player out?

No. Some rooms let dealers give a warning, but removing a player is the floor's decision. Your job is to call the floor.

Do you have to be good at poker to be a woman poker dealer?

No. You need to know the rules and procedures well, not play well. Many good dealers rarely play.

How many poker players are women?

Figures quoted at the 2019 TDA Summit put women at about 10 to 15% of casino poker players, with many more in bar, online and home games.
