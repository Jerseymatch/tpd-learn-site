I learned to deal in a casino's own dealer class in 2007. Since then I've taught dealing on YouTube for more than a decade, built a self-paced online course, and now I run a live dealer school over Zoom. So I'm not neutral, and you should know that going in. But I've auditioned more than 1,000 dealers as a supervisor and shift manager, so I've seen what good and bad training looks like at the table.

So here's my honest take on each route. The short version: in-person schools can be excellent, there are now good ways to learn from home, and none of them work if you just show up and wait to be taught.

Do you need a dealer school?

You don't need a certificate. Most casinos don't require a certification from anywhere. A certificate mostly shows that you finished a course. Some rooms do want one, or prior experience, but in my experience most just care that you can deal. Rooms audition you and see if you can do the job, and a good auditioner can just tell. One of the first questions in an auditioner's head is, "Does this feel like poker?"

What you do need is real training. Dealing looks like handing out cards, but there's a whole world of rules that rarely come up and still have to be known, like what to do with an unclear bet or an unclear verbal declaration. Learning to deal from scratch takes about 120 to 140 hours, and it can't be rushed. You need time for it to sink in between sessions. A three-week crash course is far too short.

The ways to train

An in-person dealer school

The traditional route. A typical in-person school runs about 120 to 140 hours, with 5 to 20 students sharing tables. Someone is there to correct you in person, and that matters. Learning from a screen works for some people, but it isn't the same as having someone watch you and fix your hands.

An in-person school also builds a network. The people you meet there can become supervisors and directors, and they can help you get jobs for years. Help your classmates instead of competing with them.

The weaknesses I see: in a traditional school, one person is in the box and everyone else sits around playing. They learn far less than if they were dealing. And many schools don't record you, so you never get to watch yourself.

[[moment M9lBwa8tPNI 1:15:32 | Build relationships in dealer school: classmates become supervisors and directors]]

A casino's own dealer class

Do casinos train dealers? Usually not before hiring, but some run classes, mostly rooms that struggle to find qualified dealers. They range from free to around $1,000. During the dealer shortage, some Florida rooms even paid people to take the class.

That's how I started. A small casino that struggled to find dealers ran its own class, about five weeks of blackjack, Spanish 21, three card poker and Ultimate Texas Hold'em. I was hired after it, and that casino trained me into poker in-house within three or four months. To find out what's near you, call the local poker rooms, ask for a supervisor, say you want to learn to deal, and ask what's available in the area.

A self-paced online course

My Complete Course is about 35 hours of lessons. They need your full attention, in one- or two-hour chunks, plus your own practice time. It works well for some people, especially poker players who already know the rules. The limit is obvious: a course doesn't watch you and correct you. You have to train yourself.

A live remote school

This is what I run now, and as far as I know it was the first one: a full dealer school over Zoom. It's 8 weeks. Everyone has their own table at home and practices at the same time, so nobody sits around waiting for a turn. I dictate the action like an auditioner would. And every correction is recorded, so you don't have to remember five corrections from three minutes. You watch yourself back, the way pro athletes watch themselves on video. More on how it compares to in-person training below, and what students say is in Remote Dealer School student stories.

Free videos and home games

Free videos and dealing home games usually aren't enough on their own, and a play-money casino party isn't casino poker. I cover this more in how to become a poker dealer.

My recommendation: combine them

Not everyone learns from videos. The best odds I know of come from combining an online course with in-person training. Learn the material and practice at home, and get live correction from a school.

There's another reason to combine. Schools teach what they learned as the "correct" way, but there are many correct ways in different regions, and rules differ room to room. I teach dealers all over the world, so I'm less absolute about it. Different perspectives help, and when two sources contradict each other, bring that back to your instructor as a question.

What poker dealer school costs

Prices change, so treat these as rough ranges from what I've seen:

  • In-person dealer schools usually ran about $800 to $1,200 and four to eight weeks when I talked about it around 2020 and 2021.
  • Casino-run classes range from free to around $1,000, and a few rooms have paid people to train.
  • Travel and lodging are the hidden cost. Many people have no dealer school near them, or none they'd want to attend, so they'd have to travel and pay for a place to stay on top of tuition.
  • Your home practice setup is the cheap part. A table like mine is about $150, plus cards and chips. It's what lets you practice between classes.

Look up current prices for any school you're considering, including mine.

What to look for in a dealer school

If you can, visit before you pay. Watch how people are learning, whether they're improving, and whether the staff can answer questions. Then ask:

  • How much will I actually deal? If one person deals and everyone else plays, you'll spend most of your hours watching.
  • Will I be recorded? Watching yourself is how corrections become habits. Most schools don't record students, and it makes a huge difference.
  • Will I practice under pressure? Your first audition feels intense. I remember being shocked by how intense my own first table games audition felt. A good school puts you in audition-style situations more than once before the real one.
  • Can I practice at home? When I took my class, I had nothing to practice on at home, and I would have gotten better faster with my own setup.
  • Do they teach what happens after you're hired? Typical schools teach what you need to pass an audition. Then situation after situation comes up in the casino that nobody taught you, and that overwhelms people.
  • Do they tell you rooms do things differently? There are many correct ways to do things, and rules differ room to room, so it helps to hear more than one.

And don't pick a school for its certificate. Rooms rarely care about certification. Impress them at the audition, and you can usually get hired.

Can you learn to deal poker online?

Yes. You no longer need to move or rent a place near a school. In-person schools can be excellent, and there are now two ways to learn completely from home: a self-paced course or a live remote school. More than 10,000 people have learned from my course online.

The pace of my remote school is the same as an in-person school. The real differences:

  • Everyone deals at once. Every student has their own table, so you deal all class instead of waiting for your turn in the box.
  • You're recorded. You can rewatch the class recordings and watch yourself afterward.
  • The camera adds pressure, on purpose. It's easy to slide through an in-person class. When the camera's on you, the intensity picks up, and that gets you used to the feeling of dealing with a job on the line.

What in-person schools do better: you're at a real table with other people, and students often stay after class and practice together. With remote school, you have to make that practice time yourself.

It doesn't sound intuitive that a dealer school works over Zoom. I only realized it would after years of training course members one-on-one over video. One student told me nothing he'd ever tried over Zoom had worked for him until this.

Whatever you choose, you are your own teacher

This is the biggest mistake I see, and it happens with every kind of training. Schools advertise about 100 hours, and you'll know how to deal. People hear that, relax, show up, finish the hours, and come out almost good enough. Then they don't pass the audition and wonder why it didn't work for them.

The school is your mentor. You are your teacher. Know your weaknesses. If a section is easy, enjoy it. If one is hard, put the extra time in. Practice at home between classes. The people who pass auditions are the ones who go after what they need, and they leave knowing everything the audition will ask for. I'd say the same thing about my own school and course: you have to commit and get what you need out of it, not ride along because you paid. I went through the motions on my engineering degree. Don't do that with this.

Common questions about poker dealer school

Do you need to go to school to be a poker dealer?

No. Most rooms don't require a certificate. They audition you to see if you can do the job. But you need real training from somewhere: a school, a casino class, a course or a remote school.

How much does poker dealer school cost?

Around 2020 and 2021, in-person schools usually ran about $800 to $1,200, plus travel and lodging if there isn't one near you. Casino-run classes range from free to around $1,000. Check current prices, because they change.

How long is poker dealer school?

In-person schools usually run about 120 to 140 hours, over four to eight weeks. My remote school is 8 weeks. Learning from scratch takes about 120 to 140 hours, and three weeks is far too short.

Do casinos train you to be a poker dealer?

Usually not before hiring, but some do, mostly rooms that struggle to find dealers. Some classes are free, and some rooms have paid people to train. Call local poker rooms and ask a supervisor what's available.

Do you need a dealer certification?

Usually not. A certificate mostly shows you finished a course. What gets you hired is passing the audition.

Can you learn to deal poker online?

Yes. A self-paced course works for some people, and a live remote school adds real-time correction and recordings of yourself dealing.

Is a remote dealer school as good as in person?

I think so, and better in one way: you're recorded, so you can watch yourself and fix things. In-person schools give you a real table with other people. Either one only works if you put the practice in.

What should I look for in a poker dealer school?

How much you'll actually deal, whether you're recorded, whether you practice under audition pressure, whether you can practice at home, and whether they teach what happens after you're hired.
