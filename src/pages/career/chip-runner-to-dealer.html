A chip runner job is one of the best ways into a poker room. You're in the building every shift, the supervisors get to know you, and you see how the whole room works from the inside. If you want to deal one day, it's a foot in the door.

I've hired plenty of chip runners. When I was a shift manager in South Florida, I'd train chip runners on a table in the back of the house whenever we had downtime, and that's part of why I started True Poker Dealer in the first place: I wanted somewhere to send people. One of the chip runners I hired back then is now the COO of Texas Card House. A while back I sat down with a chip runner at Texas Card House to talk about the job, and a lot of what's below comes from that conversation.

What a chip runner does

Every room sets the job up a little differently, but the core of it is the same: keep the games running without interruption.

  • Fills. Every dealer has a bank of chips. Buy-ins, tips and (in a lot of rooms) the rake slowly use up the white and red chips. When a dealer gets low, they count out the cash in their well and hand it to you. You verify how much it is, go get replacement chips, and bring them back. The whole point of the bank is that the game can run as long as possible without stopping, so the fill should get the dealer's bank back to where it lasts.
  • Lights. Dealers call you with a light or a button at the table. When I interviewed the chip runner at Texas Card House, every table had a tablet with a chip runner button, and a screen above the cage showed the numbers of the tables that needed one.
  • Time. Some rooms charge players for their seat by the hour instead of taking a rake from each pot. In those rooms, a light goes on when a player's time is up, and the chip runner comes to collect it.
  • Chips for players. Players can always go to the cashier, but a lot of the time a chip runner handles it instead: a big buy-in a dealer doesn't have room to display, chip changes, racking up a player and cashing them out. Some rooms don't do buy-ins at the table at all, so it's all chip runners or the cage.
  • Everything else. Racks for the floor, questions about the waiting list and which game is at which table, the TV channel, even taking drink orders when a waitress needs a break. It really is a "fill in for anything that's needed" job.

The chip runner I interviewed put it well: most of the job isn't the fills and the time, it's keeping too many lights from piling up at once and making sure the players around you are happy. He also told me that when he walks up to a dealer, he looks down at their bank first, so he already knows what they need before they hand him the cash. If a dealer is almost out of white chips, he hurries, because a dealer with no white chips can lose out on tips.

It's a job on your feet

A chip runner is on their feet the entire shift, running around the room. It's the same for supervisors. If you aren't ready to be on your feet for a whole shift, it's going to be rough. Get really comfortable shoes, and I heavily recommend good insoles. (Dealers sit for most of their shift. For what a dealer's day looks like, read a day in the life of a poker dealer.)

Your time as a chip runner is a six-month interview

This is the most important thing on this page. If you get a job as a chip runner, you are being evaluated the whole time to see if you'd make a good dealer. Think of it as an interview that lasts six months or more.

Think about how little a poker room gets to see in a dealer audition: one day, 10 or 15 minutes, maybe a separate interview. With a chip runner, they get six months. They remember how you talked to people, how you handled things, whether you were polite. Dealers sit at tables all day, so supervisors rarely see that side of them. They see it in you.

And the supervisors notice everything. I can relate to this from my own years on the floor. As a supervisor you notice every single thing that goes wrong. But you also notice everything that goes right, smoothly, or faster than you're used to. The supervising staff are always comparing how the room runs with one chip runner versus another. You're also being evaluated, more loosely, as a possible supervisor one day.

So:

  • Show up, on time, every time. Don't miss days and don't call out. Rooms usually won't promote somebody with bad attendance.
  • Work like it matters. A lot of people get a chip runner job, don't realize any of this, and are lazy about it. Then they come in one day and say they want to be a dealer. They might be able to pitch. But they failed their six-month interview.
  • Be good to people. Players, dealers, the cage, the floor. All of it gets remembered.

What I tell students before an audition

If they offer you chip runner, it's a good foot in the door

When students in my class go to audition, I've told them the room may offer them a job like chip runner instead of a dealer spot. That's a really good foot in the door. The next six months are essentially an interview where they get to know you and how nice it is to have you working there.

Learn every job in the room

The chip runner I interviewed had no prior experience in this field at all when he started. His number one tip was to be curious about everything: the host stand, the cage, what the supervisors are responsible for, and what it's like for the dealers. Learning how a dealer thinks made him better at his own job. A dealer wants to get as many hands out as possible in a 30-minute down, so he tries not to interrupt a player while it's their action, and not to make a dealer hand him cash in the middle of a pitch.

There's a second benefit. Supervisors need to know every job in the room. If opportunities come up, usually at slower times, to actually learn the cage job, the host job, or how to deal, take them. When a position opens, it's much easier for a room to hire you over somebody else, because you're coming in partially trained and need way less training.

How long the job itself takes to learn depends on the room. The chip runner I interviewed said it took him a couple of weeks to get comfortable, a couple more to get it right, and two or three months to really have it down. The learning curve isn't crazy high, but you can always up your game, and people notice when you do.

The break room is a classroom

The break room is an excellent place to learn about dealing and supervising. Most of the time, dealers and floors on break are happy to answer literally anything. You never get the same group of people in there twice. Sit down, joke around, and ask how things work and why they're done that way.

Learning to deal while you run chips

Some rooms will help you learn to deal. At the room where I was shift manager, we had a training table in the back of the house. On a slow morning or whenever there was downtime, I'd work with the chip runners there, and also with brushes and even cashiers, having them pitch and work on the basics.

Don't count on the room to do it all, though. You'll still have to audition, and everything I look for in an audition applies to you too. Practice the fundamentals at home: the shuffle, the pitch, cutting chips and working the bank. Then read how to pass a poker dealer audition.

The chip runner who became a COO

When I was a shift manager in Florida, around 2011, I hired a chip runner named Victor. He wasn't even a dealer yet. He was one of the two chip runners I was training in the back when I decided to make True Poker Dealer. We promoted him to dealer, then to dual-rate supervisor, and I did a lot of his training along the way. He went on to open and run poker rooms, and today he's the COO of Texas Card House, the top person there other than the owners. He and I are still close friends, and that's part of how True Poker Dealer and Texas Card House came to work together.

I tell this story in class a lot because you never know who someone is going to be. The person you're running chips for, or the chip runner you're tipping, could be the person who hires you one day. In this industry, we don't burn bridges.

Money and tips

Chip runners typically get a base wage plus tips, and the tips are what make the job worth it. Players tip chip runners, and dealers often do too. It's customary in Florida and Texas, though I don't know about everywhere. I always over-tip the chip runners. They work off tips like we do, they get a lower base pay and less opportunity for money, and it's better for me if they're excited to come to my table.

It's honestly hard to work in a poker room where the dealers make more money than you every day. But it can be enough to live on while you train yourself to get to that point. For what dealers earn, read poker dealer pay and tips.

Common questions about chip runners

What does a chip runner do in a poker room?

A chip runner brings chips to the tables. They do fills for dealers whose banks are running low, get chips for players, cash players out, collect time in rooms that charge by the hour, and help with anything else the room needs. The real job is keeping the games running without interruption.

Is being a chip runner a good way to become a poker dealer?

Yes. It gets you inside the room, and the supervisors get months to see how you work and treat people, instead of a 15-minute audition. Show up every shift, work hard and learn everything you can.

Do you need experience to be a chip runner?

Not necessarily. The chip runner I interviewed had no prior experience in the field when he was hired. What the job does demand is being on your feet all shift and being good with people.

Do chip runners get tipped?

Yes. Players tip chip runners, and in Florida and Texas, dealers usually tip them too. The tips are a big part of what makes the job worth it.

Is a chip runner the same as a brush?

The jobs overlap. The chip runner I interviewed said he sometimes picks up brush work like cleaning up. Some busy rooms also give dealers a "brush down" in the rotation instead of a break: 15 to 20 minutes handling lights, fills and player needs instead of dealing.

How long does it take to go from chip runner to dealer?

It depends on the room and on you. Treat the whole time as your interview, practice dealing on your own, and when you're ready, let the room know you want to deal.
