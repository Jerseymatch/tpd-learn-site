The audition is the test that gets a poker dealer hired. The casino doesn't care about your degree, and nobody is going to ask for a dealer certificate. They put you in the box and watch you deal, because they need to be comfortable having you deal poker in their poker room, with real money on the table.

I've been on the other side of that table a lot. At Calder Casino I was hired as a supervisor months before the poker room opened, and I ran about 90% of the dealer auditions there. Over my years as a supervisor and shift manager I ran more than 1,000 auditions and hired around 300 dealers. That's the part of my career I lean on most when I teach. This guide is what I'd tell you before you walk in.

What the auditioner is really looking for

There are three things: your technical skill, your communication, and whether it feels like poker. The last one is the big one. When I'm deciding whether someone passes, the number one question in my head is: does it feel like poker with this person in the box?

"Feels like poker" means the game moves and looks the way a real game does. Your shuffle, your pitch, how you bring in bets and push a pot all look like something a trained dealer does. Anything that falls way outside normal procedure is incredibly loud to an auditioner, even if it's a small thing. When I see something like that, I get uncomfortable right away, because I'm thinking: what else is this person going to do that's that far outside the procedure? That's why I care so much about little habits. It's not just about the audition. It's about looking like a dealer in every way, so no red flags go off.

Communication is the other piece people underestimate. Talking your game clearly, announcing the action and the pots, explaining a rule when a player asks: that's one of the secrets to making a mistake and still getting hired. I work on it with students from about the fourth week of class.

They will notice your speed, and you can't be painfully slow. But if you deal at a steady, comfortable pace you'll be fine, and accuracy matters far more. A lot of students make the mistake of speeding up before they're ready because they believe they have to be fast to pass. It's about being clean, not about being fast. Speed comes with time on the job.

What happens at a poker dealer audition

There's no single format. It's all over the map. Here's what you're likely to run into.

  • One at a time, or a group. When I ran auditions, sometimes one or two people came in and I'd take each one for 15 to 30 minutes. When I could, I'd get 10 people in at once. One person deals and everyone else plays, and the whole group stays for a few hours. Expect higher-end poker rooms to take closer to 20 to 30 minutes per person. At a smaller room you might be the only one there, and they might just have you shuffle, pitch and answer a couple of questions.
  • Usually not a live game. Poker auditions are generally not on live tables. More often it's a training table, with the auditioner or other applicants playing.
  • The auditioner calls the action. They tell you how many hands to deal and what each player does: call, raise, fold, all in. They'll throw in things to test you, like asking what the blinds are, or making players buy in or give you big chips so you have to make change from the bank.
  • Several games. A typical audition starts with hold'em. If they like you, they may move to Omaha to see whether you can read hands. Some rooms now audition pot-limit Omaha, and I've even heard of a double-board PLO bomb pot on an audition. (That's where poker is right now.)
  • At least one big all-in. When I put a six-way all-in on an audition, I'm not grading how you move the chips. I'm checking that you build the pots correctly and the right players get the right money. Build the pots out and announce them. That gives you more leeway if something goes wrong.
  • Showdown on their terms. Some auditioners will ask you to read every hand. If they don't ask, treat it like a real game and read the winning hand. Wait for the auditioner to tell you when to have the players turn their cards over.
  • Sometimes an interview. There are no set questions, and it isn't that kind of job. They might pull you aside for 10 minutes or ask you about some rules. What matters most to them is that you're reliable and you show up on time, so say so. If they ask a rule you're not 100% sure about, say what you think it is and that you'd call the floor in your first month until you're sure. That's better than guessing.
  • Sometimes a video. Some rooms will do an audition over video, or ask you to send in a video of yourself dealing.

Rooms have their own quirks, too. If they ask you to do something a specific way, even something I've never seen in the industry, do it their way.

What I tell every class

At a group audition, the poker you play is the interview

If 10 of you show up and one person deals while nine play, the time you spend in a seat is your interview. I've watched people deal a nice hand, then sit down and be a jerk to everyone else at the table. Not hired. And every time I've run a group audition, somebody at the table takes shots at the dealer, trying to make them look bad. You don't get the job by making someone else fail. Treat people well while you wait.

Find out exactly what's on your audition

This is the easiest edge you can give yourself, and most people never do it. Call the poker room and ask.

  1. Look up the poker room's phone number.
  2. Ask to speak to the poker room supervisor.
  3. Tell them you're a student at a poker dealer school and you want to know what their audition is.
  4. Write down what they say: the games, the stakes, anything specific.

Rooms have no problem telling you. What's on the audition isn't the part they try to trick you on. (In the audition itself, they might.) In my school, there's a day where students call the rooms they want to work at while someone else is dealing, and then I make sure each student practices exactly what their room tests. My students are all over the country, and every region's auditions are a little different.

The habits that can fail you

Most auditions aren't lost on one big mistake. They're lost on a habit the auditioner sees once and can't unsee. These are the ones I stop students for in class, because I don't want anybody walking into an audition with a fail trigger. A few of them are instant fails. The rest make an auditioner nervous about what else you'll do.

  • Covering the top of the deck. Putting your hand or fingers on top of the deck, even for a second after you pick it up or pitch the last card, is not allowed in poker. The WSOP has historically failed people for it. It's one of the things I focus on most.
  • Cutting the deck with two hands. One hand cuts, then that hand puts the deck in your deck hand. I've stopped students for doing it while talking. It's an instant fail.
  • Crossing your arms, or a hand below the table. Either one alone can fail you. Keep your hands visible and above the table, even while you tidy up the cards.
  • Grabbing chips in your palm. Use your fingertips. Never pick up a pot or scoop chips into your hand.
  • Going into the muck. A dealer never goes into the muck, not even to answer a player's question. Doing it fails an audition.
  • Your pot sitting on top of the muck. It's a big no-no. I failed someone in an audition for exactly this.
  • Moving chips without counting them. If you move chips into the main pot and pull some back out without recounting, it looks like you're taking money from the pot.
  • Counting the pot chip by chip. Don't physically count down the pot. It takes up everybody's time. You should already know how much is in there.
  • Breaking bets down too close to the pot. It scares the players, because it signals you might make a mistake. Break bets down clearly, away from the pot, so the players, surveillance and the auditioner can all verify them.
  • Non-standard chip cuts. Twenty reds for $100 always go in stacks of five. Working dealers use shortcuts, like cutting a $60 bet as 20-20-20, but non-standard stacks make surveillance review harder and could fail you in an audition. Do what you want once you're hired.
  • Holding the deck and talking. Once the deck is shuffled, pitch. Holding the deck while you chat makes an auditioner uncomfortable with your pace. If you're going to talk, keep dealing while you do it.
  • Cursing at the table. It's something you don't want to do in an audition.

What I correct in class

The two most important parts of the job

I quiz my classes on this constantly: take the rake, and verify every single bet amount. When I ran auditions, I saw people forget the rake all the time, and a supervisor will always notice. Taking the wrong rake, even a dollar too much, is the kind of error that's disqualifying, and being short on the rake is close to unrecoverable unless the spot lets you fix it cleanly. And if you realize mid-audition that you missed a rake (say, in a split pot), don't try to fix it quietly. Call the floor.

When you make a mistake (you probably will)

Here's the biggest myth about auditions: that you have to be perfect, and one mistake means you failed. It doesn't. Most people who walk in to audition know there's still stuff they're not perfect at, and they're terrified any mess-up will cost them the job. In my school we literally teach you how to make mistakes and still pass the audition.

  • Call the floor. Calling the floor is not failing. The people hiring know that floor calls happen to everyone, including dealers with years in. I have floor calls too. A floor call is your chance to show them you can handle a tough spot, and handled well, it can make you memorable. They're very interested in how you handle pressure.
  • Don't fix it yourself. The urge when you've messed up is to go back into the muck or rearrange the chips. Don't. Tell the floor what happened, accurately (which street, how much was in the pot, what each player had), and let them decide, even if you think their decision is wrong.
  • Acknowledge it, don't grovel. You want to acknowledge the mistake, not be apologetic about it. Keep your head up and keep a good impression.
  • The next hand is your chance to prove yourself. Never give up on yourself during an audition.
  • If a card might have been exposed, say so. If a card flips during the cut, I say out loud that it could have been exposed and I shuffle again. To an auditioner, that's integrity.
  • Lost track of the shuffle? Restart it and move on casually.
  • Don't know how to handle something? Be honest and humble about it. If you've never quartered anyone before, say so and ask the simplest way they'd like you to do it. That's fine.

When a spot comes up where you aren't sure what the room wants, like a strange setup after a hand, just ask: "What would you like me to do with these?" It's a really good habit as a dealer never to make your own decisions. Always defer to the floor.

Deal by the book (even stricter than a real game)

In an audition, and for your first six months on the job, you enforce the rules strictly, even in spots where an experienced dealer or a floor person might let something go. My favorite example: an older gentleman playing 1/2 no-limit puts $2 in, not realizing it's been raised to $15. An experienced dealer might let him take the $2 back. You make him keep it in. Seasoned dealers learn the nuance over time. You're focused on passing the audition and building the right fundamentals.

  • No shortcuts unless you ask. If you want to use a shortcut, like leaving bets out and running the board, check with the auditioner first. I'll ask, "I'm happy to make these pots, I'm also comfortable doing it the other way. Do you have a preference?" If they don't say, build the pots.
  • Build pots from the main pot out. It's really hard to hire somebody who does a multiway all-in inside-out on an audition. Nobody cares how many hands you get out. Handle the main pot first and work your way out.
  • A "pot" declaration is binding. Once a player says pot, they can't decide to fold instead. Letting them is an automatic fail.
  • Follow their instructions exactly. Sometimes an auditioner tests whether you can follow instructions while you're dealing, like telling you to use certain chips. Listen for it.
  • Call the floor for anything involving money changing hands. If you'd take money out of the pot to give to a player, or a player tries to take back an under-call, call the floor in an audition unless you're 100% sure.

The rules side is where a lot of working dealers slip. Unclear bets and whether an all-in reopens the action are two things even hired dealers get wrong. Nail them in your audition and you've shown you're not one of those dealers. I cover both in the technical rules of betting.

If your audition includes pot-limit Omaha

If there's PLO on your audition, they'll test the pot math right away, often the first time someone says "pot." Here's the formula dealers use:

Three times the previous bet, plus the trail, plus the pot.

  • The trail is any money already out in front of the other players this round, including blinds.
  • The trail in front of the player whose turn it is never counts. Their own chips are already part of what they'd have to call.
  • An all-in for less doesn't get multiplied by three. It becomes part of the trail.

In a 300/600 game, if the first player to act raises the pot: three times 600 is 1,800, plus the 300 small blind trail, plus nothing in the pot, is 2,100.

Track the pot every street before you bring the bets in, so you never have to count it when someone asks. (In pot-limit you do tell a player how much is in the pot when they ask. In no-limit you don't.) And it's fine to talk it out on the way to the number, "three, four, five times 600 is 3,000," as long as you get there quickly and get it right. It's more important to be right than to look like a computer. Fumbling the math out loud is what scares someone watching.

The rest is repetition. The first time you see a calculation you have to figure it out. By the hundredth time, it's memorized. That's mostly why experienced PLO dealers look like rock stars.

How to practice for your audition

  • Set up like the real thing. A tray (or a rack if you don't have one), a stack of your smallest chips for change (you don't need a ton), spacers, a button and a cut card. You can use a joker as a cut card, but it presents poorly. Think of it like dressing right for any interview.
  • Practice each correction until it's a habit. At least 20 reps of anything you're fixing, and keep going until your brain doesn't have to think about it. Once you start the reps for a correction, finish them that day. Then sleep on it.
  • Deal under pressure. Practicing alone has no pressure, and an audition is all pressure. Get someone to call the action and watch you, or record yourself and watch it back for every correction. Feeling that pressure in practice is what desensitizes you to it.
  • Practice the hard version. If you can handle a multiway all-in that takes 45 minutes in practice, a real audition hand won't faze you.
  • Get the fundamentals in your instincts. I can tell how someone was trained the second they make change in the bank. When an auditioner throws a monkey wrench at you, the basics have to run on instinct so your attention can go to the problem.

The core skills are all on this site: the shuffle, the pitch, working the bank, side pots and the rake.

What to wear to a poker dealer audition

Traditional casino attire for a poker dealer audition is a white button-down dress shirt, black dress pants and black shoes. You don't need a tie, and you don't have to button the top button.

If you don't pass

Whether you get hired comes down to supply and demand as much as skill. Every auditioner has a pool of dealers to choose from. If the pool is strong, expectations go up. If a room has two openings and 50 applicants, that's hard. If a room is hiring 200, or the WSOP is hiring 1,500 to 1,700 dealers, it's much easier. So if you're close, think about where you audition, not just how.

Some rooms make you wait before you can try again, sometimes months. When someone on a pathway call is close, I've told them to go for it anyway, and if they don't pass, keep training while they wait, so they walk into the next one ready. For the bigger picture of getting your first job, read how to become a poker dealer.

Common questions about poker dealer auditions

What happens at a poker dealer audition?

You deal hands while the auditioner calls the action. Expect hold'em, often a multiway all-in, making change from the bank, and sometimes Omaha or a quick rules question. It can be you alone for 15 to 30 minutes or a group where everyone takes turns dealing and playing.

How long is a poker dealer audition?

Usually 15 to 30 minutes per person. Group auditions can keep everyone there for a few hours. A small room might just have you shuffle, pitch and answer a couple of questions.

Can you make a mistake and still pass?

Yes. One mistake doesn't fail an audition. What matters is what you do next: stay calm, call the floor, don't try to fix it yourself, and deal the next hand well.

Does calling the floor during an audition mean you failed?

No. Floor calls happen to every dealer, and the people hiring know it. Handling one calmly and accurately can actually help you.

How fast do you need to deal to pass?

A steady, comfortable pace is fine. Clean and accurate beats fast. Don't speed up before you're ready. Speed comes with time on the job.

What can get you failed at a poker dealer audition?

Cutting the deck with two hands, scooping chips into your palm, going into the muck and letting a player back out of a "pot" bet are instant fails. Covering the top of the deck, crossing your arms and taking the wrong rake can fail you too.

Do I need a dealer certificate to audition?

No. Poker rooms care whether you can deal, and the audition shows them. A video of you dealing a clean hand says more than a certificate.
