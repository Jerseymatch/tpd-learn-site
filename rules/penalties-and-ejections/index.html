What Gets You Kicked Out of a Poker Room? Penalties and Ejections

Most bad behavior at a poker table never gets anyone thrown out. But some things do, in a well-run room. Here's what crosses the line, how tournament penalties work, who decides, and what the dealer's job is when it happens.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 20, 2026 · 10 min read

I spent years as a floor supervisor and shift manager, and I've probably kicked out 500 poker players in my career. Some of those were for the day, and some were permanent. It depends what you do. So I can tell you what actually gets a player removed from a poker room, and what usually doesn't.

The short version: in a cash game, the room's only real tool is not letting you play. In a tournament, there's a second tool, the penalty, where you sit out hands. Either way, the decision belongs to the floor supervisor, not the dealer. The dealer's job is to call the floor, fast, and say exactly what happened.

What gets a player kicked out

These are the things that get a player removed for the day or longer:

  • Cheating. Always. (How casinos catch cheating at poker covers what surveillance looks for.)
  • "Let's take it outside." Any version of asking somebody to fight gets you out for the day in a well-run room.
  • Fighting. Players fighting each other is an ejection.
  • Cursing at someone. Cursing to yourself after a bad beat isn't over the line. Cursing at another player or at the dealer is.
  • Raising your voice so that a supervisor can hear it from far away.
  • Damaging cards or throwing chips. In my opinion, players who spike cards at the dealer should be kicked out.
  • Touching a dealer. There was a player who kept putting his hand on dealers' legs. He got kicked out for a week, then a month, then six months. If that ever happens to you, man or woman, please don't think it's part of the job.

Most of these come with a lot of judgment. A supervisor finds out what happened and hears the player's side first. And behavior that doesn't break any one rule can still get a player banned if it keeps hurting the game for everyone else.

Out of the poker room, or out of the casino?

The poker shift manager and security decide that together. Sometimes it's just the poker room. A 24-hour ban can follow the casino's gaming day rather than the clock. In a poker room attached to a full casino, a ban is usually casino-wide, and that's one reason casinos don't like kicking people out.

Fights are rarer than people think

People imagine poker rooms are full of fights. In my experience, they're rare. I've seen maybe seven in ten years. When one did break out, the players at the table broke it up. Poker is a confrontational game, though, and drinking plays a part. They happen more at night. Fights almost never come out of nowhere, which is exactly why the dealer can usually prevent them. More on that below.

Cash games vs tournaments

In a tournament, a penalty means something. The player misses hands, and there's a limited number of them. In a cash game, the hands are unlimited and each one stands alone. You can't give someone a penalty and have it mean much to them. So in a cash game, the room's weapon to get people to follow the rules is not letting them play.

In a cash game

Take acting out of turn. In a cash game, the out-of-turn action is its own penalty, because the skipped player now knows what's coming and can use it. The first time, the dealer tells the player to wait their turn. If it keeps happening, the dealer lets a supervisor know, the supervisors log it, and a player who won't stop gets kicked out. Notice what's not on that list: killing the player's hand. Killing a hand is about the worst thing you can do in a poker room, short of collusion. The industry got away from killing hands as a penalty because it's too harsh. (Action out of turn and dead hands cover both.)

Taking chips back is similar. A player who reaches back into the pot for a call puts themselves at risk of getting kicked out. A new player might just get a reminder. An experienced player taking a shot is a floor call, because the floor needs to know it might happen again, and eventually they stop letting that player play. Other things, like folding when nobody has bet, the room can't do much about in a cash game.

In a tournament

In a tournament, a player who breaks the rules can be given a penalty: sitting out for a round, or a set number of hands. A supervisor can scale it to the damage. Skipping one player in level 1 isn't the same as an angle in a heads-up all-in pot. In rooms I've worked, the first time is usually a warning and the second a penalty. In my experience, a player who's on penalty isn't allowed in the tournament area while they serve it. Their stack still gets dealt in and still posts blinds, and since they aren't at their seat, the hand is dead.

Things that can bring a tournament penalty include:

  • Exposing cards during a hand, even by accident while folding, and showing cards to a neighbor.
  • Heads-up players showing their cards, unless they're the last two players in the whole tournament.
  • Acting out of turn, scaled to how much it affected the hand.
  • Folding when nobody has bet, in rooms that enforce it.
  • Dodging blinds. A player who takes their time getting to a new seat after their table breaks, or holds onto a seat ticket until the blinds pass, is supposed to get a penalty under the TDA rules.

Penalties are rarer than you'd think. In all the tournaments I've dealt, I've almost never had a player on penalty at my table. How to deal a poker tournament covers everything else that's stricter in tournaments.

Who decides: the floor, never the dealer

The dealer runs the game and explains the rules. The supervisor makes the decision. That's the best thing about being a dealer: you never have to throw anybody out, and you never have to argue about a penalty. Some rooms let dealers give a player a warning. But the nuance is hard, and dealers are in a tough spot, because they rely on those same players for tips. Removing a player is the floor's decision every time.

How a good supervisor handles it

When I made these decisions, I started by finding out what happened, and hearing the player's side first. I'd pull the player aside and lead with "What happened?" Let them speak so they don't feel cornered. Acknowledge the parts that make sense. Then explain why the decision stands. A lot of players I ejected left understanding why.

On a close call, the floor's conversation is a hunt for the truth, and then an application of the rules. It's serious and efficient. Supervisors also have to be careful not to set a precedent, because players and staff remember rulings and expect the same thing next time. And when things are escalating between two players, supervisors are often taught to split them up, or eject both.

What the dealer does

Your job is small but it matters a lot. You call the floor, and you report what happened.

  • Call early. Fights almost never come from nothing. As soon as two players start going at each other, call the floor, loudly, with your table number. That call is how a dealer prevents it.
  • Some things are always a floor call. Anything that sounds threatening. Someone cursing at another player, with no exceptions. A racist comment, every time. A player cursing at you is a floor call too.
  • Report only what happened. No opinions. Tell the supervisor, as accurately as you can, what was said and by whom, including both players. (Calling the floor covers how to make the call clean.)
  • Handle the small stuff lightly. For excessive casual cursing that isn't aimed at anyone, a chill "language, language, come on" usually works. Frame it by the place, "we don't do that here," rather than "there's a woman at the table." If it continues, alert a supervisor instead of telling the player what to do.
  • If a fight breaks out, step back. Yell for the floor and security until they come. A poker bank is small, not like a table games bank, and nobody expects you to take blows protecting it.
  • Tell someone after. If a player was making the table uncomfortable without technically crossing a line, tell a supervisor after your down. Staff can act on that.

It's easy as a dealer to go with the room's flow, especially when tips are involved, and let things slide because the players say it's no big deal. Call the floor on any confrontation anyway. Talking to players covers handling a player who's insulting you.

What I correct in class

Adding your opinion to the floor call

When students role-play a floor call, they tend to add what they think: what should or shouldn't be allowed, or what the player meant. The supervisor only needs what physically happened. Let them reach the conclusion.

Arguing instead of calling

Once a player resists, a student will keep explaining, even when the student is right. Don't. As soon as there's resistance, call the floor.

Knowing where the line is

In class I'll run a scenario where one player calls another stupid, and then the other one curses back. At the name-calling, the dealer says something like, "Hey, we don't talk to people like that here." When it turns into cursing at somebody, that's "Floor," with your table number.

If you're a player

Most players will never get anywhere near a penalty. Follow poker etiquette, protect your hand, and act in turn. If someone else at the table is crossing a line, like using slurs, don't confront them yourself. Players who take it on directly risk getting thrown out too. Slide away, and quietly tell a supervisor. Offer to be involved if it helps. In a small room, ask to meet somewhere out of sight. That gives the room what it needs to deal with it.

Common questions about penalties and ejections

Can a poker dealer kick a player out?

No. Some rooms let a dealer give a warning, but removing a player is the floor supervisor's decision. The dealer's job is to call the floor and say what happened.

Can you get banned from a poker room?

Yes. Bans range from the rest of the day to permanent, depending on what you did and your history. Cheating can mean anything from 24 hours to life. In a room attached to a casino, a ban often covers the whole property.

Can you get kicked out of a poker room for cursing?

Cursing to yourself after a bad beat usually isn't a problem, though rooms differ. Cursing at another player or the dealer is a floor call, and it can get you removed.

What is a penalty in a poker tournament?

It's time away from the table, like sitting out a round or a set number of hands, given by the floor for breaking a rule. Your stack still gets dealt in and posts blinds while you're gone, and those hands are dead.

Can you get a penalty in a cash game?

Not usually. A penalty doesn't mean much when the hands are unlimited. Cash games use warnings, and a player who keeps breaking the rules gets removed.

Does the dealer decide the penalty?

Never. The dealer explains the rule and calls the floor. The supervisor decides whether there's a warning, a penalty or an ejection.
