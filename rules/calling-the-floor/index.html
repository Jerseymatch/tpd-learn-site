When Should a Dealer Call the Floor?

Calling the floor is the best tool a dealer has. You never have to fight with a player. Here's when I call, exactly how to do it, and how to talk to the supervisor once they're standing behind you.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 16, 2026 · 15 min read

The dealer runs the game and enforces the rules. The floor supervisor makes the rulings. That split is the best thing about the job: you never have to fight with a player. You state what happened, and as soon as a player gives you resistance, you call the floor and let the supervisor handle it. You can even root for the player to get what they want.

New dealers worry about bothering the floor. Don't. The industry instructs dealers to call the floor if there's any resistance. Making things up instead is the habit that gets dealers fired. I spent years as a supervisor and shift manager, and the floor would much rather walk over to your table than clean up a mess afterward.

The short answer: when to call

When you're new, the absolute safest way to handle anything you're not sure about is to call the floor. Integrity is always there when you call. Nothing bad happens. Here are the situations that should always go to the supervisor:

SituationWhy it's the floor's
A player resists or disputes what you said, even when you're rightYou never argue with a player. Once there's resistance, it's the supervisor's job.
A player asks for a floorJust call it. Don't try to find out why first.
You made a mistake (chips pulled into the wrong pot, a live hand mucked, a pot pushed wrong)Dealers don't fix their own mistakes. You never take money back out of a pot without a floor.
A premature flop, turn or riverAlways a floor call. See premature cards.
Your muck slides onto a player's live cards, or a folded card lands on someone's handOnce cards touch, you technically don't know what's what anymore.
A card goes off the table or on the floorSome rooms, Texas Card House among them, have a supervisor pick it up.
You set the deck down and need to pick it back upThe supervisor is the witness that nothing fishy happened.
Substantial action questions on a misdealIt can differ room to room, so don't guess. See substantial action.
A player shows their cards to someone who then tells them what to doA serious one player per hand violation.
A player curses at another player, or anything sounds threateningCursing at somebody always crosses the line.
Something makes no sense, even if the whole table agrees to itThe table can't approve something nonsensical. Neither can you.
Words, gestures or a situation you've never seen beforeNew dealers don't interpret. The floor does.

And a lot of things you don't need the floor for, unless the player resists: making a player complete a raise under the 50% rule, an oversized chip being a call, a string bet, a card you flashed on the deal, or "you said pot, I need the 25." You state it and keep the game moving. The moment they push back, it becomes a floor call.

How often dealers call the floor

Because we're going through all of these at once, it sounds like they come up a lot. They don't. A decent dealer calls the floor zero to four times over the course of a day. When I was dealing eight-hour shifts, I averaged maybe once a day, maybe twice.

A floor call takes about 45 seconds to a minute and a half. If you ever find yourself skipping one because you can't afford to lose a hand or two of tips, look yourself in the mirror. Getting away with stuff has no real benefit. The floor coming to the table is an important part of the game.

How to call the floor

  1. Say "Floor," then your table number. "Floor, table six." Not "I need to call the floor." Some rooms say "supervisor" instead; use whatever your room uses. Always say the table number. It's how they find you, and if you don't get in that habit, supervisors will get in the habit of disliking you.
  2. Say it loud, and raise your hand over your head so they can see where the call is coming from as well as hear it. If they don't hear you, wait a moment and say it again.
  3. Stop the hand. In almost every case, when you call the floor, you stop the hand in progress. The rare exceptions will make sense when you get there.
  4. Freeze. Once you've called, don't touch anything unless you're instructed to. Don't start moving chips around to fix it before they arrive.
  5. Don't announce that you made a mistake. Not to the table, not out loud to yourself. "Oops, I messed up" isn't the procedure. Calling the floor is.

I'm teaching what I know from the United States. I've never dealt outside it, and it wouldn't surprise me if some countries don't want dealers calling out across the room.

What to say when the supervisor arrives

This is the part dealers almost never get trained on, and it's one of the most helpful things you can do for the supervisor and the table. Most dealers over-explain or editorialize. Here's the cleaner way.

Only say what happened, in order

Point to each seat and walk through the action: "This player bet 10. This player called. This player raised to 30. This player threw out two green chips, and I announced raise to 50. Then seat one called 50, and seat two asked if seat eight was supposed to be a call." That's it. You're not saying what anybody intended, what the rule is, or what should happen next. Only what happened.

Start at the beginning, then get to what went wrong. Don't open with the mistake. If a player said something unusual, give the supervisor the exact words the player said, not just the confusion it caused.

Get the details right

The details matter, because the supervisor is going to take over the hand from what you tell them. In class, a student gave an almost perfect explanation, with every number right, but said it happened on the turn when it was really the flop. In an audition, that alone could fail you, because the supervisor is going to tell you to bring the next card based on the street you gave them.

Don't say "intent," and don't propose a ruling

Don't say "he didn't mean to," or "do you want to go check the cameras?", or anything else that tells the supervisor what the rule is or how to solve it. Those are the supervisor's conclusions to reach. "Intent" is a bit of a trigger word for some supervisors, too. Instead of "it was a string bet," say "I viewed it as a string bet." It gives the supervisor room.

Don't make it bigger than it is

If you made the mistake, say it plainly: "These two players were in for 1,000. This player was short. I brought the bets in and brought the turn." You don't need to say "I made a huge mistake." Players who play a lot see this sometimes, and they're likely to tell an upset player, "It happens, you're fine." If you call it huge, nobody can defend you.

Be honest, and be humble about what you remember

Never lie about the circumstances. If a player remembers it differently than you do, you don't need to be that serious about it: "Look, I could be wrong, but this is what I remember." Only say you're 100% sure when you are. Saying "I'm pretty sure it was three white chips" when the player says it was a red chip implies he's lying. There's a neutral ground where you can be humble, and right or wrong, the supervisor is still going to figure it out.

Once they're there, it's between the supervisor and the players

  • If players start talking to you, you don't respond unless the floor asks you to.
  • Answer only the question you're asked. If the supervisor asks how much the bet was, don't recount the whole hand. When I was on the floor, I'd sometimes say to a dealer, "I need you to do exactly what I'm saying."
  • Follow their instructions exactly, one step at a time.

Never say "never mind"

If the situation sorts itself out before the supervisor gets there, tell them what it was anyway: "It's resolved now. Someone said fold. I thought it was this player. It wasn't." Having been a floor for years, I can tell you there's a feeling when you walk over and the dealer says "never mind": that the players talked the dealer into something they weren't supposed to do. That's the number one fear.

What I correct in class

Fixing your own mistake

When you're dealing, your instincts as a human kick in. You made a mistake, so you fix it. Watching someone else, it's easy to say, "Call the floor, what are you doing?" In the moment it's hard. We don't allow dealers to fix their own mistakes. In class, a student pulled all of a player's chips into the pot instead of just the call amount, and started working out how to count the pot and sort it out. We stopped and did the floor call instead.

"But both players are fine with it"

Students ask: if both players are okay with the dealer handing back a chunk of change, do you still have to call the floor? Yes. The casino industry views quietly fixing it as you putting your job at risk over something that doesn't matter. The players, who tip you, want things to keep moving. After years in a room, there are times I might handle something like that myself. If I tell a new dealer to do it, they could lose their job. Teach yourself to never do it.

Calling a floor too early

It goes the other way too. A player said "pot," then tried to fold, and a student jumped to "Would you like me to call the floor?" That's premature. Just say, "No, no, I need the 25. You said pot," and keep it moving. If they still resist, then it's a floor call.

What never to say to the player

Never tell a player what the floor is going to rule. Don't say, "The supervisor is going to say the same thing." That's ego. And if the floor rules the other way, you're stuck dealing to that player for the rest of your down, maybe 30 minutes. Stay neutral: "Let's find out what the supervisor says. I hope they let you do it."

Years ago, a player facing a 20 bet said "I raise 65," and he meant 65 more. I argued the rule with him. Today I'd just say I'm happy to get the floor and find out. It's not about my ego.

Be on the player's side, too. Instead of flatly telling someone they can't raise, try: "Listen, I can't make it a raise at this point. If you'd like, I can call the floor and we can see if they allow it." Now they know they have your support; you're just the one enforcing the rules. Often they'll say, "It's fine, I'll just call," and the decision was theirs. And if the floor does let them raise, you don't care.

When a player wants you to break a house rule, my script is: "This is a rule of the house. Would you like me to call the floor?"

Offer the floor, or call it?

Offering is fine for a rule you're enforcing. But if a player says you actually made a mistake, call the floor; don't offer. Someone at the table will probably tell the floor anyway, and hiding a mistake is far more serious than whatever training or paperwork comes from calling it.

When you're new: call it every time

For your first six months to a year, call the floor any time you're not sure. That's how you find out what your room expects of you. A few spots where I tell my students to call every single time early on:

  • Cards land on another player's hand. A card tossed across the table lands on a player's live cards. Maybe you're sure which is which. Call the floor anyway. It's very unlikely, but possible, that someone did it on purpose to help a friend.
  • A player brings a big stack forward and then tries to fold. If it's $5 or $10 to call, I'm not that worried. If it's $440 to call and they brought a stack of green out, call the floor. A player can bring chips out, watch how the others react, and then decide. A lot of places make them put that money in. Say, "I actually don't know if you're allowed to fold now. Let me just call the floor, and we'll find out."
  • A player shows their cards in a tournament. Call the floor every time.
  • Words you don't understand. A player says "going up" and throws out a call. Is that a call or a raise? An experienced dealer can probably handle that. A new dealer says, "You said 'going up' and put out a call amount. That could be read as a raise. I'm just going to get a floor over here."
  • You inherit a messy bank. If the bank is a disaster when you sit down, call the floor as soon as you start organizing it, so they see it.
  • You go blank. If you lose your way on a chop or a quarter, call the floor. Never start digging through the pot to count it.

Sometimes you'll call and nothing needed to happen, and the supervisor tells you that you didn't need to call for that. That's fine. They might tell you that you did a great job. Either way, you protected the game, and it opens the conversation about how to handle it next time. You'll still learn a lot in the break room, too, from other dealers talking through what came up. Use your head in gray areas: if you think there might be a confrontation, it's good to call the floor even if it turns out you didn't need to.

Everything else gets added on the job. You'll never know everything once you're hired. Poker rooms do on-the-job training, and "I didn't know, let me get a supervisor" is a fine thing to say at a poker table, in a way it isn't in table games.

The supervisor is always right

No matter what I teach you, your supervisor is correct. There's no telling them "this is what I learned," and there's no telling them "another supervisor here says you're wrong." Something I teach you will absolutely be different at the room you go to. If they tell you to do it differently, you say, "Yes, sir." You can ask, "Can I do it this way?" You never tell them, "I was taught differently."

I wish someone had told me that when I started. In 2007, I politely told a supervisor, "Oh, no, that's not the way I'm supposed to do it." She was not happy. We don't burn bridges in this industry. You burn one bridge, you might be burning 50.

The same goes once the floor is handling a hand: don't undermine them. If money is clearly about to go to the wrong person, depending on the floor, speak up quietly.

Who's above the floor

The poker director or manager (rooms use either title) oversees the whole room. Below them are the shift managers, and there has to be one on property at all times. Below the shift managers are the supervisors, who handle everything that happens on the floor. Then the dealers. If a supervisor doesn't know what to do, they go to the shift manager, and the shift manager can go to the director.

Supervisors make mistakes too, just like dealers. There's some relatively universal training for dealers, but not for supervisors. Most get promoted from dealing and learn on the job. That's part of why some dealers don't want to call the floor: they don't know what they'll get. Call anyway.

Calling the floor in an audition

A lot of people assume that calling the floor in an audition means you failed. It doesn't. The people hiring know this can happen to anyone, including dealers who've been dealing a long time. I have floor calls sometimes too. A floor call handled well can even make you memorable, because it shows you can navigate a hard situation.

What fails people is not calling the floor: messing up side pots and trying to fix it themselves, and making it worse. If your muck slides onto a player's cards in an audition and you call the floor, you're probably fine. If you don't call the floor, you probably fail. The audition guide has more on handling mistakes there.

How to practice floor calls

Everyone practices floor calls in my class. We treat every class hand like a real game. When something goes wrong, you say "Floor, table six" out loud, I play the supervisor standing behind you, and you walk me through what happened. Then we do it again until it's clean. You can practice the same way at home:

  • When you make a mistake in a practice hand, don't fix it. Say "Floor" and a table number out loud, like it's a real game.
  • Explain the hand to an imaginary supervisor, seat by seat, street by street. Check yourself: did you say only what happened? Did you get the street right? Did you slip in "he meant" or "I think"?
  • Say the player-facing lines out loud until they're automatic: "Let's see what the floor says." "This is a rule of the house. Would you like me to call the floor?"

Common questions about calling the floor

What does "call the floor" mean in poker?

The dealer calls a floor supervisor to the table to make a ruling or fix a problem. The dealer says "Floor" and the table number, stops the hand, and tells the supervisor what happened.

Do dealers make rulings in poker?

No. Dealers explain and enforce the rules. Rulings are the floor supervisor's. When a player resists or a situation isn't clear, the dealer calls the floor.

Can a player ask for a floor?

Yes. If a player asks for a floor, the dealer calls one right away, without asking why.

Is it bad to call the floor a lot as a new dealer?

No. Supervisors would rather come over than clean up a mess, and you can be fired for not calling. New dealers should call any time they're not sure.

Does calling the floor fail a poker dealer audition?

No. Not calling the floor, and trying to fix a mistake yourself, is what fails people. A floor call handled well can help you stand out.

What should a dealer say to the floor?

Only what happened, in order, seat by seat. No opinions about what a player meant, and no suggested ruling.
