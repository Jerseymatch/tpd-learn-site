What Is the Poker TDA? Rules vs Recommended Procedures vs House Rules

Sooner or later a floor supervisor will tell a player "we follow TDA." Here's what that means, how the rules get made, why they aren't the same as your room's house rules, and how to read them like someone who's been to the Summit.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 20, 2026 · 11 min read

If you deal poker long enough, you'll hear a supervisor tell a player, "We follow TDA." The TDA is the Tournament Directors Association, and its rules are the closest thing poker has to an industry standard. When I teach a rule in class, a lot of the time I'm teaching what the TDA says, and I reference their rules over and over.

But the TDA isn't a law, it isn't universal, and it doesn't cover cash games. Knowing what it is, what it isn't and how to read it will make you a more confident dealer. I'm a member and I've been to the TDA Summit twice, so here's how it actually works.

The short answer

  • The TDA is a volunteer organization of the people who run poker rooms and tournaments. It publishes a set of tournament rules that rooms can choose to adopt.
  • It has no power over any poker room. Following it is a choice. A room that says "we follow TDA" is agreeing to its numbered rules.
  • Rules and recommended procedures are different. The numbered rules are the standard. Recommended procedures are test rules: not required, but a room that uses them has the TDA's backing.
  • The TDA is tournament-only. There's no TDA cash game rulebook, so cash games run on each room's house rules, often based on the TDA.
  • The rules change every two years, at the TDA Summit. Always check the current version.
  • Dealers explain rules. The floor makes rulings. Knowing the TDA helps you explain a rule to a player, but a decision is always the supervisor's.

What the Poker TDA is

The TDA started in the early 2000s because the rules were different from room to room. Every other year, the people who run poker get together at the TDA Summit in Las Vegas: the heads of the big tournament series and tours from around the world, plus a whole bunch of poker room directors and tournament directors. They go over the important rules, one at a time, and publish a rule set any room can adopt.

As far as I understand it, nobody makes money off of it. People do this for the good of the industry. That's why the website looks the way it does. I call the TDA the most intelligent, organized group in poker.

The Summit is a really unique thing. You're in a room with the heads of poker rooms from all over the country and the world, and you hear the history behind the rules instead of just reading the words. Anybody can go, but it's meant for people who run poker rooms or tournaments, or sometimes supervisors. If you're a dealer, it doesn't matter as much yet. If you want a lifelong career in poker, it's worth it eventually.

TDA rules vs recommended procedures

The TDA document has two parts, and the difference matters.

  • The rules. About 70 numbered rules, many with sub-rules. If a room says it follows TDA, these are the rules it's agreeing to follow.
  • The recommended procedures. These are the TDA saying, "we think this is a very good idea." Instead of forcing the change on the whole industry, they recommend it. Rooms can choose to use it, and if they do, they have the TDA's backing. Two years later, at the next Summit, they ask all the rooms that used it how it went. If everybody loved it, it becomes a real rule. If not, they might get rid of it.

That testing system is one of the most powerful things I've seen in the casino industry. It came out of a rule change years ago that got a lot of pushback. Now they recommend a change and test it first. That process is a big reason I tend to side with the TDA.

A real example: the way premature cards are handled. Every premature card, including the turn, now gets reshuffled into the stub, and that all lives in the recommended procedures. I was at the Summit where it was voted on. It failed on the first day and passed on the second, which I had never seen happen. See premature cards for the whole procedure.

TDA vs house rules vs WSOP rules

The TDA doesn't have any power over any individual poker room. When the TDA makes a change, only the rooms that follow the TDA make that change. Not everybody in the poker world cares, and changes don't filter in everywhere. So wherever you work, you have to learn what we do here.

Cash games run on house rules

The TDA does tournament rules. It doesn't do cash rules, so there are no true universal cash game rules. Each room has its own, and you get all kinds of variations from room to room. The TDA helps, but it doesn't cover everything. Rooms base a lot of their cash rules on it anyway, and because the TDA rules are public, any room can point to them to show a player it isn't just making something up.

That's part of what I'm trying to change with my training: teach the common methods, pick the best one, and get dealers on the same page, wherever they end up working.

The TDA and the WSOP

For years, the World Series of Poker had more influence on the rules rooms used. Now the TDA does, through the Summits. The two don't always agree, and the WSOP doesn't follow the TDA perfectly. One room I have dealt in has gone back and forth between TDA and WSOP rules.

The big blind ante is a good example. When a short stack can't cover both the big blind and the ante, which one gets paid first? The TDA used to recommend the ante first. The WSOP refused, so that a player can always go all in and get called by everyone. In the rules published in 2023, the TDA dropped its ante-first recommendation and left it up to each room. My vote is the big blind first, because it protects recreational players. That's my opinion, not a rule. See big blind ante rules.

And remember that the WSOP is known for tournaments, not cash. Using its rules to run a cash game is a little like asking a pizza place how to run a deli.

TDA rules every dealer should know

Rule numbers move around between versions, so learn what the rule says, not just its number. These come up all the time:

  • Rule 1: the best interest of the game and fairness. These are the top priorities in decision-making, and unusual circumstances can call for a common-sense decision over a technical rule. The floor's decision is final. Read it carefully: it's from the floor's perspective, not the dealer's. It isn't something a dealer uses to decide a hand. It's why the supervisor gets the last word.
  • Rule 2: player responsibilities. Protect your hand with a chip, a card cover or your hand on top of it, act in turn, keep your cards visible, table all your cards at showdown, and speak up if you see a mistake.
  • Substantial action. Any two actions in turn where at least one puts chips in, or any three actions in turn. Posted blinds don't count. A lot of floor decisions, like whether a card is premature, depend on it.
  • Methods of raising. A raise is either the full amount in one motion or a verbal declaration. The TDA dropped the old option where a player says "raise," puts out the call amount and then goes back for more. In TDA tournaments, that's now only a min raise. Plenty of cash rooms still allow it, so know your room. See the technical rules of betting.
  • Counting bets. The TDA's way, which most rooms now follow: announce clear bets and raises, and don't count an all-in unless the player facing the action asks. See counting bets and all-in rules.
  • Tabling your hand. It used to be that cards had to be flat on the felt, face up, to count as tabled. The TDA now allows a hand held up in clear view of the dealer and the table to count. Some rooms still want it flat on the table, so know your room. See the showdown.
  • All-in buttons. The recommended procedure: the dealer keeps the buttons, not the players, and places one in full view in front of an all-in player. It's a recommendation rather than a rule so that an all-in still counts if the dealer doesn't use a button.

Dealers explain rules, the floor makes rulings

Knowing the TDA lets you explain a rule clearly when a player asks. But when a decision has to be made, you call the floor, even if you think it's obvious, and the supervisor explains it. See calling the floor.

How to find and read the TDA rules

  1. Go to pokertda.com and find the TDA Poker Rules section. The documents themselves are kept in a Dropbox.
  2. Open the current version. The long form includes examples, and they're really clear.
  3. Look at the redline. The redline version shows exactly what changed from the last version. It's the fastest way to see what's new.
  4. Read for familiarity, not memorization. I give every class the TDA link and ask them to glance through it. I don't need you to memorize it. I want you to know how poker rules are written, so when I bring up a rule in a lesson, you have context for it.

The hardest thing about the TDA rules, for supervisors, dealers and anybody, is interpreting them with the correct history. You can read a rule and think you know what it means without understanding the language, and sometimes the rules don't make sense until somebody explains where they came from. That's the part I try to teach in class.

What I teach in class

Mention the TDA in your audition

If a player says "raise," puts out the call and then goes back for more during an audition, you could say, "I don't know the rules in this house, but TDA says this is a min raise now." If you follow the old rule instead, you're not going to fail the audition. But you're really going to impress them by showing you know what the TDA says nowadays.

When you're new in a room

A lot of players are still used to the old raise rule, and plenty of cash rooms still allow it. If you're new in a room, you really want to know what your room's rule is before it comes up. In a tournament, I might call the floor.

The courses and the rules change

Some of my video lessons were recorded years ago, before the TDA changed things like tabling a hand and the raise rule. I point those out in class, because staying current with the rules is part of the job.

The TDA exam and the Summit

The TDA has an exam you can take online for a small fee. Students who have taken it tell me it has a lot of stud questions, which makes it hard if you've never played stud. I never took it, because I was already supervising before it mattered, but I would take it now. If you're in my class and you're passing the TDA exam, you're way ahead of the game.

If you want to train yourself toward the floor, here's a habit a dealer on one of my community calls described: when you hear the floor called to a nearby table, listen, then look the situation up in the TDA rules on your break. Your relationship with the rules gets better and better that way. See dealer or floor.

Common questions about the Poker TDA

What does TDA stand for in poker?

Tournament Directors Association. It's a volunteer organization of poker room and tournament directors that publishes a standard set of tournament rules.

Are the TDA rules mandatory?

No. The TDA has no power over any poker room. Rooms choose to follow it. A room that says it follows TDA is agreeing to the numbered rules, and the recommended procedures are optional even then.

Do the TDA rules apply to cash games?

No. The TDA writes tournament rules. Cash games run on each room's house rules, which are often based on the TDA but vary from room to room.

What is a TDA recommended procedure?

A test rule. Rooms can use it with the TDA's backing, and two years later the TDA asks how it went before deciding whether to make it a real rule or drop it.

How often do the TDA rules change?

Every two years. The rules are reviewed at the TDA Summit, held every other year in Las Vegas, and the new version comes out after that, along with a redline showing what changed.

What is TDA Rule 1?

The best interest of the game and fairness are the top priorities in decision-making, and common sense can override a technical rule in unusual circumstances. The floor's decision is final.

Does the WSOP follow TDA rules?

Not exactly. They may follow them closely, but not perfectly. They used to disagree on the big blind ante, for example. One room I have dealt in has gone back and forth between TDA and WSOP rules.

Should a poker dealer learn the TDA rules?

Yes. You don't need to memorize them, but reading them makes you clearer and more confident, especially for tournament dealing, and it's how you start learning to think like a floor.
