How Do Bad Beat Jackpots and High-Hand Promotions Work?

A bad beat jackpot pays when a monster hand loses. The money comes from a separate drop that's meant to go back to the players. Here's how it works, why the burn cards matter, and how high-hand promos changed poker rooms.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 19, 2026 · 11 min read

It feels really bad to have an awesome hand, like four of a kind, and lose. So at some point, poker rooms decided that for that rare circumstance there should be a jackpot that builds up. Instead of just losing a big pot, the player now has a shot at something like $100,000. That's a bad beat jackpot.

The same money can also pay for smaller, more frequent promotions too, like high hands. This page covers where the money comes from, what it takes to qualify, and the part most players never think about: how the dealer protects the jackpot.

Where the money comes from: the jackpot drop

Look at a $15 pot. The dealer takes the rake from it, which is the money the casino makes. It pays the employees and the expenses, and it's what makes it worthwhile for the room to be open. Many casinos also take a promotion drop, or jackpot drop, from the same pot. That money is separate from the rake.

In the United States, states generally allow casinos to take this promotion fund so they can give it away as jackpots. That can mean a full bad beat jackpot that builds up to a huge number and pays out under a rare circumstance. Or it can mean promotions that happen frequently, like high hands, or splash the pot.

  • It's meant to go 100% back to the players. As a player, you're not winning or losing with the jackpot drop in the long run. It's the fun part. Some states allow something like a handling fee, so it won't quite be 100% there. Having worked in casinos, I think that's fine. It takes a lot of work to track and manage all of it, with teams counting down the box at every table every day.
  • Everyone at the table needs an equal shot. That's one of the requirements. A room couldn't say the only way to win a high hand is to sit in seat four. That would be unfair to everybody contributing.
  • There are regulations in each location about what is and isn't okay to do with this money.

Some rooms take nothing for jackpots at all. And in Florida, some rooms pay big promotions partly out of the rake, not just the jackpot drop, to stay competitive.

How much gets dropped

A common jackpot drop is $1 at a $10 pot and another $1 at $20, for a $2 max. Some places take $3. I've seen more than that, and I've seen $1 and nothing. Some rooms take $2 automatically at $10. Whatever your room says, you do. Rake, promo drop and tips shows how to take both, street by street.

Almost every room follows "no flop, no drop" for the rake. But many rooms take at least $1 for the jackpot even when there's no flop. In rooms with that policy, I'd estimate dealers take it only about 70% of the time. That makes the dealers who do it right look like the bad guys. Surveillance watches the rake closely, and the jackpot drop less so. Take it anyway. Before you deal a hand, you need to know the rake and jackpot structure.

Why Omaha and stud are often left out

Texas Hold'em will often have a jackpot drop. Omaha and stud often won't, because rooms often don't include those games in the promotion. The odds of making the big hands are different. In Omaha you get four cards in your hand, which makes four of a kind, a full house or a straight flush more likely. It would be unfair to put a limit Omaha game in the same high-hand pool as Hold'em. So when you're dealing Omaha, you often only take a rake.

What qualifies as a bad beat

Each room sets its own qualifying hand. At the first poker room I worked in, back in 2007, a bad beat was aces full of tens or better getting beaten. Another multi-property bad beat I know of needed four tens or better beaten by a straight flush or better.

Rooms also have rules about how the hand is made. Bad beat jackpots often need both hole cards to play. So a straight flush made with only one card from a player's hand beating four of a kind can be a bad beat at the table without qualifying for the jackpot. Read the posted rules where you deal or play. They're not the same everywhere.

How the jackpot gets split

A bad beat jackpot doesn't all go to one person. A typical split is about 50% to the losing hand, 25% to the winning hand, and 25% shared by the rest of the players at the table. Rooms set their own percentages.

What the dealer does when a bad beat is possible

When a bad beat jackpot gets hit, the room has to verify it. Back around 2008, the procedure at my first room was to go to surveillance and verify everything before paying: the burn cards, the muck, all of it. The number of burn cards matters, because if there's the wrong number of burns, the sequence of cards that came out on the board would have been different. That would have changed the result of the bad beat. If something couldn't be verified, the house wouldn't pay. At the time, there was a feeling in casinos that they didn't like paying out jackpots. I'm not saying rooms would do that now, and a dealer pulling the burn cards into the muck probably isn't going to be the difference. But it is still possible that a place won't pay a bad beat if the burn cards aren't right.

The shortcut that kills a jackpot

At that first room, there was an experienced dealer who, after the river came out, would take the burn cards and get everything ready so he could start the next hand the second the pot was pushed. He only did it when no bad beat was possible on that board. He'd check the board first.

A new dealer watched him and copied it, every hand. He didn't understand what the experienced dealer was actually thinking about. On a board with two jacks and an ace, aces full of jacks is a potential bad beat, and pulling those burn cards could kill it and take the jackpot money away from everyone at the table. I was a new supervisor at the time, and I had to pull him aside and explain it before it actually cost anyone.

That's my point about shortcuts. Every shortcut that has ever been created exists because there's risk built into it. If you're experienced and you understand the risk, you can stay away from it and save time. If you're new and you learn the shortcut as part of what to do, you're almost definitely putting yourself at risk.

  • Read the board. Anyone who understands what they're doing can tell when a bad beat is possible and when it isn't.
  • When it's possible, don't clear anything early. Leave the burn cards, the board and the muck where they are.
  • Keep the burn cards where they can be seen. In my class, we stair-step the burn cards, so you can really see them. A lot of dealers bury them under the pot, and then the only way to verify them is to watch the dealer, which is hard, because someone could manipulate that.
  • Follow your room. Pulling the burn cards right as you're about to push the pot is a normal procedure, and it makes pushing the pot easier. Some dealers pull them earlier, when the river comes out, to get ready. That's fine in most rooms, but every once in a while a room won't want you to do that. If your supervisor tells you how they want it done, you do it their way.

Dealing a game with a big jackpot

When you're dealing a game with a big bad beat jackpot, bring the energy. Hyping the jackpot gets players locked in and keeps the game moving. Just be careful with shortcuts like mucking the burn cards, if your room verifies them for a bad beat.

And whenever a big hand loses, keep your announcement level. Instead of "A set of kings loses to deuce-five for a straight," just say, "I see a set of kings. I see deuce-five for the straight." With real money on the line, take the emotion out of it. The showdown covers reading and announcing hands.

High-hand promotions

When I started working the floor full time in 2009, promotions were basically bad beat jackpots. Then big high-hand promotions came along, often paid every hour, and they changed who shows up and how the game is played. Some players started treating poker like a table game, chasing the promotions. In Florida, big high hands trained players to buy in short and limp along hoping to make a high hand, instead of playing each other.

Stalling and collusion

High hands are paid by period. That creates a problem: players stalling on each street so their high hand lands in the next period. In one room, that was the most common reason a high hand got disqualified. The other usual reason was apparent collusion. As a supervisor, I disqualified at least 10 high hands, using surveillance to review them. Disqualifying a high hand is the supervisor's decision. As a dealer, your job is to tell players they can't stall, even if it costs you the tip.

Adding promotion winnings to your stack

In a cash game, a player can't add chips to their stack during a hand. Occasionally you'll see a poker room with a promotion where, if someone wins a high hand for a couple hundred dollars, they let the player add it to their stack. I feel like that kind of goes against the integrity of poker. If you ever see it as a player or a dealer, it's worth asking, "Hey, is this okay?"

My take from running a poker room

From the management side, bad beat jackpots don't move the needle much. Promotion money is best spent on things that bring in new business. I'd also rather see promotion money go to the weaker players, because the pros end up with it anyway. That's a room's decision, not a dealer's, but it helps to know why your room runs the promotions it does. Opening a poker room goes deeper into how rooms are built.

Common questions about bad beat jackpots

How does a bad beat jackpot work?

The room takes a small jackpot drop from pots, separate from the rake, and builds up a jackpot. When a big qualifying hand loses, like aces full of tens or better beaten in the first room I worked, the jackpot is paid out and split between the losing hand, the winning hand and the rest of the table.

Who gets the bad beat jackpot?

It's split. A typical split is about 50% to the player who lost, 25% to the player who won, and 25% shared by everyone else at the table. Your room sets the exact percentages.

Do both hole cards have to play for a bad beat jackpot?

Often, yes. A hand that only uses one of your cards may not qualify, even if it's a huge beat. Check the posted rules.

Where does jackpot money come from?

From the jackpot or promotion drop, a dollar or a few taken from pots and kept separate from the rake. It's meant to go 100% back to players, minus a small handling fee in some states.

Why does the dealer take a dollar when there's no flop?

Many rooms take at least $1 for the jackpot even when there's no flop, while still taking no rake. If your room does that, take it every time.

Can a bad beat jackpot be denied?

It can happen. Years ago, my first poker room verified everything through surveillance before paying, including the burn cards, and wouldn't pay if something couldn't be verified. I'm not saying rooms do that now, but it's still possible a room won't pay if the burn cards aren't right. That's why dealers don't clear anything early when a bad beat is possible.

Why aren't Omaha games in the jackpot?

With four cards in your hand, big hands like four of a kind and straight flushes are more likely, so rooms often leave Omaha and stud out of Hold'em promotions.
