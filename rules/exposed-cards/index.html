What Happens When a Card Is Exposed?

Who exposed it, and when, decides everything. A card the dealer flashes on the deal gets replaced. A card the player flips themselves doesn't. Here's how I teach every version of it.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 16, 2026 · 13 min read

An exposed card is any card that flashes or lands face up when it isn't supposed to. Cards flip for every new dealer. It starts getting better within a couple of months of practice. For me, it was a year or two before it really stopped, and it gets less over time. So you don't need to be scared of it. You need to know exactly what to do, because the answer depends on two things: who exposed the card, and when.

Here's the short version, then each situation in detail.

When the dealer exposes a card on the deal

Any card that flashes, or lands face up, during the pitch is an exposed card. That includes a card that hits a chip stack, or hits a player's hand and flips before they've had a chance to secure it. If the player knocks it out of the air before it gets all the way to them, that's still on the deal, and it gets replaced.

  1. Leave it face up in front of that player and keep pitching. Don't stop to fix it.
  2. At the end of the deal, replace it with the burn card. The player gets the next card, the one that would have been your burn.
  3. The exposed card goes face up on top of the deck, and you announce it. "Queen of spades is the burn." That's the card you burn, face down, when you bring the flop.

The TDA rule says it the same way: a player whose card flashes may receive the burn card in its place.

Whenever a card is face up at the table, you always say what it is. That's how everyone at the table gets the same information, which is the whole idea of one player per hand.

The blinds, and the first card off the deck

If the first card off the deck is exposed, or the first card to either blind in a hold'em game, it's a misdeal. "Misdeal, everybody." Bring the cards back, drop the deck, shuffle up and deal again. That goes for any blind, even if the card bounced off the player's hand.

It's the first card each blind gets. If a blind's second card is exposed, that's just an exposed card, handled like any other. One supervisor remembering this wrong can confuse a whole room, so get it straight.

Two exposed cards is a misdeal

One exposed card gets replaced. If a second card is exposed during the same deal, it's a misdeal. A card off the table counts toward that too: if one card already flipped and the next goes off the table, that's your second exposed card.

A flash is enough

A card doesn't have to land face up to be exposed. If it's possible someone saw it on the way out of your hand, or the way it landed, we count it as an exposed card even if it's still face down. You don't go around asking, "Did somebody see this?" Poker players don't want to tell you if they know what somebody's card is.

When you clearly flashed one, just say, "That card flashed. I need it flipped up." You don't have to name it or prove it. If players push back, don't go back and forth with them. Call the floor and let the supervisor handle it. Some players will say they didn't see it whether they did or not. Protecting the game's integrity matters more than being right in the moment.

Some dealers, when a card flashes, just keep going because they don't want to deal with it. If a card flashed enough that anybody could have seen part of it, and you ignore it, you're the one breaking one player per hand.

A card off the table

If a card lands on the rail, that's strangely okay. If it goes below the rail and off the table, it's a supervisor call, and it will be treated as an exposed card. Make sure the player doesn't pick it up. That's for a supervisor or another poker room staff member to do.

Don't make a big deal of it

When you drop your head and make a whole thing of a flipped card, you bring a lot of attention to it. Treat it like a regular part of the game. Put it on top of the deck, say "Jack of spades is the burn" like it's a thing that happens, and keep going. If the table treats it as no big deal, that helps you and everyone else.

And if a player is upset that you flipped their ace? Over-apologizing feeds it. Shrug it off kindly: "It could have been the six of diamonds that flipped up, and the jack of spades could have been under. It was never meant to be yours." You never know if those aces would have held.

What I correct in class

Pretending it didn't happen

In class, we treat every hand like a real hand. If you flip a card, it's an exposed card, and you handle it. If it's a misdeal, you misdeal it. Nobody fake-continues through a real procedure issue, because that's the habit you'd take into a casino.

Calling a flash a misdeal (or the reverse)

A student flashes a card and assumes the hand is over. Unless it's the first card off the deck, the first card to a blind, or the second exposed card, it's not a misdeal. It's an exposed card, and the burn replaces it. The exception to remember: a flashed first card to a blind is a misdeal, even if it lands face down.

Pitching too far

When cards keep flipping in class, the fix is usually a shorter, softer pitch. Get your arms out, pitch softer, and turn your deck-hand wrist up toward the ceiling, so the card comes off flat. How to pitch cards covers the technique.

When the player flips their own card

This is the one new dealers mix up. If the card was pitched correctly, landed in front of the player and it's theirs, and then they flip it over while picking it up, it is not replaced. You announce it to the table, "Two of diamonds exposed," and ask them to turn it back over. No floor call needed.

We can't let a player look at their hand, flip over a card they don't like, and get a new one. That's not how this works. And one day, in a real poker room, a player will try to make you think you flipped it, because they don't want their deuce. If you're new and you fall for it, you fall for it. But know that people try it. If you pitched it and it landed there, and they flip it over, or pretend to flip it over, after that point, they keep it.

When a player shows their cards during the hand

A player exposing their own hand is a one player per hand issue, and what you do depends on who could see it and how many players are in.

  • They show someone who's still in the hand. Everyone needs to see those cards now. Tell the table, "He's got cards, he's in the hand, so I have to show everyone." Then read each card out loud, rank and suit: "Jack of diamonds, eight of diamonds, six of hearts, four of spades exposed." Usually it's a clear accident, so keep it friendly: "Hey, maybe don't do that again." If they give you any resistance, then call the floor. (Calling the floor right away isn't wrong either.)
  • They show someone who isn't in the hand. In most cash games you don't expose them automatically. If anyone asks to see them, you put them aside and show them after the hand. Cards speak covers that, and how to say it.
  • Heads-up in a cash game, a player turns a card over to get a read. Typically, we're fine with it.
  • Three or more players in the hand. Tell them, "You've got to turn that over." If they resist, or do it again after you've already told them, call the floor.
  • There's an all-in player and two players on the side. They can't flip their cards up, even if it's just the two of them betting. Whether one of them calls or folds can change who wins the main pot, so showing cards affects the all-in player's result. Explain it simply, and get a floor if they resist.

A player who tables their hand by mistake

Sometimes a player turns their hand face up because they think everyone folded, and someone's still in. If the cards are clearly identifiable and it obviously wasn't a fold, in most professional rooms the hand stays live, even if it's partly on the muck. Face-down cards in the muck would be dead. Players often think the first way they learned it is the only rule. If someone insists it's wrong, offer to get the floor. Offering it builds confidence.

The dealer never flips a player's cards

You don't turn a player's down cards face up. That's the player's job. You never flip over cards that have been folded or mucked either, not even as a joke. And if a player tables their hand when you didn't ask them to, you don't turn it back over. That's covered in the showdown.

What I correct in class

Replacing a card the player flipped

When a player flips their own card while gathering it up, students often say it's a misdeal, or that the burn replaces it. Ask yourself who exposed it. If it reached the player and they flipped it, they keep it.

Letting the all-in flip go

A player facing a bet with a third player all in turns their cards up to get a read, and students aren't sure whether to allow it. We do not allow this. Set the hands up and play it out both ways, and you'll see how it changes the main pot.

Exposed cards in tournaments

A cash game is a series of separate hands, and a player can leave whenever they want. A tournament is one ongoing event, and the rules are strictly applied. If a player exposes their hand at all during a tournament, it can result in a penalty in plenty of rooms, so you don't just say, "You're not allowed to do that." You call the floor: "Floor, table 72. He was exposing his cards during the hand." That goes for a player looking at their cards so others can see, mucking face up, or talking about the board.

Flipping a card over to get a read isn't allowed in a tournament, even heads-up in the hand. The only exception is when there are only two players left in the entire tournament.

It's possible you call the supervisor and nothing happens, and they ask why you called. That's fine. Especially as a new dealer, go the extra mile so it's handled consistently. Players get annoyed when dealers let exposed hands slide in tournaments.

Premature cards: a board card that comes out too early

A premature card is a flop, turn or river that the dealer brings before the betting round is complete (premature cards has the full procedure). Keeping it would be an insanely unfair advantage: a player would get to decide whether to put money in knowing what the next card is.

  • It's always a floor call. Even if you think it's obvious. If you ever skip it intentionally at a job, that's a serious violation.
  • Tell the supervisor exactly what happened, without the drama. "These two players were in for 1,000. This player only put in 225. I brought the bets in, and I brought the turn." You don't need to say, "I made a huge mistake." Players who play a lot see this sometimes. If you call it huge, nobody can defend you.
  • The supervisor fixes it. First, they work out whether there was substantial action. Then the missed action gets completed, and the premature card gets shuffled back into the deck. The TDA now handles every premature card, turn included, with a reshuffle.
  • Don't rush to fix it. A dealer who just made a mistake wants to fix it immediately. Wait. The reshuffle happens when the next card is actually needed, and it's done face down. When you mix and square the deck mid-hand, keep it low and don't expose any card.

The best fix is never bringing one: tap the table before every flop, turn and river, and keep track of how many players still have to act. How to bring the flop, turn and river has the routine, including what to do when the burn card flips.

Exposing cards while you handle them

Some exposed cards happen after the deal, from the way the dealer handles cards. These are all habits I correct:

  • Folded hands on the way to the muck. Keep them parallel to the table and close to the felt. Tilt them toward the players and you show everyone what the player folded.
  • The deck angle. Keep the deck basically parallel while you pitch. Tilt it toward the players and every card can flash.
  • Rolling the deck. When you square or hold the stub, never turn it more than about 45 degrees off flat. Past that, the bottom card shows, and someone who wanted to could see into the deck. Some rooms, the World Series among them, treat rolling the deck as an automatic no.
  • The burn card. Grab it from the corner so the side of the table next to you can't see it.

Procedure exists so that across every hand, dealt by every dealer, including the sloppy ones, a card is never exposed.

Common questions about exposed cards

Is an exposed card a misdeal in poker?

Usually not. One exposed card on the deal is replaced by the burn card. It's a misdeal if it's the first card off the deck, the first card to either blind, or the second exposed card.

What happens to an exposed card in Texas hold'em?

It stays face up until the deal is done. The player gets the next card, and the exposed card goes face up on top of the deck, gets announced, and becomes the burn card for the flop.

What if a player flips their own card?

If the card reached them and they flipped it, it isn't replaced. The dealer announces it and has the player turn it back over.

What if the dealer flashes a card but it lands face down?

If anyone could have seen it, it's treated as an exposed card. The dealer doesn't ask whether anyone saw it.

Can a player show their cards during a hand?

Heads-up in a cash game, rooms are typically fine with it. With three or more players, or with a player all in, they can't. In a tournament, any exposed hand is a floor call and can bring a penalty.

What happens if the dealer turns a card too early?

That's a premature card, and it's always a floor call. The supervisor has the betting finished, and the card gets shuffled back into the deck.
