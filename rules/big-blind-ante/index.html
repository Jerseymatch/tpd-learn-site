Big Blind Ante Rules: Who Pays First When a Stack Is Short?

The big blind ante made tournaments faster and simpler. It also created one question rooms still don't agree on: when the big blind is short, does the ante or the blind get paid first? Here's how each way works, how the dealer handles the ante, and where I stand.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 21, 2026 · 10 min read

Most poker tournaments now use a big blind ante. Instead of every player putting in an ante every hand, the player in the big blind pays one ante for the whole table. It's simpler, and you get way more hands out.

But it created a question the industry still doesn't agree on. When the big blind doesn't have enough chips to cover both the big blind and the ante, which one gets paid first? The answer decides whether that player can win chips from everyone who calls, or only win back their own chips. I was at the TDA Summit where the TDA changed its position on this, and I have a strong opinion about it. First, how it works.

What a big blind ante is, and why it exists

Antes exist to drive action. Say the blinds are 100/200 and every player antes 25, nine-handed. Before anyone acts, the pot goes from 300 to over 500. There's more to win, so people play more hands.

The problem was collecting them. The dealer can't deal until every ante is in, so going around the table every hand slowed the game down. And in the old tournaments where you had to take an ante from everybody, players took shots all the time. They'd just not pay their ante and make the dealer ask them, every single hand. It was a nightmare, and I'm glad that's gone.

So the industry looked at the math. With 100/200 and a 25 ante from nine players, the antes come to about 225. That's pretty close to 200. So instead, the big blind pays a 200 ante for the whole table. It jams up the big blind a little, but it keeps everything so much simpler.

The big blind ante started showing up around 2016, in high-stakes events, to save time. The TDA recommended it within a few years, and now most tournaments use it.

How the dealer collects the big blind ante

This is the part you'll do every single hand, and it's something an auditioner will notice.

  1. Shuffle up to the cut, then stop. After your last riffle, put the cut card in front, but don't cut yet. In a tournament, the cut is when you start dealing.
  2. Get the ante into the pot. The ante goes where the pot goes, close to you, in a spot where you still have room for the muck. Have the player put it out. You don't take it off their stack. If they haven't, ask for it: "I need the ante."
  3. It has to be exact. At 200/400 with a 400 big blind ante, the player puts out 400 for the blind and another 400 for the ante. If they push out a 500 chip next to a 400, that's fine: take the 400 and leave the 500. If they only have 500s, push one around the table before you cut: "Does anyone have change for this?" There are no extra chips in a tournament to make change with, so the players help. Once they realize slow change costs them hands, they start making change for each other quicker. Making change in a tournament has the details.
  4. Then cut and deal. Once the ante's in the pot, you cut and go. Every stack gets dealt in, whether or not the player is in the seat.

It's fine for a player to have the wrong denomination out for the big blind. The ante in the middle is different. It has to be correct, because that money has already been brought in.

The blinds need to be out before you start pitching, too. Some people teach that you have to force both blinds out before you even cut. I don't personally teach that, so do whatever your room wants. But if you really want to impress an auditioner, get the big blind ante in, in exact change, before you cut. When you're brand new to tournaments, it's okay to wait for the blinds to be out before you deal. There's something grounding about it. Not every room enforces "ante before the cut" all the time, and it isn't a hard, fast rule everywhere, but it's my rule of thumb. What I correct in class

Waiting instead of getting the ante

I've seen dealers who don't even pay attention to it. They ask for the blinds, somebody's talking, and they just sit there until the blinds go out. We don't want to be that dealer. Ask for the blinds, but the ante is the most important. Get it in before you cut.

Putting the ante too far away

I've had students set the ante way out in the middle of the table. Bring it in, about six inches toward your chest, to the spot where the pot will be built, with room left for the muck.

When the big blind can't cover both

Here's the problem. The blinds are 1,000/2,000 with a 2,000 big blind ante, and the player in the big blind has exactly 2,000 left. Is that 2,000 the big blind or the ante?

  • If it's the ante, the player is all in for the ante. The ante is dead money that started the pot, so the player can only win the ante back. Nobody else's calls go into a pot they can win.
  • If it's the big blind, there's simply no ante that hand. The player's 2,000 plays as a live big blind, and they can win 2,000 from everyone who calls.

When they have some of both

It also matters when the player has more than one but less than both. Say it's 5,000/10,000 with a 10,000 big blind ante, and the big blind has 15,000:

RuleThe anteThe big blind
Ante first10,0005,000
Big blind first (the WSOP way)5,00010,000

With ante first, that player is all in for a 5,000 blind, so the most they can win from each caller is 5,000. With big blind first, they've posted a full big blind and can win 10,000 from each caller.

The chop that busts a player

Ante first creates some situations that make no sense. Say the big blind only has half a big blind left. It folds to the small blind, who completes, and the hand chops. With the ante taken first, the way the odd chip works out can eliminate the big blind on a hand they tied. It's one of the stupid situations ante first creates. It's rare, and it won't come up in an audition, but it's a good example of why rooms argue about this.

The TDA, the WSOP, and your room

The argument for the ante first is in the word itself. "Ante" means before, so it gets paid before anything else. For years the TDA recommended exactly that. The World Series of Poker refused, so that a player can always go all in and get called by everyone.

At the 2022 Summit, the TDA dropped its ante-first recommendation entirely. It left the decision to each room, because the committee was split. What it kept is a short line saying not to reduce the ante, even at the final table.

So today there's no single answer. In my experience, most places have taken the ante first, but it isn't universal, and some rooms go back and forth between TDA and WSOP rules. I can see why a room would hesitate to switch, too. After years of explaining "ante first" to angry players, changing your answer isn't easy. The TDA's own rules and how they differ from house rules are in what is the poker TDA.

My vote: big blind first

This is my opinion, not a rule. I think the big blind should count first. Not because it's more logical, but because the players' experience is better.

Experienced players know the rule either way. Recreational players get blindsided. A player goes all in for their last big blind, gets called by four people, wins the hand, and finds out they only won their own ante back. Now they're upset, and the dealer is the one sitting there. Big blind first needs no explanation. Pros can take care of themselves. Ante first is a booby trap for recreational players, and dodging eight callers for the same reward as one doesn't feel right to me.

If I could pick my own fix, I'd rather have the small blind post the ante.

As the dealer, though, your opinion doesn't matter at the table. You follow your room's rule exactly, and if a player argues with it, you can explain the rule. If anything needs a decision, call the floor. When to call the floor covers how.

How to practice it

  • Make "ante before the cut" automatic. Every time you practice a tournament hand, stop after your last riffle, get the ante in the pot, then cut.
  • Practice making change for the ante. Give the big blind only large chips and practice getting exact change from another player before you cut.
  • Work the short-stack math both ways. Pick a level, give the big blind less than a blind plus an ante, and figure out what goes where under ante first and under big blind first. Then figure out what the big blind can win from each caller.

Common questions about the big blind ante

What is a big blind ante?

Instead of every player paying an ante, the player in the big blind pays one ante for the whole table, usually the same amount as the big blind. It makes tournaments faster and simpler.

Who pays the big blind ante?

The player in the big blind, every hand, in addition to the big blind. Nobody else at the table antes.

Does the ante or the big blind get paid first?

It depends on the room. Many rooms take the ante first. The WSOP takes the big blind first. Since 2022 the TDA leaves it to each room. Ask your room which it uses.

What happens if the big blind is all in for less than the ante?

In an ante-first room, the chips go to the ante, and the player can only win back what they put in. In a big-blind-first room, the chips play as a short big blind, and the player can win that amount from each caller.

Does the big blind ante get smaller when the table is short-handed?

Not under the TDA's recommendation. It says not to reduce the ante, even at the final table.

Does the ante count toward the pot in pot-limit?

Yes. It's the same formula: three times the bet, plus the trail, plus the pot, and the ante is part of the pot. Dealing Omaha covers pot-limit math.
