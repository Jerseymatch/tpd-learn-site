Poker All-In Rules: What Counts as All In?

Once a player says "all in," they're all in. Most of the trouble comes from everything else: a stack pushed forward silently, one oversized chip, a mumbled word in a loud room. Here's what counts, and how I teach dealers to keep every all-in clear.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 18, 2026 · 12 min read

When a player says "all in," they're committing everything in front of them. When a player pushes chips forward without saying anything, or tosses one chip, or mumbles something in a loud room, somebody has to work out what just happened, and the dealer is the one who has to keep it from getting that far.

This page covers what counts as all in, how to make every all-in clear to the table, and how to use the all-in button. The betting math around all-ins has its own guides, which I link as we go.

What counts as all in

Saying "all in" is binding

Once a player says "all in," they're all in. They're committing to the maximum action. They don't have the option of folding anymore, and if they wanted to fold, they'd still be committed to the full amount of the bet. If a player says "I'm all in" in a no-limit game and you let them fold, that's a failed audition. Verbal declarations covers why words bind the same way chips do.

Pushing a stack forward without saying anything

Say a player goes all in for 90, and the next player silently pushes all 120 of their chips forward. That's all in. Multiple chips, plus every player's right to go all in at any time, win out over the half-raise rule here. They might say they only meant to call, but nobody can prove intent. If they meant to call, they needed to say "call."

The exception is when every chip they pushed is needed just to call. Then it's a call, because no chip in there is extra. That's the TDA's multiple-chip rule, and it's not used in every single poker room. If someone's all-in is barely more than the amount to call, some rooms treat it as a call too. The technical rules of betting walk through the oversized-chip and multiple-chip rules in detail.

One oversized chip

Facing a bet, a single oversized chip is a call unless the player says "raise" (or "all in") before it hits the felt. That's true even if it's their last chip. If a player has one 5,000 chip left and tosses it in without saying anything, they're calling, even if they meant to go all in. To go all in with it, they have to say so.

Saying "all in" when they can't raise

Sometimes a player says "all in" when the betting isn't open to them. They already acted, and the all-in in front of them wasn't a full raise, so they can only call. Don't give them options, and don't say "you can only call or fold." They already committed to the maximum action. Just say, "That's a call for 170. Give me the 170," and keep it moving. That also lets the next player know it's a call, not a raise. Whether an all-in reopens the betting is in all-in for less and reopening the action.

Betting an amount they don't have

A player says "130," and they only have 86. Use the words "all in": "All in for 86." Normally, you don't break an all-in down until somebody asks. This is the exception. They announced a specific amount and they don't have it, so the table needs the real number.

"I put you all in"

If a player says "I put you all in," your job is easy: call the floor. You can't put somebody else all in. You can't force another player to bet their chips. It's confusing language, so let the supervisor handle it.

Get chips in the pot

A verbal "all in" counts. But if no money goes into the pot, it gets hard to hold someone to it. Players mumble. You, the dealer, are interpreting words, and "all in" and something that only sounded like it are very different. So when a player says all in and doesn't push anything, announce the all-in, then be preventative: "I need you to push your chips out." At the least, have them put one chip into the pot. The room I deal in sort of has this as a rule: at least one chip goes in, in addition to the all-in button, so the player can't dispute their action later.

Never reach into a player's area to pull their chips out yourself, even though it's convenient. Tell them to push them out.

Here's why it matters. I was dealing a bomb pot in a loud room, and a player threw one red chip and said all in. I didn't hear the "all in," so I announced $5, and the next player called $5. At the showdown, the floor ruled that the caller was all in. I didn't love that ruling, but I understand it: it puts responsibility on the players to confirm the amount. A dealer who hears the action and announces "all in" clearly every time keeps a hand from getting to that point.

The last couple of chips

A lot of players move in and keep a couple of white chips back, or toss their last dollar or two to the dealer. (In a game where those chips play, like 1-2.) If someone goes all in and leaves chips behind, motion for them to put those in too. The most annoying thing in poker is a dealer who burns and turns while a player still had chips, and a decision, left.

Loose change and hidden chips

  • In most rooms, loose change doesn't play, even in an all-in. Bets go in increments of the chip used for the big blind. At 2-5, an all-in of $92 is an all-in for $90, and the $2 goes back. Cash game procedures covers increments.
  • Every chip has to be visible. If a player bets mixed chips and a large-denomination chip is hidden behind small ones, you do pull it out so the table can see it, even in an all-in. It's the one exception to not touching an all-in.

How to use the all-in button

All-in buttons have become really popular in poker rooms over the last ten years or so, because they cut down on a lot of errors and confusion. There isn't one official way to use them, so listen to your poker room manager, and use your head.

The TDA's recommended procedure (RP-1) says the dealer keeps the buttons, not the players, and when a player goes all in, the dealer places a button in front of them in full view of the table. It's a recommendation, not a requirement, so a player's all-in counts whether or not the dealer uses a button.

  1. Keep one or two buttons in your tray. Where in the tray is up to you.
  2. When a player goes all in, put the button in front of their chips or their betting area, completely visible. Not under their chips, and not on top of them. Touching a player's chips with the button is less professional, gets confusing, and can create tells you never meant to give.
  3. Still say "all in." The button doesn't replace announcing it. Sometimes I say "ship it" for fun, but I still wait a split second and say the words "all in." Be creative if you like, but always say the words.
  4. The button isn't a replacement for chips. A dealer throwing out a button can't turn something that wasn't an all-in into one. Have them put at least one chip in, or their whole stack.
  5. Leave it out until the end of the hand, then clean the buttons up with everything else.

Some players will want to use the all-in button as a card protector. I let them, though I'd prefer they didn't. You may work at a room that doesn't like it, so do what your supervisor says.

And if you're ever not sure whether to use it, don't. It's a tool, just like your voice. Never use it in a situation you don't understand.

More than one all-in

With one button, move it to whoever went all in most recently. With two buttons, the first goes to the first all-in player and the second to the next one. If a third player goes all in, take the original button and give it to them. The most recent all-in is the most relevant to the action, so that's where the button belongs.

The call side

Many all-in buttons say "call" on the other side. Picture a player going all in for exactly the amount of the bet. If you didn't hear "all in," it looks like a call. Maybe a player has headphones on. The all-in side makes it obvious, and one of the biggest things the button prevents is undercalls. The call side is for the next player who calls. In a 1-2 no-limit cash game, I rarely put the call side out. In a tournament, it's really helpful: knowing a second player is already in can change whether you risk your tournament life. I don't believe the TDA says anything about the call side. It's just a healthy practice in the right spot.

What I correct in class

Throwing the button late

The player goes all in, and the button goes out way after. The button goes out when the player goes all in, along with the announcement.

Putting the button on the player's cards

Students keep setting the button on top of the player's cards. We don't do that as dealers. It goes in front of their chips.

Using spacers instead of the button

If the table has an all-in button, use it, and move it between players as they go all in. In a real poker room you'll only have the buttons, not a pile of spacers to mark every all-in.

Giving the button to a player who called

A player who just called isn't all in. The button follows the all-in players only.

Taking it back right away

A student puts the button out correctly and then picks it back up. Leave it there until the hand is over.

Announcing and counting an all-in

Every time a player goes all in, you announce it. Anytime the amount to call changes, every single player at the table needs a chance to hear it, even someone facing away. You can't be lazy about this.

  • When a player can't fully call, say "all in for less." That's when you start building the pots.
  • Counting it: rooms differ. Many only count an all-in when someone asks or acts on it. Some want it counted right away. When to count an all-in covers both, and how to break it down.
  • Leave a short all-in splashed. If a player is all in for 95, don't stack it back up neatly. A restacked short stack looks like a full stack to the table and to surveillance.

All-ins in tournaments

  • All in for less than the big blind. Preflop, if the big blind is all in for less than a full big blind, everyone else still calls the full big blind. On the flop, a player all in for less than a big blind can be called for just that amount.
  • Everyone shows. In a tournament, when a player is all in and the action is done, all hands are shown, including players who kept betting into a side pot. The showdown covers it.
  • Use the call side of the button. As above, a call matters a lot more when every player's tournament life is on the line.

After the all-in

Get the chips in before the cards come out

Always have the players push their chips in before you run out the cards. How to build side pots covers multi-way all-ins step by step.

When the player who lost walks away

If a player was clearly covered, calls on the river, loses and walks off, it's fine to closely watch the winner take the chips. Any chips the loser left behind still belong to them. They're not a tip. And don't make a player who just got knocked out push their own chips to the winner. That's needling them.

Two players on the side

When there's an all-in player and two players still betting on the side, those two can't agree to "just go all in" or flip their cards up. Whether one of them calls or folds can change who wins the main pot. Exposed cards covers what to say.

Common questions about all-in rules

Can you fold after saying all in?

No. Once you say "all in," you're committed to the maximum action. If a player says it and then tries to fold, the dealer tells them it's still all in, and calls the floor if they resist.

Do you have to say "all in" out loud?

No. Pushing all your chips forward can be all in too. But saying it is the clearest way, and with a single oversized chip, you have to say it, or it's only a call.

Is one chip an all-in?

Only if the player says "all in." Facing a bet, one oversized chip tossed in silently is a call, even if it's their last chip. (A last chip that's smaller than the bet is an all-in for less.) When a player does say all in, dealers like to see at least one chip go into the pot.

What if a player goes all in for less than the bet?

It's an all-in for less. Announce "all in for less," and the other players still owe the full bet. The extra goes into a side pot. Side pots shows how to build it.

Does an all-in reopen the betting?

In no-limit, in most rooms, only if it's a full raise. A short all-in doesn't reopen the betting for players who already acted. Betting rules has the details.

Do you have to use an all-in button?

No. The TDA recommends it, but a player's all-in counts whether the dealer uses a button or not. They've become common in poker rooms, so follow what yours wants.

What happens to the chips a player leaves behind after an all-in?

If the chips play in that game, have them push those in too, before any more cards come out. Chips that don't fit the game's betting increment, like $2 left over in a 2-5 game, go back to the player.
