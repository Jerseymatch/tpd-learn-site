Live Poker Etiquette: Your First Time at a Casino Poker Table

Playing in a casino for the first time? None of this is hard, but nobody at the table has time to explain it. Here's what a dealer wishes every new player knew, from someone who's dealt millions of hands.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 19, 2026 · 10 min read

A poker table is one dealer and nine players. Most of what slows a game down is small: a tip set on top of folded cards, a check nobody saw, a bet left way back by a player's stack. None of it matters much in the moment. But there's no time to explain it at the table, and added up, it slows down every game.

So this page is the explanation I can't give you mid-hand. If it's your first time playing poker in a casino, this is what the dealer is hoping you'll do. The betting rules that go with it, like what counts as a call, have their own pages, and I link them as we go.

The short version

  • Start small and watch first. Sit down at 1-2 or a low-limit game.
  • Buy your first chips at the cage.
  • Ask the dealer how things work, not how to play your hand.
  • Protect your cards. Put a chip on them.
  • Say your action out loud, in turn. Check by tapping the felt clearly.
  • Push your bets and your folded cards out where the dealer can reach them.
  • Never set your tip on top of your cards.
  • Phone down when the action is on you.
  • If you win, show your hand. Don't slow roll.

Before you sit down

Start small, and watch first

If you're new to live poker, start at the 1-2 game or a low-limit game, and watch for a while before you play.

All you really need to sit down is to know the hand rankings and how the betting works. The showdown guide covers how the dealer reads and compares hands.

Buy your first chips at the cage

Rooms differ on whether your first buy-in for a new game has to happen at the cage. As a dealer, I follow whatever my supervisor tells me. As a player, it's smarter for everybody if you get your chips first and sit down ready to play. A large rebuy at the table may need a supervisor's approval anyway. If you're curious what happens on the dealer's side of a buy-in, buy-ins and color-ups walks through it.

Ask the dealer (about the game, not your hand)

You can ask the dealer how to put your chips in, where to push your bet, or what your options are. That's part of what dealers call running the game. What a dealer can't do is tell you how to play your hand. A dealer can't influence how you play.

When you ask what your options are, a good dealer gives you all of them, starting with fold: "You can fold, you can call two, or you can raise." Then they'll show you where to push your bet.

Protect your hand

This is the one that costs new players the most. Put a chip, or a card protector, on top of your cards.

Here's why. A dealer takes everyone's cards, every hand, all day. I worked out once that over my career I've folded something like seven to ten million hands, two cards at a time. Cards sitting loose on the felt look exactly like a fold, and a dealer's instinct is to take them. Once your cards hit the muck, they're usually dead. Learn this once and you'll never have to learn it the hard way. It's on the player to protect their hand.

Make your action clear, and act in turn

Say it out loud

The clearest way to act is to say it: "fold," "check," "call," "raise to 30." In poker, when it's your turn, what you say is binding. Verbal declarations covers exactly what that means. And wait for your turn. Acting out of turn can change the hand for the players before you.

Check by tapping the felt

A clear check is a tap on the felt in front of your cards, where the dealer can see it, or the word "check." One of my pet peeves as a dealer is the one-finger check: a pinky lifted, a tiny tap on the rail, a nod. I once replayed a hand from a livestream where a player checked with a little tap on the rail while he was shuffling his chips. It was outside where a dealer's eyes are trained to look. When I explained it to him afterward, he understood why dealers kept missing his checks.

When I see a check I'm not sure about, I say "check" out loud.

One oversized chip is a call

If you're facing a bet and you toss in one big chip without saying anything, it's a call, not a raise. If you want to raise, say "raise" before the chip goes in. And put the whole bet out in one motion. Going back to your stack for more chips without saying "raise" first is a string bet, and only the first amount counts. The technical rules of betting explains both.

Bets and folds within the dealer's reach

There's no line on the felt the dealer can point to. It's up to you. If your bet is sitting back by your stack, the dealer has to reach for it, over and over, all shift long. That's harder on a shorter dealer's body than mine, and even I feel it in my back. Get your bet at least halfway to the pot, and the dealer will thank you.

  • Stack your bet in front of you, and don't throw it into the pot. A stacked bet is the fastest thing for a dealer to handle.
  • Fold toward the muck. Folding your cards two inches forward makes the dealer reach across the table for them. When a player folds right to the muck, I compliment them, because I want them to keep doing it.
  • Seats 1 and 9, watch your hands. There's only one path for a card to reach the seat next to the dealer. If your hand or your chips are in it, the pitch has nowhere to go.

What I teach dealers in class

Give every option, starting with fold

When a player asks "what are my options?", a dealer has to say all of them. I always start with fold. With a newer player, I'll make it simple: "You can fold, you can call two, or you can raise any amount up to all your chips."

Unprotected cards are the player's responsibility

Dealers are trained to take cards that are sitting on the table face down. A dealer can't feel bad about it, because they fold hand after hand all day. And for a dealer's first six months to a year, if a folded card lands on another player's cards, they call the floor every time.

A player on the phone when it's their turn

Say clearly, "It's your turn." Give it three to five seconds, no more. Then, "I need you to get off the phone." If it comes back around to them on the phone again: "I need you to either hang up or exit the hand." How strict to be depends on how the room operates.

Tipping: never on top of your cards

I'm appreciative of any tip. There's just one way to do it that slows the whole game down: setting the chip on top of your cards when you muck them.

Your cards and your tip go to two different places. So the dealer has to stop, pull the chip off, put the cards in the muck, then deal with the tip, and that breaks their rhythm. It also risks flipping a card face up, because cards are light and easy to lift. Hand the tip over separately, after the pot's been pushed to you. I've had players tell me they'd stop once I explained it. How tips work on the dealer's side is in rake, promo drop and tips.

Phones at the table

Some rooms used to ban phones at the table to stop cheating. To me, that was killing an ant with a sledgehammer. I'm not against phones. The problem is a player who's on the phone when the action is on them. I just prefer that you don't use your phone while you have cards. Every room enforces this differently.

Leave the button to the dealer

The dealer button is one of the things dealers get wrong most. I keep it right by doing the same steps in the same order every hand: push the pot, move the button, drop the rake, drop my tip. An interruption is what breaks that. If you move the button yourself, tell the dealer.

At the showdown

  • Show your hand. Cards speak, so the dealer reads every hand that's tabled. But showing a weaker part of your hand first to draw out the other player's hand, then turning over the winner, is a slow roll. It's poor etiquette, and a room can ask a player who keeps doing it to leave.
  • Don't show your cards to another player who's still in the hand. That's not allowed. And if you show your hand to anyone during the hand, even a player who's folded, the rest of the table can ask to see it at the end of the hand.
  • Folded players comparing cards. In a relaxed room, I've had two friends who'd folded compare cards. Both hands were dead, but I don't love it, because it makes other players uncomfortable.

Who has to show first, and what happens when a winner gets mucked, are on showdown disputes.

Dealers are human

Players give the dealer credit when they win. The flip side is that they blame the dealer when they lose. I choose to go along with it. But the dealer doesn't control the cards, and dealers make mistakes and have feelings like anyone else. A dealer I interviewed in the video at the top of this page says that remembering that would change what players say after losing a big hand. When something does go wrong, the rules are there to handle it, and the floor supervisor makes the call. A good room's supervisors will tell you that dealer mistakes are part of the game. How dealers handle the other side of this is in talking to players.

Common questions about poker etiquette

Can I ask the dealer what to do with my hand?

No. A dealer can tell you your options and how to put your chips in, but not how to play. That's why a dealer gives you every option, so they don't influence your decision.

How do you check in live poker?

Say "check," or tap the felt in front of your cards where the dealer can see it. Tiny gestures, like a pinky tap or a nod, get missed.

What does "protect your hand" mean?

It means putting a chip or a card protector on your cards so they can't be mistaken for a fold. If loose cards get taken into the muck, the hand is usually dead, and that's on the player.

Is it rude to tip on top of your cards?

Any tip is appreciated. It's just one of my pet peeves, because it slows the game down and risks exposing a card. Hand the tip over separately, after the pot's pushed. How much to tip the dealer covers the rest.

Can I use my phone at a poker table?

It depends on the room, and every room enforces it differently. Wherever you are, don't be on your phone when the action is on you.

What is slow rolling in poker?

Making an opponent think they've won, then showing the winner, for example by showing a weaker part of your hand first. Show your winning hand right away.

Where should I buy in at a casino poker room?

Rooms differ. Some require your first buy-in at the cage, and some let you buy in at the table. Getting your chips first is smarter for everyone.
