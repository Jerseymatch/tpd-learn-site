What Poker Rooms Look For When They Hire Dealers

When I was hiring, I wasn't looking for the fastest dealer. I was asking two questions about every person in the box. Here's what the people doing the hiring actually notice, and how to give them what they want.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 17, 2026 · 11 min read

I was hired as a supervisor at Calder Casino months before its poker room opened, and I ran most of the dealer auditions there. Over my years as a supervisor and shift manager, I ran more than 1,000 auditions and hired hundreds of dealers. To teach this job, you have to know what supervisors look for, because they're the ones deciding.

The audition guide covers what happens at the audition itself. This page is about the other side of the table: what's going on in the hiring manager's head, before, during and after.

The two questions I asked about every dealer

After more than 1,000 auditions, it really came down to two questions:

  1. Does it feel like poker when this person is in the box? Could a player relax at your table? Is it fast and clean enough to feel like a real game?
  2. Can I trust you?

The second one is the one people don't think about. One of the hardest things for a manager is actually watching dealers deal. There's too much else going on. So once you're hired, you're on your own most of the time. You have to be someone they can trust unmonitored: representing the business, getting hands out, and above all doing it correctly, every time.

That's also why a room isn't hiring the best dealer in the building today. A new dealer spends anywhere from three months to a year in the box before their habits and speed catch up with the room. A hiring manager is judging whether you'll fit and whether you'll get there.

Small details are loud

The biggest reason people fail auditions is small details they never cleaned up. When I see something odd, my next thought is: what else is this person unaware of?

I once auditioned someone who did well overall but kept the muck under the pot. A habit that far outside normal procedure made me wonder what else they didn't know, so I hired them as a chip runner instead. (They became a dealer later.) Flop cards touching each other, with no space between them, is the same kind of thing. A supervisor sees that and assumes you have other odd habits too. That's why I'm picky about little things in class.

Everyone who hires has pet peeves, too. Mine was scissor pitching. But an audition isn't the game Operation, where one touch and you lose. Rolling the deck wasn't an automatic fail for me with an experienced dealer. I'd hire them and retrain the habit. The audition guide lists the habits that fail people most.

What you do after a mistake

You can make a mistake in an audition and still get hired. What I'm watching is what you do next. Can you adjust when you get feedback, or have you just memorized steps without understanding them?

  • Own it. The auditioner is going to see your mistakes, so don't hide them. Fix whatever affects the game, like wrong change, and save "I know I missed that" for the end.
  • Call the floor for it. The point of an audition is to make the supervisors comfortable hiring you. The worst case of calling them is "thanks for telling me."
  • Show you'll improve. A failed first audition isn't the end if your attitude shows you'll get better. If you don't pass, follow up and tell them what you're doing to improve before you re-audition.

A room isn't like an acting audition where one person gets the part. They're usually hiring several dealers and just need confidence that you can do the job. A viewer once told me they froze on a rake question and cut a stack of six instead of five, and still passed.

What the interview is really testing

A lot of rooms do a sit-down interview with the director or a manager, sometimes HR too. There's no script. They're checking whether you'll fit their culture, whether you have integrity, and whether you'll be a problem.

  • "What was your last job like?" That's partly a test of whether you'll badmouth an employer. It matters, because break rooms are full of venting, and they don't want to hire more of it.
  • "How do you handle confrontation? What if you're the one in the wrong?" A good answer is to stay calm and get someone above you to sort it out. That's how the job actually works: for anything you're not sure about, you call the floor.
  • Be genuine. They have to want you to work there. Be authentic and likable in a believable way. Interviewers are people with egos, so don't make them feel challenged. Rooms weed out people who don't have basic politeness.
  • Tell them you're reliable. What matters most to them is that you show up, and show up on time. Say so.

Personality is a real part of it. I've passed on great dealers who had the wrong personality for the room.

What I saw as a hiring manager

Every moment in the room is your interview

When rooms are hiring, almost any trained dealer gets offered an audition. But if the staff don't like you, they won't recommend you, and every interaction counts. At a group audition, the sit-down interview can almost be a decoy. How you act at the table while other people deal is the real interview. Get along, don't be confrontational, don't play to make the dealer in the box look bad, and leave your ego at home.

Reliability is the job

The number one reason dealers get fired isn't mistakes or stealing. It's attendance. Rooms usually run on a points system: a call-out is a point, being late is half a point, and at around eight points you're let go. My own record has stayed at one or two points. Reliability matters. What they want after you're hired

Getting hired starts a probation period, usually a 90-day evaluation. Here's what supervisors are looking for in a new dealer:

  • Do it the room's way. Once you're hired, anything I taught you is wrong if your boss tells you to do it differently. Say "got it." Never tell a supervisor "that's not how I was taught," and never cite a teacher or a book. Ask to learn their way.
  • Be your own teacher. From my years as a supervisor and shift manager, the worst thing a new dealer can say is "you didn't tell me this." Nobody's going to teach you everything. Treat your coworkers as mentors, learn what you need to know, and rooms love that attitude.
  • Call the floor when you're new. Supervisors mostly appreciate it, and you can be fired for not calling one. Making things up is a habit that gets dealers fired. More on that in when to call the floor.
  • Don't complain out loud. Rooms change things, even the size of the cards when an order runs out. A professional adapts. An attitude of expectation burns bridges.

And don't burn bridges anywhere. The poker world is small. At Calder I hired Victor for his first-ever poker room job and later promoted him all the way up to supervisor. Today he's COO of Texas Card House. You never know who will be in what position later.

Not every room is looking for the same dealer

What "good enough" means depends on the room and the market.

  • Rooms that are opening are the easiest to get into. They hire in bulk and are less strict. They need dealers who can do the job well enough to open, not five years of perfect mechanics. You'll improve on the job.
  • Established rooms in established markets can pick top dealers. They hire a few at a time and expect more. The desirable rooms usually take experienced dealers, so new dealers often start at a less desirable room that still pays decently, then move after six to twelve months.
  • High-end rooms usually mean one of two paths: pass an audition at an easier room and deal six to twelve months first, or train mixed games and sharpen them at the WSOP or a circuit series. More on that in dealing the WSOP and the circuit.
  • Rooms audition on the games they spread. If a room spreads pot-limit Omaha, you'll most likely need to deal it. Find out what a specific room spreads and practice exactly that. It's reasonable to call and ask what they audition on.
  • Texas rooms still have a standard. Dallas rooms, for example, often reject the mechanical habits people pick up in underground games. Learn it the casino way.

Timing matters, too (more in how to get a poker dealer job). Big rooms have turnover, so they hire every few months. And when a casino reopens after a shutdown, many former dealers have moved on, so it's a very good time to get in.

How to get in front of the people who hire

  • Apply, then show up ready. This is advice I've passed along and agree with: apply online first, then visit dressed for it, in black pants, a white button-down and black shoes, ready to audition on the spot. Keep it to about two minutes, know their games and their rake, and come back every few weeks while you keep practicing. Don't tie up the supervisors, and don't talk like a player.
  • Call ahead. A manager at the end of a long day is thinking about dinner. One of my students called the room after our last class to make sure the manager was there, drove over and was hired on the spot. Calling first is professional.
  • Never misrepresent your experience. If a posting asks for experience you don't have, call or visit and ask a supervisor honestly what they're hiring for and how to get an audition. They'll know the moment you deal, so be honest and confident.
  • A recommendation goes a long way. A strong word from someone a room already trusts makes a huge difference. A friend of mine targeted the busiest room in his area, kept applying, stopped in to chat pleasantly with the supervisors, and a former coworker's recommendation got him hired.
  • Consider getting in the door first. Chip runner, brush, cashier or host jobs get you close to the supervisors. When I hired, candidates who were close at their dealer audition often got chip runner jobs. Six months as a runner shows a room who you really are. From chip runner to dealer covers that path.

You don't need a certificate or a degree. The audition is the test. Rooms care whether you can do the job, and a good auditioner can just tell.

Common questions about getting hired as a poker dealer

What do poker rooms look for in a dealer?

Whether the game feels like poker with you in the box, and whether they can trust you to deal correctly without anyone watching. After that: clean habits, a good attitude, how you handle people and whether you'll show up.

Do you need experience to get hired as a poker dealer?

Not at most rooms. When rooms are hiring, almost any trained dealer can get an audition, and rooms that are opening are the easiest place to start. Established rooms in desirable markets often want experience.

What should I wear to a poker dealer audition?

Dress like a dealer: black pants, a white button-down shirt and black shoes, so you're ready to audition on the spot.

What questions do they ask in a poker dealer interview?

There's no set list. Expect questions about your last job, how you handle confrontation and what you'd do if you were wrong. Stay positive about past employers, stay calm and say you'd get a supervisor involved.

Can you make a mistake and still get hired?

Yes. Auditions aren't about perfection. Own the mistake, fix what affects the game, call the floor when you should, and keep it feeling like poker.

Why didn't I get hired after a good audition?

Often it's supply and demand. If a room has two openings and 50 applicants, including experienced dealers, it's hard. A room hiring many dealers at once, or a tournament series, gives you much better odds.
