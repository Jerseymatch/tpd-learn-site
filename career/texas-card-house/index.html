Working at Texas Card House: What It's Like to Deal There

I've picked up dealing shifts at Texas Card House since 2021. Here's what's different about dealing in a Texas card room, what the house procedures are like, and how people get hired there.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 17, 2026 · 12 min read

When I went to Dallas in March 2021 to deal at Texas Card House, it had been years since I'd really dealt. I'd spent most of my career as a supervisor and shift manager. About half the room had learned to deal partly or fully from my videos, which was a strange way to start a new job. I've gone back to pick up shifts ever since, and in 2026 Texas Card House partnered with True Poker Dealer to send people to my Remote Dealer School.

So you know where I'm coming from: I'm a dealer there, not part of the company's decisions, and the COO, Victor, is a close friend. I hired him at Calder for his first-ever poker room job, and later promoted him all the way up to supervisor. This page is what it's like from the box. Rooms change their procedures, so treat the details as how they were when I dealt them, and learn your room's way once you're hired.

The rooms

In the Dallas area there are two rooms about 15 minutes apart: the original Dallas room and TCH Social in Las Colinas. You can pick up shifts at either one. Austin is its own room, and it's where the WSOP Circuit has run. Each room has its own little differences in procedure, from the bank to the run-it-twice rules.

What I like most is that it feels like casino poker. Texas has a lot of underground games and a home-game feel, but Texas Card House hires real casino people. The GM in Dallas when I started was someone I'd helped train as a dealer, who worked his way up.

No rake: how a Texas card room makes money

Texas rooms don't take a rake, a jackpot drop or a promo drop out of the pot. Players pay a membership and then pay for their seat by the hour. Players load money onto an account and get scanned into the table on the tablet, and the time is collected at the seat. Promotions like the bad beat are paid for by the house. I stay out of the legal details. What matters for you as a dealer is how it changes the job:

  • You don't pull a rake. There's less change-making. Coming from raked rooms, my hands kept trying to pull a rake on my first day.
  • Time matters. The table shows when a player's time is running out. Flag players whose time is running out.
  • Slow games are fine for the house. Raked rooms often don't love PLO because it's slow. When you charge for time, a slower game isn't a problem, which is part of why Texas rooms spread so much of it.
  • More seats, more revenue. Games run nine-handed, and the livestream table runs ten.

All hand shuffling

There are no shuffle machines, so you hand shuffle every deck, all shift. When I started, my pinkies got sore from it. New dealers feel the same thing, and it goes away. It's also a great place to get your shuffle good. In Austin especially, they care about a quick, clean shuffle and pitch.

You also count the stub, like in any room. The policy I learned there is twice per down, once in your first few hands and once in your last few, and I've personally dealt a whole down with a missing ace. A deck with a card missing or an extra card is a game integrity problem. The game protection guide covers how to count it.

The games you'll deal

Texas games play big. Without high-hand promotions to chase, players buy in deep and play to stack people. In Dallas, 1-2 no-limit plays more like a bigger game elsewhere, and 2-5 plays like 5-10. In the 2-5 games, once someone has more than the max buy-in, anyone can buy in to match the biggest stack. You eyeball it. You don't count down the whole stack.

  • Bomb pots everywhere. A lot of the games include double-board bomb pots. In Austin, the 1-2 no-limit games have PLO bomb pots, so every dealer there needs to deal PLO, even if you were hired for hold'em. In Dallas the 2-5 games have $15 PLO bomb pots, so with nine players you'll have 135 memorized fast. The bomb pots guide walks through dealing one.
  • PLO and "Congress." In Texas, Big O (five-card pot-limit Omaha high-low) is called Congress. In double-board hi-lo, the room I dealt it in used one "ultimate high" and one "ultimate low" across both boards instead of splitting each board. That's that room's procedure, not universal.
  • Mixed games. Dallas spreads mixed games most shifts, and dealer's choice tables can hand you a game you've never dealt. I learned one at the table. An experienced hold'em dealer should still pass, but expect them.

House procedures that will be new to you

Every new room means relearning a hundred small procedures, even for an experienced dealer. Here are some I had to learn at Texas Card House:

  • The bank. Both Dallas rooms use a $2,200 bank: $2,000 in green and $200 in white. You count it down when you sit down, and in Las Colinas you count the cash too. The trays fit three tubes per side.
  • Tapping in. You tap in on the side opposite the tip box, and the rooms differ on which side the tip box is on.
  • Sticky chips. The chips get sticky, so I cut them sideways with my thumb. The cards are thinner, so they flip more easily on the pitch. Turn your wrist up a little more.
  • The tablet. The floor light is on the table's tablet, which also shows players' names and photos.
  • All-ins and calls. At least one chip goes in when a player calls or is all in, which I think is a very good rule. The room also wants bets pulled in before the next card.
  • A card on the floor. You get a supervisor, and the supervisor picks it up.
  • Straddles and running it twice. Straddle rules differ even between the rooms. Running it twice with three or more players needs a thumbs up from everyone. More on that in running it twice.

The smartest prep for a new room is to deal some practice hands of their games before your first shift. Once those are trained, you have the mental space for everything else.

The supervisors and the culture

Dealers always wish their room did things better. At Texas Card House, I don't. It's the only room where I've found every supervisor high quality, and that's a big difference-maker for dealers. The standard starts with Victor. The way I see it, a supervisor's customers are the dealers, and a manager's job is to make supervisors good.

  • You call the floor for support. In a lot of rooms, dealers avoid calling the floor because they're afraid of a write-up. There, the floor is your safety net. A dealer I interviewed there was terrified on his first floor call, and the supervisor just asked what happened and explained what to do.
  • Coaching first. Floors give support, and if you need coaching, they coach you instead of reaching for a write-up.
  • Fair rotations. Strings are usually four tables and then a break. I've even been asked whether I wanted a long or a short break when a table broke.
  • Stay or go. Supervisors ask each dealer once a shift whether they want to stay or go home, so the people who want to work get to work.
  • Room to be yourself. They let dealers have their own style and personality.

That doesn't mean it's loose. Is Texas Card House strict on dealers? A good room has to be. You have to care that dealers follow procedure and treat guests right, and Texas has a lot of dealers who learned in underground games. It's a casino standard. Victor told the staff that clearing your hands is for you, not management: if anything is ever investigated, clean hands on camera show you did nothing wrong.

Picking up and giving up shifts

Dealers used to envy players because players set their own schedules. At Texas Card House you can pick up shifts, give up shifts and finish other people's shifts through a scheduling app. If someone needs to go home, another dealer can come in and finish the shift. Schedules come out about a month ahead.

  • New hires get the worst schedules, like anywhere. But swapping means you aren't stuck with them.
  • Dallas is easier than Austin for swapping, because there are more games. Dallas has also let dealers work long days. Austin was stricter.
  • It can be a side hustle. One beauty of dealing is that it doesn't have to be your main hustle, and a system like this makes that possible. It's how I can live in another state and come back to deal for a couple of weeks at a time.
  • Don't moonlight across the street. They'd prefer you not also work at a nearby competing room.

The players

Dallas players are the nicest I've ever dealt to, and Austin's were even nicer. There's very little complaining or negativity, which is rare in this industry. Players don't blame the dealer for the cards, they're patient with mistakes, and they still give you feedback. When I enforced every rule for a day, the players backed me up.

You still need thick skin. My line when someone gets rude is "we don't talk to people like that here." More on handling that in talking to players.

Employees are allowed to play there, and they're held to a standard when they do. Many of the dealers are converted players, so they care about games being run well.

The livestream table

Dallas has a raised stage table for the TCH Live stream, and I've dealt it. It's different from any other table:

  • RFID cards. Each seat has a reader spot, so players have to place their cards on it. If a hand isn't read, the production booth asks the dealer to stop the action and have the seat realign. On a stream game, TV comes first. The cards are thick and expensive, so you'll ask players not to bend them.
  • A headset. You wear a one-ear headset and announce every action and amount to the booth, even folds, which you'd never do normally. Break down every bet clearly, because the cameras can't read chip amounts.
  • Shorter shifts. Two dealers split the stream in two-hour shifts. There's no tip box, so dealers wear a fanny pack.

I once burned too close to the board reader on a three-way all-in. I suspected it, asked the booth, and they fixed it before the graphic went wrong. Dealing it, I knew any mistake would follow True Poker Dealer forever.

How people get hired there

  • No dealer license. As of 2026, Texas doesn't license poker dealers, so you skip the licensing step most states have. Partly because of that, the auditions are tougher.
  • A tough audition. The Dallas audition has a high standard. In the past it has typically meant five-card pot-limit high-low and 1-2 no-limit with all the math. A dealer I interviewed there failed his first audition, got specific strong and weak points, was invited back, and passed two weeks later. Tough auditions are a big reason the dealers are so good.
  • Learn it the casino way. It's a little easier to get hired in Texas because there are fewer professional dealers, but rooms there often reject the mechanical habits people pick up in underground games.
  • Time it with a tournament series. They tend to hire around their series, when they need more dealers. Rooms that are opening are the easiest of all: when the Dallas room opened, it was hiring almost anyone.
  • Expect it to take a while. Hiring depends on whether a room is hiring right then, and can take one to three months.
  • There's no in-house dealer school. They don't teach you to deal. You show up able to.

For the uniform, the room has provided the shirt, and the black pants and black shoes were my own. More on what managers look for in what poker rooms look for.

The partnership with my school

In 2026, Texas Card House needed a lot more dealers for its WSOP Circuit series in Austin, so it ran ads sending people to Remote Dealer School. There was never a guaranteed job. You still had to earn the recommendation.

Common questions about working at Texas Card House

Does Texas Card House take a rake?

No. Texas card rooms don't take a rake from the pot. Players pay a membership and an hourly charge for their seat, and promotions are paid by the house.

Do you need a license to deal poker in Texas?

As of 2026, no. Texas doesn't license poker dealers, so you just get hired and start. That could change someday.

How do you get hired as a dealer at Texas Card House?

Learn to deal the casino way, pass their audition, and apply when they're hiring, especially around a tournament series. Expect to deal pot-limit Omaha, because bomb pots are part of the no-limit games.

Do Texas Card House dealers shuffle by hand?

Yes. There are no shuffle machines, so you hand shuffle every deck and count the stub during your down.

Is a card room better to work at than a casino?

Both are good. Casinos usually have more people, more stability and free employee dining, but stricter procedures, and often you can't play there. Card rooms are less corporate and give you more freedom to be yourself. I slightly prefer card rooms.

Can you pick your own schedule as a dealer there?

Mostly. New hires start with the worst schedules, but you can pick up, give up and finish other dealers' shifts through the scheduling app.
