How to Open a Poker Room: What It Really Takes

Everyone thinks opening a poker room is ordering tables and chips. I helped open a 29-table room and I've helped other rooms open since. Here's what it really takes, and the mistakes I see new rooms make.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 19, 2026 · 11 min read

In 2009 I was part of a three-person team that opened a 29-table poker room at Calder Casino in South Florida, where I went on to be a shift manager. I've helped other rooms open since then, including training a brand-new room's first dealers over Zoom and running their auditions. And I have the same conversation with players over and over: "I could run a poker room."

Maybe. But running a room is a lot more than poker. This page is what I'd tell anyone thinking about opening one: what the job really is, what goes into building and staffing a room, how to set your rules, and the mistakes I see new rooms make. I'm not going to tell you what your state's law requires or what a build costs. Those depend completely on where you are.

Playing poker doesn't mean you can run a room

The number one mistake is thinking that because you play poker, you understand how to run a poker room. You don't. I've been a dealer, a supervisor and a shift manager, I've consulted for rooms, and I've been to the TDA summit, and I still don't think I know enough.

Running a room takes business sense, marketing, managing people and knowing your market. Dealing and playing don't teach you those. Even in a casino, where the poker director has HR, surveillance, security and cashiers behind them, it's a full load. It's not a quick cash grab.

If you really want to do it, get inside a room first. Be a dealer for at least two months and a floor for at least two months. That's the minimum I'd do first. (If the floor side interests you, dealer or floor? covers what that job is like.)

A director I helped through an opening said that beforehand, he wondered what a manager really does besides telling dealers where to deal. Four days in, he had his answer: you're pulled in a million directions at once. Customers have questions, a computer goes down, someone has a tournament question, tables are out of balance, and the staff all need training to handle it.

Someone has to own the regulations

Opening a room means a cage, laws and regulations, an electrical layout that works for your tables, and a lot of vendors. The poker room director is responsible for knowing every regulation that applies to the room. In a state where poker is brand new, that can mean reading proposed regulations line by line and underlining every word, because all of it is new. When we opened in South Florida, I wasn't the one responsible for the regulations, but I heard a lot about them.

I'm not a lawyer, and the rules are different everywhere. The point is that somebody on your team has to know them cold, and it should be the person running the room.

Tables, shufflers, chips and cards

If I designed the ultimate poker room, the first priority wouldn't be the amenities. It would be getting the core room running like a well-oiled machine. That's the hardest part. After that, here's where I'd put the money:

  • Shuffle machines: absolutely yes. They pay for themselves with more hands per hour, and they protect the game.
  • Good tables, felt and chairs.
  • Good food.

Shuffle machines

Shufflers go through strict testing and certification, and that's a good thing. Without testing that proves a machine is fair and random, a shuffler could be programmed to do things you'd never want. The director I interviewed for my "Opening a Poker Room From Scratch" series chose a machine that reads every card's rank and suit and flags a missing or extra card. He pointed out that the cheaper kind weighs the deck instead, which catches a missing or extra card but not a duplicate swapped in for another one.

For your dealers, none of that matters much. A dealer doesn't need to know how the machine works, just how to use it. From the table, it's deck goes in, deck comes out. Running the room is what takes the specialized knowledge.

Chips

When you figure out how many chips to buy, plan for the worst case. Count what every table would have on it at its deepest, then add what sits in the dealers' wells, the tip boxes and the drop. A new independent room also has a harder time with vendors than a big casino company that just reorders. The director in my series waited months for some suppliers, and overseas chip makers meant heavy shipping costs and long lead times. On security, he found most manufacturers offer embedded infrared, and RFID is the best but very expensive. My opinion on RFID: it's an old new technology that will keep advancing slowly, and it may take over once it's cheaper and more practical.

Cards

Don't let decks go stale. Old, sticky cards slow the dealer down, and in a raked room fewer hands means less rake. Big casinos' budget processes often don't see that. Smaller card rooms tend to fix it faster. How dealers handle deck changes and suspect cards is in game protection.

Staffing: hire high, then adjust

When we opened Calder, I ran something like 330 to 340 auditions, and we hired around 95 dealers for 29 tables. Within about a month and a half we were down to 45 or 50. The business didn't come right away, because South Florida had a lot of competition. It went on to be a successful room, but that's how openings go. You take your best stab at staffing, then adjust.

A room that keeps improving beats a room that tries to get everything right the first time. Don't wait for the perfect plan.

How many dealers do you need?

Start from your hours and your breaks. A dealer can't deal eight straight hours. The usual rhythm is about an hour and a half to two hours on, then a 30-minute break. In my series, the director worked it out for his room: 10 tables, 16 hours a day, seven days a week came to 25 or more dealers, plus room for part-timers. Your number will be different, but that's the math.

When there are no dealers to hire

In Las Vegas, a room can send someone to a local dealer school and audition them later. In a brand-new market there may be no experienced dealers looking for work and no dealer schools at all, so the room has to create its own training. That's how I ended up training one new room's recruits over Zoom. I watched every trainee's shuffle, pitch and chip cutting live and gave specific fixes. Then I ran their auditions and, for once, got to give feedback on the spot. Most auditions don't tell you what you did wrong.

The director I worked with sorted his applicants into three kinds: people who don't know poker but want a job, people who know poker (from occasional players to people who play live every week), and professional dealers. With time short, his room wanted the pros and the avid players, but everyone still trained and had to pass an audition, which I ran.

One more staffing idea: let chip runners and hosts open tables. It beats dead-spreading a dealer, it saves the room the first few minutes of a new game, and it gives runners who want to deal real reps. Have supervisors watch and give them feedback. More on that path in from chip runner to dealer.

Setting your house rules

Pick one way to do everything

When you train a whole room of new dealers at once, you have to choose a standard for everything. When we opened in 2009, we put the muck on the right because of where the equipment sat, and we standardized where the main pot goes. Small choices like that add up to a room that runs the same on every table.

Not every choice will go your way. When I was helping set policy there, I didn't want dealers breaking down a $5 chip to chop a pot in a $5 big blind game. The experienced staff overruled me. I still think it slows the game, and players don't care.

For cash games, call the rooms that have been doing it longest

Don't set your cash game rules by committee, and don't let one director decide based on what seems logical. Logic creates booby traps. Instead, call the floors at well-established, respected poker rooms, ask how they handle it, and follow the trend. Tournaments have the TDA rules. Cash games don't have one universal rule set yet. The TDA rules are public, though, so any room can show them to a player to prove it isn't making things up.

The rules your dealers will need to explain are covered in the poker rules section. Remember the line between a rule and a ruling: dealers explain rules, and the floor makes rulings. That's in when to call the floor.

You're in the business of opening tables

A manager I worked for used to say it: you're in the business of opening tables. If your room is full on a regular basis, you don't have enough tables. A table can pay for itself in one busy day.

The room isn't playing against the players. Casinos make their real money on games with a house edge. In poker, players play each other, and the room takes a little from each pot or charges by the hour. More tables open means more money. Keeping your recreational players happy is worth more than a little lost rake.

Mistakes new rooms make

  • Thinking playing poker is enough. It's not. Get time on both sides of the table first, as a dealer and as a floor.
  • No-rake promotions. This is the biggest marketing mistake I see a new room make. Cutting the rake, the time charge or tournament fees as a promotion costs money and misses what players actually care about.
  • Advertising lower rake. Rooms don't do it for a reason. Calling attention to the rake makes players think about it, and it unsettles them. It deters more players than it attracts.
  • Trying to get it perfect on day one. Hire, see what happens, adjust. Keep improving.

If you're a dealer, an opening is your chance

A room that's opening is the easiest place to get your first dealing job. It hires in bulk, and it needs dealers who can do the job well enough to open, not five years of perfect mechanics. Opening weekend is long, too. When I helped one room through its opening, I worked it in a suit from early afternoon until 2 or 3 in the morning. Common questions about opening a poker room

What do you need to open a poker room?

Far more than tables and chips: someone who knows every regulation that applies, a cage, an electrical layout for the tables, equipment like shufflers, chips and cards, a trained staff of dealers and floors, and house rules. Most of all you need someone running it who understands how a room works, not just how poker is played.

How many dealers does a poker room need?

Work it out from your hours and the break rhythm. Dealers usually go about an hour and a half to two hours, then take a 30-minute break. One 10-table room open 16 hours a day, seven days a week, figured on 25 or more dealers plus part-timers. Expect to adjust once you see the business.

Is opening a poker room profitable?

Poker isn't where casinos make most of their money, because there's no house edge. The room takes a rake or a time charge. A room makes more by opening more tables and keeping recreational players happy. It's not a quick cash grab.

What's the biggest mistake new poker rooms make?

Thinking that playing poker means you know how to run a room. On the marketing side, it's no-rake promotions: they cost money and miss what players care about.

Do poker rooms need shuffle machines?

I'd say yes. More hands per hour pays for them, and they protect the game. They go through testing to prove they're fair and random.

How do you set house rules for a new poker room?

Pick one standard for every procedure so all your tables run the same. For cash game rules, call the floors at long-established rooms and follow the trend. For tournaments, use the TDA rules.

Do I need to have been a dealer to run a poker room?

Not always. I know managers who were never dealers. But if you're thinking of opening one, I'd spend at least two months as a dealer and two months as a floor first.
