Why Do Poker Dealers Get Fired?

There are 1,001 ways to get fired, but most dealers who lose their jobs lose them for the same few reasons, and almost none of them is a bad pitch. Here's what actually gets poker dealers fired, and what doesn't.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

I've been in casinos since 2007, as a dealer, a supervisor and a shift manager, and I've never even come close to being fired.

There are 1,001 ways to get fired, but if you take responsibility for yourself all the time, you won't be one of the people who gets in trouble. Here are the reasons I've seen dealers let go, starting with the big one, and then the things new dealers worry about that won't cost them their job.

1. Attendance: the number one reason, and it's not close

It's not money. It's not repeated mistakes. People do sometimes get fired for mistakes, and occasionally for stealing, but with cameras everywhere stealing is really hard to get away with. The number one reason poker dealers get fired, by far, is attendance. They don't show up to work. If you don't show up, eventually they fire you.

Most rooms I've seen run on a points system. Here's roughly how it works, though every room sets its own numbers:

  • Somewhere around eight points, sometimes ten, and you're let go.
  • A call-out is a point. You usually have to call at least a couple of hours before your shift, and you don't have to give a reason.
  • Being late is half a point.
  • Leaving early can still cost a full point if you've only worked about two hours, or half a point after about four.
  • Points usually fall off a year after they happen.

A lot of rooms now let you offer your shift up to the other dealers, and if someone picks it up, you're not in any trouble. People still get fired for attendance anyway. Then they often just get a job at another poker room, where their points start over. Don't be that dealer. My own record has stayed at one or two points. Reliability matters, to the room and to the dealers who end up dealing extra-long shifts with no break when people treat call-outs like candy.

This starts before you're a dealer, too. If you're a chip runner hoping to move up, rooms usually won't promote someone with bad attendance. Treat the whole job as an interview. That's in from chip runner to dealer.

2. Not knowing how to handle people

Dealing isn't just the cards and the chip math. You're running a game for competitive adults, many of them losing money, and you have to keep it fair and keep it moving. Dealer schools rarely train that. In my opinion, not knowing how to navigate the people side leads to more problems than any of the rules. When a dealer gets a write-up or gets talked to by management, most of the time it's because a situation at the table went sour and they didn't handle it well.

Remember who's at risk. A player who crosses the line can only be asked to leave. A dealer can be fired. So you can't cross those lines yourself, no matter what a player says to you. Know before you start whether you can take being insulted without losing your cool. How I teach this is in talking to players.

Hustling for tips

Never hint for tips at the table. Not hard hustling, not soft hustling. In some rooms you get fired for it, and it's never worth the risk. I know of a dealer who stopped the game and asked the table why nobody was tipping him. He got fired, and rightfully so. Breaking a pot into small chips just so you get tipped is hustling too (making change covers where the line is). What works instead: be likable, deal faster and be excellent at the job.

3. The rake and the bank

On the dealing side, forgetting to take the right rake is up there with the most common ways I've seen dealers get fired. There's no excuse a dealer can give for it. It's not taught enough, so train the habit: glance at the rake every single time your hand touches the pot. How is in rake, promo drop and tips.

The bank matters too:

  • Don't leave a mess behind. Leave a tray in a real mess for the next dealer, and chances are you'll be fired. If you inherit a messy one, fix it in order. Working the bank shows how.
  • Don't walk away from it early. Getting tapped out doesn't mean you're free to go. Sometimes the dealer who tapped you gets called somewhere else and doesn't tell you. The bank is yours until the new dealer is sitting down. I've seen somebody get fired for this.
  • Know your room's variance policy. Every room handles a bank that's off differently. Some make you make up a short bank. Some fire you if your shortages add up past a set amount over a period of time. Usually it's not something you have to worry about, if your bank habits are good.

4. Fixing your own mistakes, or making things up

For a new dealer, trying to fix your own mistakes is one of the quickest ways to get fired. So is guessing when you don't know a rule. Making things up is a habit, and it's a habit that gets dealers fired. When you make a mistake, say so, don't apologize, and call the supervisor. They'd much rather come over than clean up a mess later.

The same goes for anything you're not allowed to touch. If you muck a hand by mistake, you don't go into the muck to get it back. That's a good way to get fired. Call the floor and explain what was tabled. When to call the floor has the details, and mistakes new dealers make covers how to recover at the table.

5. Integrity

Stealing is rare, because cameras make it so hard, but it's the thing that will definitely get you fired. Small procedure misses won't. And an integrity firing can follow you, not just cost you one job, because surveillance departments share information about cheats with each other. Keep your tips separate and your chip handling clear to the camera. Surveillance explains what the cameras are watching for.

6. Trouble away from the table

  • Off-duty behavior. Some rooms let employees play and drink there. I've seen dealers let go for causing trouble while playing at another property.
  • Moonlighting. Working at a second poker room is its own policy, separate from your gaming license. Some rooms allow it and some don't. I've seen plenty of dealers work in more than one room, and I've seen dealers fired when their room found out. Ask your room.
  • Language. I grew up in the industry knowing that casino employees who curse get fired. Then I worked in poker rooms where people swear across the room and nobody blinks. Learn your room's culture. Don't assume.

7. Faking it into the job

When a room is opening, it needs dealers, and some people get in after watching my free videos and faking the rest. A couple of the ones who faked their way through the audition got fired too. Passing the audition is the start, not the finish. There's usually a 90-day evaluation period, and it takes about six months on the job to get good. If you rely on your boss to teach you everything, you might get fired. If you own your learning, you catch up faster.

What won't get you fired

New dealers worry about a lot of things that don't put their job at risk. Small procedure misses won't get you fired. Neither will a mechanic that isn't quite the professional way yet, like a deck that isn't squared up in one clean motion, or a fine point of how you splash chips. I teach those because they're professional, not because a supervisor will fire you over them. Dealers do all kinds of things once they're hired. I've never seen a dealer fired for details like that.

And relationships count. You're going to make mistakes, small and major. If you have good people skills and haven't burned your bridges, the mistakes you make can usually be overlooked and corrected, within reason. That's why I tell every class: don't burn your bridges. The skill plus your relationships with the rooms is your value.

How to keep your dealing job: a short checklist

  1. Show up, on time. If you can't, offer your shift to another dealer.
  2. Take the rake right, every hand.
  3. Leave your bank clean, and stay with it until your relief sits down.
  4. Call the floor instead of fixing mistakes or guessing.
  5. Never hustle for tips.
  6. Keep your cool with players. You can be fired; they can only be asked to leave.
  7. Learn your room's policies: attendance, variance, moonlighting, language.

Common questions about poker dealers getting fired

What is the number one reason poker dealers get fired?

Attendance, by far. Dealers who call out, show up late or don't show up at all run out of attendance points and get let go.

How do attendance points work for poker dealers?

It varies by room, but commonly a call-out is one point, being late is half a point, and somewhere around eight points (sometimes ten) you're let go. Points usually fall off after a year.

Can a poker dealer get fired for making mistakes?

Sometimes, but it's not the main reason. Small procedure misses won't get you fired. Trying to fix your own mistakes, making up rules or not taking the rake properly can.

Can you get fired for asking for tips as a poker dealer?

Yes. Hustling for tips, hard or soft, gets dealers fired in some rooms. Earn tips by being likable and excellent at the job.

Do poker dealers get fired for stealing?

Occasionally, but it's rare, because cameras are everywhere and it's very hard to get away with. An integrity firing can end your career, because surveillance departments share information.

Can a poker dealer work at two poker rooms?

Some rooms allow it and some don't. It's the room's policy, separate from your license. Ask before you do it. Dealers do get fired when their room finds out.

Can a fired poker dealer get hired somewhere else?

Often, yes. Dealers let go over attendance frequently get hired at another room. An integrity firing is different: that can follow you.
