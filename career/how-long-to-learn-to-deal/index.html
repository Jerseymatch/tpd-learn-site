How Long Does It Take to Learn to Deal Poker?

It's one of the questions I get most on my livestreams. Here's my honest answer: the hours it takes to pass an audition, why you can't cram them into a week, a realistic schedule, and how long it takes after you're hired to actually feel good in the box.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 18, 2026 · 9 min read

Dealing poker looks easy from the player's seat. It looks like handing out cards. Then you sit in the box and find out there's a whole world of rules that rarely come up but still have to be known. Nobody, however talented, can sit down and deal well without learning and practice. It's humbling.

So how long does it take? Two different answers, because there are two different finish lines: being good enough to pass an audition, and being good at the job. I've given versions of these numbers on my livestreams for years. Here's the whole picture.

The short answer

  • To pass an audition: about 140 hours of focused lessons and practice if you're starting from scratch. Realistically that's six to eight weeks. Over the years I've given a range of 120 to 160 hours.
  • If you already play poker: it can go faster. Some players who know the rules have done it in about three weeks.
  • To get good after you're hired: roughly six months in the box, working 25 to 30 hours a week. For some people it's closer to a year.

Compared with a four-year degree, that's not much time. But it's 140 hours of real concentration, not 140 hours of being in a room.

Why I say 140 hours, not 100

The industry used to say about 100 hours of intense learning to pass an audition. Plenty of dealer schools still advertise it that way. I raised my number to 140 because I've watched a lot of people finish 100 hours and not be ready. Dealer schools also expect you to practice at home on top of the class hours. How to practice dealing at home covers the setup and the drills.

The other problem with "100 hours and you're done" is what it does to your head. People hear it, relax, show up, finish the hours, and expect it to work on its own. Then they don't pass the audition and wonder why. The hours only count if you spend them finding out what you're weak at and fixing it.

What makes it faster or slower

Knowing poker already

Players who know the rules have learned from my YouTube lessons in about three weeks: roughly 30 to 35 hours of lessons, plus about 50 hours of serious practice on mechanics. Serious players learn faster than someone who knows nothing about poker. But knowing the game as a player is not the same as knowing it as a dealer. You still have to learn the procedure, the rules a dealer has to apply, and the mechanics.

Starting from scratch, plan on about six weeks at a full pace. At about 20 hours a week, it's more like eight.

Math and dexterity

If you like math, it's much easier. You'll be doing pot math under pressure, with players watching you. People who don't like math can still get there. It just takes more work. A background in music or sports helps with the hand skills. (What the math actually involves is in poker dealer requirements and how to count down bets.)

How much of the work is yours

This is the biggest factor, and it's the one you control. Some people find one section easy and another hard. If it's easy, enjoy it. If it's hard, put the extra time in. Your school or course is your mentor, but you are your own teacher.

Why you can't cram it into a week

People ask me if they can learn to deal in a week. You can't, not from scratch. You can't fit 140 hours into a week, and even if you could, it wouldn't stick. You need sleep for the information and the hand skills to sink in. There has to be time between sessions for it to settle. I once considered running a 12-day boot camp, and I decided against it for exactly that reason.

When I went to dealer school, it was four hours a day. That let me absorb it, rest and come back focused. Eight hours a day of this kind of concentration is too much. A three-week course that tries to teach everything is a lot.

And the hours aren't only about mechanics. Auditions test scenarios that rarely happen at a real table, like "Player A bets $20, Player B silently puts in $32. What do you do?" You're expected to know those, plus when to handle something yourself and when to call the supervisor. That kind of knowledge has to be learned ahead of time. (That one is covered in the technical rules of betting.)

A realistic schedule

Here's how I'd pace it if you're learning from my lessons on your own:

  1. Start with the shuffle. Learn it, then practice at least four hours until it looks like mine. Start with how to shuffle cards.
  2. Then the pitch, and give each early skill about a day of practice. Next is how to pitch cards.
  3. Practice about four hours a day, five days a week. At that pace, the first 18 lessons take about two weeks.
  4. Then slow down to one lesson at a time. The later lessons run one to two and a half hours each. Watch one, practice it at your own table, and get friends over to play so you can deal real hands.

That adds up to about 140 hours. It's at least four weeks, more likely six to eight, and it takes real commitment. The full order of skills is on the How to Deal page.

What I tell students in class

A few classes in, students often worry that their shuffle isn't at audition level yet. Nobody should expect it to be at that point. Every hand you deal in class means shuffling, and every bet means cutting chips, so you practice everything every day even when we're focused on something else. It can take until the end of week six for your skills to reach the level that passes an audition, and that's usually about when it starts to feel like poker. Everybody is a little different, and a lot depends on how much work you put in between classes.

I also tell my classes the same thing about schools that sell "100 hours and you'll be good": the people who rely on that often find it doesn't work just by showing up. Ask yourself what you need to work on, every day.

The three stages of a poker dealer

Passing the audition isn't the end. I think about a dealer's growth in three stages:

  1. Learn enough to pass the audition. That's the 140 hours above, and it's what my school teaches.
  2. Catch up. You're hired, and now the pitch, shuffle, rules, side pots and Omaha math have to become automatic so you can keep the room's pace. This takes about 3 to 12 months in the box. Hiring managers know this. They're judging whether you'll fit, not whether you're the best dealer in the room on day one.
  3. Be yourself. Everything is automatic, so you can be present with the table, read the game and get into a flow with the players. That's the fun part.

You can feel stage two happening. At first, a new dealer dreads a six-way all-in with a rake and a bank layout they haven't seen before, and hopes it doesn't come up. Later, even that doesn't scare you, and the question becomes, "How can I get faster?"

How long until you're good on the job

After you're hired, expect roughly one to six weeks of adjusting to real games. You're training your habits with real money at stake, and that feels different from practice.

Getting good and relaxed takes longer. My usual answer is about six months, dealing 25 to 30 hours a week, before it's second nature. I tell people 6 to 12 months to become a good dealer. Training gets you through the audition. Doing the job is what builds the habits.

Once you've dealt for six months or so, it's like riding a bike. These days I can sit down after two months off and deal without thinking. That's also why I generally advise new dealers not to rush off the box. If you leave after four weeks, it doesn't come back the same way. Common questions about how long it takes

How long does it take to learn to deal poker?

About 140 hours of focused lessons and practice to pass an audition if you're starting from scratch, which is usually six to eight weeks. Then about six months on the job to get good.

Can you learn to deal poker in a week?

Not from scratch. You can't fit about 140 hours into a week, and the skills need sleep and time between sessions to sink in.

How many hours a day should I practice dealing?

About four hours a day, five days a week, is the pace I recommend. Eight hours a day of this kind of concentration is too much to absorb.

Is it hard to learn to deal poker?

It's harder than it looks. It takes practice, patience and, ideally, someone correcting you, because it's easy to do something slightly differently without noticing. But it's about 140 hours of focused work, not years.

How long does it take if I already play poker?

Some players who know the rules have done it in about three weeks: around 30 to 35 hours of lessons plus about 50 hours of practice on mechanics. You still have to learn the game as a dealer, not a player.

How long is poker dealer school?

Usually four to eight weeks. My Remote Dealer School is 8 weeks. What to look for in one is in poker dealer school.

How long until a new dealer feels comfortable?

Expect a few weeks of adjusting to real games, and about six months before it's second nature. Some dealers take closer to a year.
