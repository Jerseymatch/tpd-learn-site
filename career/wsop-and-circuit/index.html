Dealing the WSOP and the Tournament Circuit

Big tournament series hire more dealers at once than any poker room, which makes them one of the best ways to start. Here's how the WSOP and the Circuit hire, what dealing one is really like, and how to make the most of it.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 17, 2026 · 13 min read

If you want your first dealing job fast, go where they're hiring a lot of dealers at once. A poker room with one opening and 30 applicants only takes the best of them. The summer World Series of Poker hires somewhere around 1,500 to 2,000 dealers for the series, and a busy WSOP Circuit stop needs far more dealers than the house has. A series like that takes nearly everyone who can deal.

I've sent a lot of graduates to the WSOP and to Circuit events, and I dealt the WSOP Circuit at Texas Card House Austin myself, the first time in April and again in September alongside eight of my Remote School graduates. Here's what I tell people before they go. (I'm not affiliated with the WSOP. Check their own dealer pages for current details.)

The summer WSOP vs a Circuit stop

They're both WSOP, but they're very different first jobs.

  • The summer WSOP in Las Vegas runs about seven weeks. It's one of the easiest places to start, because they need so many dealers and new dealers usually deal hold'em tournaments all day. After a whole summer of it, you can truly do the job. Each day you pick a section, like tournament events, cash games or sit-and-gos, unless they need to assign you. My graduates tell me shifts there are capped at about seven hours a day. You have to be 21 to deal it.
  • A WSOP Circuit stop is a series the WSOP runs with a poker room somewhere in the country, usually 11 or 12 days long. There's always another one on the schedule, at places like Turning Stone, Lake Tahoe and Cherokee, and each one needs extra dealers. Days are long. Expect around ten-hour days, ten or twelve days straight.

A Circuit stop can be much harder for a brand-new dealer. At the Austin series, the room ran about 80 tables of tournaments and cash games together and pushed dealers through all of it, without separating new dealers out. My eight graduates were among the only brand-new dealers there, and they had to handle cash games, pot-limit Omaha, high-low, double-board bomb pots and more. One of them was sent on day one to a wild straddled PLO game full of short stacks going all in. That's why the hands in my class are hard on purpose: the Circuit isn't the summer series.

My school is timed so graduates can go straight into a WSOP Circuit series, and the class hands are hard on purpose. In Remote Dealer School you deal on camera and I correct you live.

Why a series is a great first job

It takes months to get good after you're hired. Ten or twelve days in a professional series is a huge step: you put everything from class to use right away, and you come out a different dealer.

  • It's on your resume. A WSOP or Circuit series makes a permanent job easier to get.
  • It puts you on a room's radar. Working a Circuit stop is your audition, too, in addition to doing the job. Treat it that way, in terms of your personality and letting them get to know you.
  • You meet people. These events put you next to supervisors and dealers who can help you get hired almost anywhere. One graduate got noticed by HR at the WSOP and came home with a lead on a Florida job. Another networked at the Austin event and got himself onto the traveling Circuit.

It's also why a series is good if you're already dealing but not getting callbacks. Getting your foot in the door at a Circuit stop is a way in.

How WSOP hiring works

From what my students have gone through, you apply online through Caesars, then do a video test during their audition window. It can include rules questions and possibly dealt hands. If you're accepted, you go to an orientation, and there's a late orientation a couple of weeks after the first one.

You don't need to be fully trained when you apply. You need to be trained by the time the series starts. I've had students apply a few weeks into class, timed so they'd be ready by their audition, get hired and go deal the WSOP.

And don't give up if you don't hear back. One of my graduates applied, tested and heard nothing. We pushed him to go anyway. He flew out, went to the employment office, asked for someone from the WSOP and was hired on the spot at the late orientation. Within days he felt like he'd been dealing forever.

What games the WSOP audition asks for

The WSOP has a list of about 19 games it wants dealers to know. When I went through its dealer site, the audition was one hand of each type, and you don't know which one you'll get:

  • No-limit hold'em
  • Another flop game, like pot-limit hold'em, an Omaha game or Big O
  • A seven-card game, like razz, stud, stud eight-or-better or stud high-low regular
  • A mixed game, like badugi or 2-7 triple draw

Back then each section was scored, and a total over 80% moved you on to HR. A scored system makes sense for a huge operation, because it protects them against claims of favoritism. That information is older, though, and the WSOP also hires hold'em-only dealers now, alongside its multi-game dealers. How picky they are about mechanics depends on how badly they need dealers that year.

They'll also give you a thick dealer manual to study, with plenty of technical rules. Study it. One of my graduates went into his WSOP audition without studying it enough, got hit with technical questions and got in his own head. Search for the WSOP's dealer reference guide; the auditions usually run by March or April.

The audition guide covers how auditions work in general, and stud, draw and mixed games and Omaha cover the other games.

What your first days are like

The first couple of days, you're treading water. After that, you're above water most of the time, and after about a week you're on autopilot. Big pots still take extra concentration until you've dealt a few of them.

One graduate who worked nearly the whole WSOP told me they were nervous at first, and after about a week realized they were better than a lot of the dealers there. Another student went while still in class and felt better than about 80% of the hold'em dealers in his pool. You'll still be hard on yourself. Every dealer is. Even 15-year dealers dwell on a small mistake for a week.

  • Expect the tablets. At the Austin Circuit, each table had WSOP software on a tablet, and the room also used PokerAtlas. My students got almost no training on them, just a couple of hours after orientation. That's not a knock on the room. They were basically the only brand-new dealers there. Ask questions.
  • Find people to learn from. One of my graduates sought out floors and dealers to practice the shot clock and ask questions. The learning starts when you get the job.
  • Pick one thing every down. Each 30 minutes, ask yourself, "What am I working on?" Pick one thing, lock in on it, and don't worry about the rest unless you need to.
  • Take an early out before tiredness costs you. One graduate worked about eight hours, then took the early out when tiredness started causing small, silly mistakes, and came back fresh the next day.
  • Accuracy first, then speed. In class I tell you to get everything right and the speed will come. But at the Austin series my students were being compared to experienced dealers. One was freezing up under pressure from players, and I told him to focus on speed now. It made a huge difference.

Follow instructions the first time

The biggest lesson one of my graduates brought back from the WSOP: supervisors should only have to tell you once. A few things they're strict about:

  • Never walk away from your table. At the WSOP, a supervisor picked up a dealer's cards to prove the point.
  • Don't push in during the last two minutes of a level.
  • Say your table number when you call the floor. More on that in when to call the floor.

What I teach before a series

Tournament dealing is its own thing

  • Verify every player. As players come to your table, check who they are on the tablet. Many systems show the player's photo, which makes it easier. Every World Series, there's a story about someone playing at a table they weren't supposed to be at. Don't be the dealer who lets that happen.
  • Get your deck ready, then wait. When they announce cards in the air, listen to their instructions. Wash a suited deck thoroughly. I like to get it to the point where I can just cut it and go, with something on top of it to protect it while I wait.
  • Don't be chatty. In a cash game you can talk a little more. In a tournament, err on the side of caution, and don't let anything make you miss the action. Some of these players wait hours to get one hand.
  • Follow the rule book to the letter. If a player breaks a rule, like showing cards, just call the floor. Whether there's a penalty is the floor's decision.

Get your basic instincts locked in

For students headed to a Circuit stop, even for no-limit, I need the core instincts locked in, not just the hard math. A student who spends all weekend on pot-limit multiplication can slip on the little things.

What to bring

  • Dealer blacks. Black shoes, black socks, black pants and a belt.
  • The shirt. At the Austin Circuit, the room gave dealers black cotton T-shirts as the uniform. Expect one or two, so if you're traveling, plan around laundry. My suggestion: wear a black T-shirt underneath. It doesn't change the look, and it keeps sweat off the uniform.
  • A place to stay. It's not complicated, but book it. You pay your own travel and lodging when you work a series out of town, and lodging can eat a lot of what you make. One graduate only came out well at the WSOP because he had a free place to stay. His advice: work Circuit events for money, and the WSOP for experience.

If you live near a series, you're perfectly set up. People come from all over the country and rent a place to deal it. The pay guide covers what dealers make.

Circuit dealers: a career of its own

It isn't really three months here and three months there. Traveling dealers follow tournament series that bring in more players than the house staff can cover. They work 11 or 14 days straight (six weeks or so at the WSOP), pay their own travel and lodging, then take one to three weeks off. Series run with their own supervisors, because players at high buy-ins expect a consistent, experienced crew.

When series came through rooms I worked at in Florida, the circuit dealers seemed more professional than average. Those jobs are harder to get: high stakes, less common games and players you don't want to upset. But some of my graduates now work only the traveling circuit, 11 or 12 days on and a month off. Your life doesn't have to revolve around the job.

It's also a pattern that works for some people and not others. If you're young and single, long stretches of ten- to twelve-hour days are doable. Dealer burnout covers how to get through long days without wrecking yourself.

Why WSOP dealers get a bad reputation

WSOP dealers get criticized a lot. I don't think it's a hiring problem. It's a development problem. The WSOP needs around 1,500 dealers at once, under time pressure, so it has to hire a lot of underdeveloped dealers. Passing an audition doesn't make you a good dealer. It takes six months to a year to go from brand new to good, and there's no existing solution for that at that scale. I don't blame them.

It's also about outliers, not averages. One bad dealer out of 1,500, dealing the wrong direction, or sweeping every chip on the table into one pile to sort out a color-up, creates the reputation for everyone. A lot of new WSOP dealers technically know how to deal but can't communicate, and it doesn't feel like poker. That's why a competent new dealer who talks to the table stands out. Tables were happy to see one of my graduates for exactly that reason.

The WSOP has also set a lot of the standards you'll learn. The first place I learned to deal had me keep the muck on the right. Then the WSOP put the muck on the left, by your deck hand, and started spreading the stub, and those became the standards most rooms teach.

My own series

The first time I dealt the Circuit in Austin, Texas Card House needed 20 to 30 more dealers, so I came down from Dallas. I was dealing within about 18 minutes of walking in, and I learned the house rules and the WSOP software on the fly. That's why I tell students to be their own teacher. That series set records, and they scheduled another one there for September. At the September series, I dealt a hold'em high roller to that summer's Main Event champion, who paid real attention to every detail and decision.

I've never dealt the summer WSOP in Las Vegas. I'm waiting until one of my kids is old enough to come work it with me.

Common questions about dealing the WSOP

Do you need experience to deal the WSOP?

No. The summer WSOP hires a lot of brand-new dealers, and my graduates have gone straight from class to the WSOP and the WSOP Circuit. You need to be trained well enough to pass their audition, and you have to be 21.

How do you get hired to deal the WSOP?

Apply online through Caesars during their audition window, do the video test (which can include rules questions and dealt hands), then attend orientation. You can apply before you're fully trained, as long as you'll be ready by the time the series starts.

What games do you need to know for a WSOP dealer audition?

Plan on no-limit hold'em plus one hand each of another flop game, a seven-card game and a mixed game, and you won't know which ones. The WSOP also hires hold'em-only dealers now.

What is a WSOP Circuit dealer?

A dealer who works WSOP Circuit series, usually 11 or 12 days at a poker room somewhere in the country. Some are local house dealers; others travel from series to series, paying their own travel and lodging, and take time off in between.

How long is the World Series of Poker?

The summer series in Las Vegas runs about seven weeks. A WSOP Circuit stop usually runs 11 or 12 days.

Is dealing the WSOP worth it?

For experience, absolutely. It takes months to get good after you're hired, and ten or twelve days in a series is a huge step. It goes on your resume, too. For money, lodging and travel can eat a lot of it, so a free or cheap place to stay makes a big difference.
