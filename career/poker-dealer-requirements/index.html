Poker Dealer Requirements: Age, Background Check, License and More

People ask me some version of "can I even do this job?" all the time on my livestreams. Here's the honest list: how old you have to be, what the background check looks for, licenses, visas, language, math and what your hands have to do.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 18, 2026 · 10 min read

The good news first: the list of requirements for this job is short. A poker room doesn't care about your college degree or your grades, and there's no test except their audition. But there are a few things that can keep you out, and you should know them before you spend months practicing.

This page goes through each one: age, background checks and gaming licenses, what happens if you have something on your record, working in the US if you're not a citizen, language, math and the physical side. The short list

  • Be old enough. 18 at most places in the US, 21 at some rooms, and 21 for the WSOP.
  • Pass the background check and get a gaming license, in the places that require one.
  • Be legally able to work in the US. I've never heard of a casino sponsoring a work visa for a dealer.
  • Speak the room's language well enough to talk to players and supervisors accurately.
  • Basic math. Adding, quick multiplication, and memorizing the totals that come up all the time.
  • Use your hands and reach the table. There's more flexibility here than people think.
  • Pass the audition. That's the real test. How to pass a poker dealer audition covers it.

What's not on the list: a degree or a dealer certificate, and at many rooms, prior experience. You don't even need to know hand rankings before you start learning. You'll just have more to learn.

How old you have to be

Most places in the US let you deal at 18. Some rooms require 21, depending on where you are, and you have to be 21 to deal the WSOP. If you're not sure about a specific room, call and ask.

If you're 16 or 17 and already know you want this, my advice is to get a less glamorous job first, in retail or at a hotel. You'll appreciate dealing a lot more. Dealer culture soaks up a lot of the complaining players do (most players lose over the long run), and having worked a less glamorous job first gives you perspective.

Is 50 or 60 too old to become a poker dealer?

No. A friend of mine was a photographer in Manhattan for a decade or two. He got hired as a chip runner in his 60s, got promoted to dealer, and dealt for about eight years, including the WSOP. At the first room I hired for, several people started in their 60s. You could deal into your 70s.

You need basic dexterity. And age helps in one way: life experience makes it easier to talk to players. One of my students, in his 60s, went to the WSOP and was better than a lot of the dealers there.

One honest note for career changers coming from jobs where you sit a lot, like trucking: you'll be sitting here too, but there's no autopilot. Dealing takes your full concentration, and you'll take some negativity from players.

Background checks and gaming licenses

Casinos run thorough background checks, and in most places the state issues a gaming license. Every place is different. Some states have their own gaming authority, some licensing goes through tribal gaming, and tribal casinos can run their own checks. In Florida you go in and get fingerprinted. I paid for licenses and fingerprint background checks in Florida and Washington. In Texas there's no license for poker dealers right now, so you get hired and start. That may change someday.

What usually happens:

  • They run your record. They're looking for things like felonies.
  • You get fingerprinted.
  • Possibly a drug test. It depends on the state and what they care about. Some places test for marijuana even where it's legal. Legal is one thing, but they don't want you impaired at work, the same way alcohol is legal and they don't want you drunk on the job.

Licenses are state-based, and only a couple of places don't require one. So if you hop from state to state every few months, it can cost you a few hundred dollars each time for a new license, plus your moving expenses. Plan for that if you want to travel.

Can you be a poker dealer with a felony or something on your record?

It depends on where you want to work, and the people who can tell you for sure are easy to reach.

  • A felony will often keep you out. A lot of the time, the gaming industry will rule someone with a felony out. But every jurisdiction is different. Some only require that you honestly tell them about it. Others will say they can't give you a license.
  • Call and ask. You can call the gaming commission or the licensing office where you want to work and ask. They're not trying to hide that information, and they'll tell you.
  • Minor things are usually fine if you disclose them. Sometimes people don't get the job because they didn't disclose something. Never lie on the application.
  • An old arrest: bring the paperwork. If you have something old on your record, like an arrest from when you were young, tell the truth and bring the documents that explain it. I haven't seen many dealers lose a job over something like that when they tell the truth.

If you want a rough idea first of how a particular state looks at a particular kind of record, ChatGPT can give you a decent read. But the licensing office has the real answer. Everything here is my experience, not legal advice.

Working as a poker dealer if you're not a US citizen

If you're legally able to live and work in the US, you're fine. If you'd need a casino to sponsor a work visa, that's the problem. I've never heard of a casino sponsoring an H-1B visa for a dealer position. Dealing takes a couple of months or less to learn and turnover is high, so casinos have no reason to.

I don't know the specifics of every visa situation. For your own case, call a casino and ask for human resources. They can tell you exactly who they can hire. For your own immigration questions, an immigration lawyer is the right person to ask.

Language

You need to speak the main language of the room, well enough to communicate accurately with a supervisor and to explain a rule to a player. Think about explaining to someone why their string bet means they're not all in. In practice, that means being fluent in the room's language.

You don't need other languages. In fact, most rooms have a rule about them: no other language during a hand. I think there should be one language at the table, usually the local one. Poker is a tourist game, and other languages make it easy to share information during a hand.

Do you need to be good at math to deal poker?

No. I get asked this a lot. It's never algebra. It's "four players at $6 each" and adding calls together.

  • Know your single-digit multiplication cold. Be able to multiply all the single-digit numbers quickly, then learn shortcuts. For example, 165 × 3 is 150 × 3 plus 15 × 3.
  • Most of it becomes memory. The math happens while you're learning. On the job, the tenth time you do 15 × 7, you just know it's 105. By week seven of my school, totals like 15 × 7, 15 × 9 or 35 × 7 aren't math anymore, because they come up constantly.
  • Hold'em math is mostly counting. You're counting stacks and calculating the rake. In a multi-way all-in, you match the short stack from each player into the middle. More on that in how to build side pots.
  • Pot-limit Omaha is the math-heavy game. If math is your weak spot, practice dealing PLO hands more than anything else, until numbers like "pot is 90" are simply known.

Being slower at math just means more practice. One of my grads who gets bad at math under pressure made flash cards: 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35, times 2 through 9. Grade school doesn't make you memorize those, and since a table has nine players at most, it stops at nine. The counting bets guide shows how to read and announce big bets without doing math in your head.

The physical side: hands, reach and sitting

You have to use your hands and reach across the table. But dealers adapt, and there are workarounds.

  • Small hands. I've never met anyone whose hands were too small to deal. I teach a specific way to hand off chips with a bent finger, but if your hands can't do it that way, you grab them another way.
  • Short arms. Some dealers use a rake or reach tool for the far side of the table.
  • Missing fingers. I've seen a dealer missing four fingers on one hand who learned to pitch with just his thumb.
  • Wheelchairs. I don't personally know of a poker dealer who works from a wheelchair. Rooms expect dealers to help out around the room, so ask the room you're applying to about accommodations. If this is you, email me and we'll talk it through.

And know this going in: you're sitting in one place for long stretches. A down is usually 30 minutes, and you can get several in a row before a break. How much pain you go home with depends a lot on how you do the job, which is why I'm so picky about tension in class. Staying healthy as a poker dealer covers it.

What you don't need

  • A degree or a certificate. People ask if they need a dealer certification. They don't. A poker room cares whether you can do the job, and a good auditioner can tell.
  • Experience, at a lot of rooms. When rooms are hiring, almost any trained dealer can get an audition. Established rooms in desirable markets often want experience, so rooms that are opening are the easiest place to start. What poker rooms look for explains what the people hiring are really watching.
  • To already know poker. Complete beginners can learn. You'll just have more ground to cover. Liking poker helps, but I know dealers with ten years in who don't care about the game.

Common questions about poker dealer requirements

How old do you have to be to be a poker dealer?

At most places in the US, 18. Some rooms require 21, and you have to be 21 to deal the WSOP. Check with the room you want to work at.

Can you be a poker dealer with a felony?

Often a felony will keep you out of the gaming industry, but it depends on the jurisdiction. Some only require you to disclose it, and others won't license you. Call the gaming commission or licensing office where you want to work. They'll tell you.

Do you need a license to be a poker dealer?

In most states, yes: a gaming license with a background check and fingerprints. Each state, and sometimes each tribal gaming authority, has its own. Texas currently doesn't license poker dealers.

Do poker dealers get drug tested?

Possibly. It depends on the state and the employer. Some places test for marijuana even where it's legal.

Can a non-US citizen work as a poker dealer in the US?

Yes, if you're already legally able to work in the US. I've never heard of a casino sponsoring a work visa for a dealer job. For your situation, ask a casino's human resources department.

Do you have to be good at math to be a poker dealer?

No. You need basic addition and quick single-digit multiplication. On the job, the common totals become memorized. Pot-limit Omaha takes the most math, so practice it more if math is hard for you.

Is 50 too old to start dealing poker?

No. At the first room I hired for, several people started in their 60s, and a friend of mine started in his 60s and dealt about eight years. Life experience makes talking to players easier.

Do you need to know how to play poker to become a dealer?

No. Complete beginners can learn to deal. Knowing the game helps, and you'll learn hand rankings and the rules as you go.
