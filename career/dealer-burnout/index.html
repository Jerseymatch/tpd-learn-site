Poker Dealer Burnout: Why It Happens and How to Avoid It

Burnout usually doesn't hit in your first year. It hits once the job stops being new, and it hits hardest when your spending means you can't take a day off. Here's what I've learned about avoiding it, including from trying 50-hour weeks myself.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 17, 2026 · 10 min read

A dealer I helped hire asked me about burnout, and it's a question worth answering before you ever start. I've left this industry and come back more than once, and the job has been there for me every time. I've also tested how much dealing I can take. I set out to deal 50-plus hours a week for six months, quit the challenge early, and found out what that does to your body and your head.

What I've learned is that burnout usually isn't about the job itself. It's about how many days you deal, whether you actually rest, and above all whether your life has been set up so that you can't take a day off. Most of that is in your control.

The learning phase and the earning phase

I think about a dealing career in two phases.

  • The learning phase. It takes six to eight weeks to learn the job. After you're hired, it takes two to six months to get comfortable, and about a year, maybe a year and a half, for the job to be second nature. During that first year it's new almost every day. You're learning, and time flies.
  • The earning phase. Once the job is second nature, you're mostly there to make money. That's where burnout mostly hits. People who don't care about the game burn out more easily, and it happens more at five days a week than at three. If you actually enjoy poker, it hits less.

For some people, the learning phase lasts years. Mine still continues as a player. And you can keep it going on purpose. I got most bored when I wasn't working on something. Working on something every down keeps dealing enjoyable.

"What am I working on?"

Every time you sit down at a new table, about every 30 minutes, notice where you feel tension, mental or physical, and work on that. Repeat that cycle and in a year or two you're relaxed, enjoying the social side and reading the game underneath the game. The other option is "let me just tread water," and that's the road to boredom. Dealer health covers the physical side of this habit.

What burnout feels like

Burnout is the feeling that too much of your life is at the table, with no way out. It's not one bad shift. Some shifts are going to be far below an average day. That's the other side of the good days. Burnout is when the whole job starts to feel like a trap.

Some of what wears dealers down comes with the job:

  • Lack of control. To me, that's the worst part of dealing. You don't choose your table or your players, and at first, you don't choose your hours. You work holidays and weekends, especially when you're new. The people making decisions about you won't always get them right. They'll send the wrong person home or skip you, and you can't always make plans for after your shift.
  • Players. Everyone walks into a poker room to compete, and sometimes players direct that energy at the dealer. If you're uncomfortable around competition, the poker room can be emotionally hard. Players treat you like any dealer, and new dealers get thrown in before they're perfect and have to take criticism even when they're right.
  • The break room. In some rooms, the break room is endlessly down on the company and the supervisors. I see less of it in Texas. It's often tied to dealers' relationship with money.

The break room also exists for a good reason. When I was a supervisor, dealers would come to me hurting over an abusive player. Part of what the break room is for is letting dealers vent and let it go. Just don't let it become the place you soak in it.

How many days a week to deal

This is the biggest lever you have. Dealing is one of the few jobs where you don't need 40 hours. I think 40 hours a week is more than you need.

  • Five days a week is a toll on your mind and body, and it gets to most people after about a year.
  • Four days makes a huge difference.
  • Three days is digestible, and it can be fun. I know dealers who cut back to three days a week to recover from burnout.

My conclusion after the 50-hour weeks: about 30 hours a week is the sweet spot. At about 30 hours, dealing is amazing, and it's always there when I need money. The 55-hour weeks were harder on my body than I wanted: a tired neck from leaning over, tired fingers, and worry about trigger finger or an injury that would put me out for weeks.

If you're burnt out, say so and take a break. My recommendation is to work under full time, 30 hours or less, and use the extra time to rest or build something else. Some dealers have a full-time job and deal one or two days a week. Others work long hours on two days. Ask yourself how long you can comfortably wear the mask, and find the amount you can deal peacefully.

If you're going to work long days

Sometimes long days make sense: a tournament series, or a goal you're saving toward. A dealer once told me they could do five-hour days seven days a week, but not 13-hour days. I learned I can do back-to-back 12- and 13-hour days and feel fine. Here's what makes long days doable:

  • Decide before the day starts. When I see the schedule, I decide how long I'll stay, and I build my whole day around it, so I'm at peace with it. Choosing a long day is much easier than getting stuck in one.
  • Sleep first. Sleep is the first rule of long dealing days. I aim for seven hours minimum, and if I wake up early I stay in bed resting. On short-sleep days, I napped in my car on break.
  • Make your breaks real breaks. The break room can turn into nonstop conversation or errands. It's loud and chatty, and that's tiring. On long days I'd go to my car and shut my brain off for seven to ten minutes before my next four tables. If you're working long hours, your break has to recharge you, or you'll burn out.
  • Break the day into pieces. Set your end time and get through the first rotation. Have a small treat to look forward to on break. After two rotations, I think, "Seven hours left. That's a regular shift."
  • Take the early out when you're making mistakes. One of my graduates worked about ten-hour days during a series. When tiredness started causing small, silly mistakes, he took an early out and came back fresh.

Coming back after time off takes some rebuilding, too. After five weeks away, I wasn't rusty, but table after table wore me out mentally. The hardest part was how tempting it was when they asked, "Do you want to go home early?"

Before you're hired

The learning doesn't end at the audition

School teaches you to pass an audition. Your first year on the job is where it locks in, and that year goes fast because it's new every day. After that, think about how many days you want to deal, and what you'll build with the rest of your time.

Comfort is part of the job

Little comfort habits prevent burnout too. I bring a cut-up yoga mat to sit on every shift, I pitch gently so I don't get trigger finger, and I shuffle with a light touch. Dealer health covers all of it.

The money mistakes that cause burnout

Nobody sat me down about this when I started, and I wish somebody had. Many new dealers' income doubles, and the extra money burns a hole in their pocket. Here's how it turns into burnout.

Raising your expenses to match your new income

Go from $1,500 rent to $4,500 rent because you can, and now every shift is mandatory and stressful. You can't cut back to three days, and you can't take a week off when you're burnt out, because your life needs full-time dealer money. And time off doesn't pay much for a dealer. Most of your money is tips, so paid time off, if you have it, pays at the base rate, not your tipped rate.

When you don't have much money, you're in the habit of spending all of it. When you get more, the first thing you do with it becomes the second thing you do with it too. Your first habit with new money becomes your habit.

Anchoring your life to your best day

Dealing income is variable. Don't go into a shift expecting a number. Set a goal, but come in to put the hours in. The mistake we all made after one big day was getting to a place where we needed that much today, when that's not our average. Build your life around your average, not your best day.

Picking up poker players' money habits

People learn from the people around them, and a lot of poker players' habits around money aren't healthy. Dealers can build a gambling habit of their own. Don't imitate the charismatic players playing status games. I've seen young, great dealers whose lifestyle inflated until they were gambling big and out of control.

What works instead

  • Build your life so you need less money. You're there for the money, so the less you need, the fewer days you have to deal. If you're stuck at five days a week, look at reducing your expenses.
  • Know what each shift is for. What made my 11-hour days easiest was budgeting every dollar, so I knew exactly what each shift's money was for.
  • Have a plan. I know young dealers with low expenses who work as much as they can until they hit a monthly goal, then relax. A plan is the antidote to burnout.
  • Save while you're young. Most dealers, me included, miss the chance to save while their expenses are low. Treat a few years like college and put money aside.

I'm not a financial adviser, and none of this is financial advice. It's what I've seen happen to dealers, me included.

Common questions about poker dealer burnout

Do poker dealers burn out?

Some do, usually after the first year, once the job stops being new. It happens more to dealers who work five days a week, who don't enjoy poker, and whose spending makes every shift mandatory.

How many hours a week should a poker dealer work?

I think about 30 hours a week, three or four days, is the sweet spot. Five days a week gets to most people after about a year. You don't need full time to pay your bills if you keep your expenses in check.

Is being a poker dealer stressful?

It can be. You don't control your table, your players or, at first, your schedule, and players can take their frustration out on the dealer. Real breaks, fewer days and spending below your means make a big difference.

How do dealers handle 12-hour shifts?

Decide on the long day in advance, sleep at least seven hours, rest on your breaks instead of chatting or running errands, and break the day into rotations. If tiredness starts causing mistakes, take an early out.

Can you deal poker part-time?

Yes, and I think it's the dream setup: deal three or four days a week to pay for life while you build something else on the side. Once you have the skill, you have it for life.
