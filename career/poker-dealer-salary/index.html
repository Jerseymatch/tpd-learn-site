Poker Dealer Pay & Tips

I tell my students $30 to $50 an hour, and I mean it. Here's how that money actually comes in, why new dealers start at the low end, what changes your number, and the mistake that keeps good earners broke.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 22, 2026 · 13 min read

A student once asked me, about a week before the end of my class, "Can you really pay for your life with this job?" I almost fell out of my chair. Yes. You can pay for your life and then some. I've paid off tens of thousands of dollars of debt dealing poker. And I realized something from that question: people hear numbers thrown around and don't believe them.

So here's my number. A poker dealer makes $30 to $50 an hour, base pay plus tips. I don't tell students "$30 to $50 plus" or quote the best night anyone ever had. That range is a baseline I stand behind. Every poker room is different, and everything below is what I've seen and what my students and alumni have told me, not a promise about any particular room.

How poker dealers get paid

You get an hourly base plus tips, and the tips are the majority of the money you make. Players tip you every hand, so the more hands you deal in your 30 minutes at a table, the more you make.

The base pay varies a lot by state and by room, and it changes how the job works:

  • High base pay. Washington State requires minimum wage for everybody, including dealers, and it's a high minimum wage. The catch: when someone hits overtime, rooms like that send them home, because they don't want to pay time and a half on that base when they have other dealers available.
  • Low base pay. In a lot of rooms, the base is low and the tips make up the rest. That's very common in the industry. People see the hourly and say "that's all I get?" Then they realize they can work overtime, and they like it better. I think a low base is actually a benefit for dealers in a room that lets you pick up hours, because you can make way more money choosing to work more hours in a week.
  • Benefits. Some poker rooms offer insurance if you work their minimum number of hours. It's hit or miss; more on that below.

Cash games vs tournaments

In a cash game, players tip you directly and you drop it in your tip box. (How to handle a tip is in rake, promo drop and tips.)

Tournaments work differently. In most rooms, cash tips in a tournament don't go in your box. They go into a pool that gets split across every down. It's fair: the dealer who happens to deal the final table didn't do any more work than everyone who dealt the tournament all day. A down is one 30-minute stretch at a table, and the room pays out a down rate for each one.

In a typical day you deal three to five tables, take a break, and repeat, so an eight-hour day is roughly a dozen downs. You're paid per down while you're dealing, not for your breaks. A new dealer who dealt nothing but tournaments at the WSOP told us it came to roughly $33 to $35 an hour, the low end of my range. Cash games tend to pay better.

Tournament downs pay well during a series. Outside of one, tournament hours can be slow, especially if a competing room is running its own series.

What moves your number

I've watched dealers in the same room, on the same tables, take home very different money. Here's what makes the difference.

  • Being excellent at the job, first. The top earners I know are excellent dealers who also know how to work a table. The dealers who complain about their tips are often the ones who aren't very good at dealing yet, whatever else they've got going for them. In some rooms, the better dealers also get put in the bigger games.
  • Your speed. New dealers start toward the low end of the range. It takes a few months to get up to the higher numbers, because your first hand isn't as fast as your millionth, and every 30 minutes you're improving one habit at a time. Even taking a second off every shuffle matters in a cash game, because every hand is a chance at a tip. Players tip even dealers they don't love, as long as the hands keep coming. (More in how fast a poker dealer should be.)
  • The shift you work. Overnight is usually the best money. By about four in the morning, the players who were losing have gone home. The ones still there have bigger stacks, they're often having a good night, and they tip more. The early morning shift can be good for the same reason: at a 24-hour room, the 8 a.m. dealer walks into games that have been running all night, everyone's deep-stacked and the pots are bigger, and you get to hit each of those tables a few times before the room fills up. During the day you get more dollar tippers; at night, more "hey, I'll throw you 10 bucks." But there are plenty of poker rooms where the daytime actually beats the night. Wherever you are, you have to build your regulars.
  • Where you work. New markets tip the highest. Florida was like that early on, and Texas was when it opened. I've been told Maryland is good, and Washington has that high base. Right now, when I put the money next to the cost of living, Texas is my best overall answer.
  • Staying for your whole shift. Every time you say yes to an early out, you're giving up hours of pay. A former student of mine kept taking early outs, so I texted him a reminder. He stayed the full shift, and his last hour at a pot-limit Omaha cash table took his tips for the day from about $60 to $134.
  • How you treat people. Say thank you, sincerely, every time. Be likable and memorable. Match the player's energy: calm and friendly with the quiet ones, poker-is-fun with the ones having a good time. Learn who tips. Good-natured teasing about things everyone can laugh at is fine; never go over the line. In poker you can't chat whenever you want the way you can at a blackjack table, because the players are concentrating, so learn the culture first and then build the kind of interactions that make people want to tip you. What you can't do is hustle, hard or soft, like breaking a pot down into white chips just so it's easier to tip you. It pressures players, and it can get you fired.
  • Staying calm at a short-handed table. Short-handed tables and slow tables happen. If you go in with expectations and get frustrated after a few hands with no tip, it shows, and then you'll get nothing. I knew a dealer who muttered under his breath every time he got stiffed, and I don't think he ever realized how much it cost him. Stay calm, be yourself, and get the hands out.

What I tell my students

Don't carry the tips you didn't get

Some hands you get nothing. Most of the time you get one, two, five. Be grateful when a tip comes, and neutral when it doesn't. If a player who didn't tip makes you angry, that tells you you need that dollar too much. The dealers I see getting mad about tips are usually the ones whose expenses have caught up to their income.

Do poker dealers keep their own tips? Pooled tips and tip-outs

In poker, almost every dealer keeps their own now. I'd say about 99% of US poker rooms are keep-your-own: what goes in your tip box is yours. Table games are different: those dealers usually pool their tips. My own first poker job was a small room where the poker dealers were also the table games dealers, and for my first two years there, the tips were pooled with table games.

I'm against pooling in poker. When every dealer keeps their own, every dealer has a reason to get better and deal faster, which also means more rake for the room. When the tips are pooled, dealers care less, and you end up with worse dealers. A table games trainer I interviewed pointed out that a dead table hurts more when you keep your own. True, but it cuts the other way too. If you connect with a player on a slow table, that relationship can pay off in tips down the road.

What a tip-out is

Some rooms take a tip-out: a share of every dealer's tips goes to the people who support the game, usually the supervisors. Where there is one, you usually keep 90 to 95% of what you make.

I support a tip-out, and here's why. Poker rooms are surprisingly hard to make profitable, and some lose money. Without a tip-out, supervisors often make less than the dealers they supervise, so they daydream about going back to dealing, and good people stay dealers instead of moving up. When I've worked with a tip-out, the supervisors were more engaged and opened more games. If I were setting one up, about 10% feels right to me, shared with the cashiers, brushes and chip runners, and I'd clear it with a lawyer first. Tip-out rules depend on the room and the law, and this isn't legal advice. But if I'm tipping out to my supervisors, I expect them to treat the dealers well.

(Dealers also say "tipping out" for cashing out your tip box at the end of a shift. That's in rake, promo drop and tips. For the player's side, how much to tip and why, see how much to tip a poker dealer.)

Real numbers from my world

  • Long weeks. I know dealers who choose to work 70 hours a week. That's hard on the body, and they usually do it to save up, not forever. In a room that lets you pick up shifts, the extra hours are where the big weeks come from.
  • The WSOP. One of my graduates dealt the summer WSOP: tournaments only, no overtime, 10 to 11 downs a day, roughly $33 to $35 an hour. Then subtract a flight and a room in Las Vegas. His advice was to work the circuit events for money and the WSOP for experience. People have told me a full summer can clear about $12,000 to $13,000. You have to be 21 to deal it.
  • The WSOP Circuit. One graduate told me 12 days dealing a circuit event equaled a month and a half at his old job.
  • Compared with retail. If you're working retail now, a poker dealer will likely make at least double.

Dealer vs supervisor, and dealer vs player

People are surprised by this, but poker dealers usually make more than poker supervisors. Supervisors often get full-time benefits, and dealers generally earn more. (I believe the WSOP is an exception, where supervising is considered a privilege.)

And compared to playing: a winning player in a 1/2 pot-limit Omaha game might make $5 to $7 an hour. The dealer always wins. If you're a poker player who's at the table all the time anyway, the dealer's seat is the one seat that's guaranteed to make money. There are no real swings, just really good tables, okay tables, and the occasional bad one.

I know this one personally. Around 2015 I tried playing poker for a living with too small a bankroll, and I got beat. As a dealer, you leave every 30-minute down with more money than you sat down with. No losing months, and you never pay the rake. A poker pro I work with says a good dealer does better than grinding 1/2 or 1/3 as a player. Some of my friends who play professionally split their time, half dealing and half playing.

How to keep the money you make

This is a mistake I see all the time. Too many dealers start making more money than they ever have, and their expenses go up right along with it. When you don't have much money, you're in the habit of spending all of it. Get more, and the first thing you do with it becomes a habit too. Move from $1,500 rent to $4,500 rent because you can, and now every shift is mandatory and stressful. That's how dealers burn out and get stuck.

Nobody sat me down about this when I started, and I wish somebody had. So in my school we talk about it directly, and a financial professional comes in to talk to each class about the basics. I'm not a financial adviser and this isn't financial advice. But here's the idea in one of my videos: work hard for a few years while you're young, put money aside, and give it time to grow.

Common questions about poker dealer pay

How much do poker dealers make an hour?

In my experience, $30 to $50 an hour with tips. New dealers start toward the low end and move up over the first few months as they get faster. Every room is different.

Do poker dealers get paid hourly or by tips?

Both. You get an hourly base pay, and tips on top. The tips are most of your income.

Do tournament dealers make less than cash game dealers?

Usually a little less. Tournament tips are pooled and paid per down, and cash games tend to pay better on average. During a big series, tournament downs can pay well.

Do poker dealers make more than table games dealers?

In the US, poker dealers usually earn more than table games dealers. It depends on where you are. Poker dealer vs blackjack dealer compares the two jobs.

Do poker dealers make more than supervisors?

Usually, yes. Supervisors often get benefits, but dealers generally earn more.

Do poker dealers get benefits or health insurance?

It's hit or miss. The bigger the company, the more likely there are benefits, and a poker room that's part of a casino is more likely to have them than a casual card room. Even then, benefits often go only to dealers who work enough hours, and some rooms that offer benefits try to keep their part-time dealers under 30 hours a week. Most dealers don't get benefits through work. I buy my own health insurance through healthcare.gov. This is my experience, not insurance or financial advice; ask the room and a licensed professional about your own situation.

Do poker dealers keep their own tips?

In almost every US poker room, yes. Some rooms take a tip-out, so you keep around 90 to 95%. Table games dealers usually pool.

What happens to a dealer's pay if you get hurt?

Most of your pay is tips, and tips only come when you're dealing. If you get injured and can't work, your income drops way down, and paid time off is often paid at minimum wage, not what you'd make with tips. That's one more reason to keep savings.

Are poker dealers in a union?

Sometimes. Some casinos have dealer unions, in places like Connecticut and Maryland. I've chosen to work at rooms without one, and in my experience I haven't seen much benefit from them; they can slow change down. I'll admit I don't know a lot about them. Where dealers are treated badly, I understand why people want one. Good HR matters a lot, and in my experience casino HR departments are generally effective.

Is being a poker dealer worth it?

For the money, the flexibility, and a skill you keep for life, I think so. It does mean working holidays, long weeks that can be hard on the body, and starting at the low end of the pay range while you get faster.

Can you live off a poker dealer's income?

Yes. I've done it, and I've watched plenty of dealers do it. The key is not raising your expenses to match your new income.

Start earning sooner

The faster you're trained, the sooner you're making it. Eight of my graduates each made back three times what they paid for school in 11 to 12 days of dealing a WSOP Circuit event. That was one busy series, not a promise: every room is different, and new dealers start toward the low end of the range.
