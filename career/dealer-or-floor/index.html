Dealer or Floor? The Poker Career Decision Nobody Warns You About

I was a supervisor for about seven years, two of them as a shift manager, and then I went back to dealing. Here's how I think about the question every good dealer eventually gets asked: do you want to be a floor?

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 17, 2026 · 10 min read

Usually it happens like this: a supervisor leaves, management starts thinking about which dealers would be good on the floor, and someone comes over and asks if you'd like to do it. It's a real compliment. You think, "Really? Me?" And then you don't want to let them down, and a lot of people just say "Sure." It's pretty rare for somebody to say no.

It isn't an easy question, and there's a bunch of factors in it. I've been on both sides. I got promoted to dual-rate supervisor a few months into dealing, supervised for about seven years (two of them as a shift manager), and went back to dealing at Hard Rock in 2016. I've also been the shift manager trying to figure out which dealer to ask. I talked through all of this with one of my Remote School graduates, who got put on the tournament floor in his fourth week of dealing.

What a floor supervisor actually does

Dealers run one game. The floor is responsible for the whole room: the dealers, but also the cage, the hosts up front and the wait staff. When I think about flooring, there are basically three positions:

  • Running the rotation (sometimes called the DC): deciding which dealer goes to which table and when everyone gets a break.
  • Running the tournament.
  • Support: backing up whoever's in charge of the room.

I work ahead. When I'm running a room, I'm simulating in my head what could happen in the next 30 to 60 minutes and preparing for it. With the rotation, I'm usually an hour or two ahead. At first that's tiring. Once you're used to it, it's relaxing, because for the most part nothing goes wrong.

Then there are floor calls. In the room where I was a supervisor, the supervisor came up on the dealer's shoulder and spoke quietly but audibly, which makes the table go silent. The dealer explained what happened to the supervisor only, then the supervisor talked to the player. If a player needs to hear a ruling they won't like, I pull them aside and lead with "what happened?", so they get to speak first and don't feel cornered.

And you don't have to know every answer. People who just became supervisors often think they have to walk in with an authoritative hammer. If you don't know exactly what to do, get someone with more experience and get it solved. There's pressure in poker not to stop a game, because more hands means more money for the house, but getting the right answer is worth it.

What a dual-rate supervisor is

Most dealers don't go straight to full-time floor. They become a dual rate: you deal some days and floor others. Sometimes it switches in the middle of a shift, when they tap you out of your table and tell you to put on a dress shirt and floor.

Here's what nobody tells you. When I was a dual rate, about three and a half months into being a dealer, I ended up flooring most of the time on the difficult days and dealing on the slow, boring ones. When they didn't need me on the floor, they had me deal. You become a tool in the room's tool belt, not in a negative way, and they use you the way that works best for them. There can be weeks, even months, where your whole schedule is flooring because someone is on vacation or someone left, and you're looking at it thinking, "When am I going to get to deal again?"

The money: floors often make less

This is a weird thing about the industry, but it needs to be talked about. In most of the industry, supervisors make less per hour than dealers. As a supervisor, you're often paid a flat rate. You might be taking a 25% pay cut to be a floor.

So why do people do it? Dual rates usually go the full-time route, because they're so valuable to a room. That often means full-time hours, PTO you actually get paid for, and full-time benefits. If you need the benefits, do whatever you need to do. It's a real reason. Just know the trade-off, and ask around before you give them an answer. For more on this, read poker dealer pay and tips.

Tournament director jobs vary property to property. Some are salaried and have you work the floor as well. A traveling tournament director is usually paid hourly.

Deal first, and get your fill

I generally advise new dealers to stay dealers longer than a few weeks. It takes about six months to get good as a dealer. And once you've dealt for six months, it's like riding a bike. Years later, you get back on, practice a little, and it comes back. I'd been off the box for about seven years, flooring the whole time, and when I passed the audition and dealt my first table, I felt totally comfortable again in 30 to 60 minutes. If you never get that six months, it doesn't come back the same way.

The dealing foundation also matters for the job itself. Supervisors who went to the floor before they really dealt long enough don't always understand what it's like for the dealers, and they can be detached in ways that make it hard to relate to the people they manage.

And I personally think the first year as a dealer is pure enjoyment, because there's so much to learn. You can always supervise later. I never really got my fill of dealing before I moved up. It took me from 2009 until 2016 to get back to dealing full time, and I missed it the whole time. I don't really regret anything, but I wish I'd spent more time dealing early on.

Why the floor can be worth it

None of this means the floor is a bad move. There's far more learning in flooring than in dealing. It took me a few years on the floor to get to where I could walk in relaxed, ready for whatever came up. Before that, every floor call was "I need to remember what to do here." If you love learning poker and the depth of it, flooring, and especially tournament directing, is a yes.

  • It makes you a better dealer. Dealing is so much easier once you have the perspective of the floor calls. And after years as a supervisor, knowing how to have hard conversations makes dealing to difficult players much easier.
  • You're never stuck at a table. If there's a player with bad breath or an annoying player, you get to walk around.
  • It's where you learn to teach. Teaching isn't something you learn as a dealer. I can do True Poker Dealer because I floored for so long.
  • It builds a long career. If you're going to be in the poker world for the rest of your life, the experience on your résumé gives you an edge. As a tournament director you'll almost definitely get to go to the TDA Summit at some point, and I highly recommend that experience.

It isn't all upside. As a supervisor and shift manager, I was always thinking about how to make the room better, even after my shift. As a dealer, you do your thing and go home. That's a big part of why I chose to go back to dealing. And looking back on my floor years, I was professional, but I wish I'd been way nicer to people.

What makes a great tournament director

Self-responsibility. There are great tournament directors out there, and there are also people who get the job, even smart ones, who don't own every detail that impacts the series when nobody specifically told them to. The great ones do. Before going down that road, though, deal it all: pot-limit Omaha and stud tournaments, and no-limit hold'em cash, where you learn to make change efficiently in the box and deal to cash players.

What I teach my classes

Be your own teacher. They will throw you into things.

I tell my classes over and over that once you're hired, you're going to be thrown into situations nobody trained you for, and you'll have to figure them out. One of my graduates found that out in his fourth week, when he came in to deal and was asked to floor the whole day. Over the following months, with very little formal floor training, he trained himself: whenever the floor got called to a nearby table, he listened, and on his break he looked the situation up in the TDA rules. That's the habit that makes a good floor.

Before you say yes, ask these questions

  • Can I go back to dealing? Find someone who's been at the room a while and ask whether supervisors who decide they just want to deal again are fully supported. Some rooms, like the Texas rooms I'm involved with, let you go up and down pretty much whenever you want, though sometimes they make you wait a couple of months while they fill the position. Others frown on it. Find out before you answer.
  • What's the pay? Find out what you'd make on floor shifts and for PTO. Most places won't pay a dual rate's PTO at the dealer base rate. They usually give you a mixed rate.
  • Do I need full-time or benefits? If you do, that can settle it.
  • If I'm temporary, does this lead to a permanent job? Locking up a permanent position comes first. My graduate was in a 90-day temporary job when they started having him floor. I suggested he say something like: "I'm loving this, and I'm a little stressed not knowing if I get to keep working here after 90 days. Is having me floor a sign this leads to a permanent position?" Then let them advise you.

Ask for what you want

The poker industry is the best example I can think of a place where you should ask for what you want, in the right way. There's no built-in part of the job where someone comes to you and asks what you want. I learned this the hard way, because for a long time I didn't ask and expected people to read my mind. As a manager, so many dealers, chip runners and brushes came to me asking for what they wanted, and if I could give it to them, I did. If they want you on the floor four days a week and you want to keep dealing, say so, and suggest a schedule that works for both of you.

My rule of thumb

If every day you go in looking forward to dealing, I wouldn't be a supervisor yet. One day you'll go into work and think, "I could use a break from this. I've been doing this for a while." That's the day to be a supervisor.

So when they ask, I ask myself one question: do I still look forward to dealing every day? If I do, then unless the floor job pays more than I'm making now (it usually doesn't), or I need full time, I'm content being a dealer for now. You can tell them, "I'm definitely interested, just not quite yet. I've still got a little more to learn as a dealer." That's a good answer.

Common questions about becoming a poker floor supervisor

Do poker floor supervisors make more than dealers?

Usually not. In most of the industry, supervisors make less per hour than dealers. Supervisors often get full-time hours and benefits instead.

What is a dual-rate supervisor in poker?

A dual rate is a dealer who also works as a floor supervisor, dealing some shifts and flooring others, sometimes switching in the middle of a shift.

How long should you deal before becoming a floor?

At least six months, so dealing becomes second nature and comes back easily later. Ideally, deal until you stop looking forward to it every day.

Can a poker supervisor go back to dealing?

It depends on the room. Some rooms fully support moving between dealing and flooring, and others frown on going back. Ask someone who's worked there a while before you accept.

What does a poker floor supervisor do?

Runs the room: the dealer rotation and breaks, floor calls and rulings, tournaments, and the staff around the games, including the cage, the hosts and the wait staff.

How do you become a poker tournament director?

Deal first, including Omaha, stud and cash games. Then floor tournaments, learn the TDA rules, and take ownership of every detail of the events you work.
