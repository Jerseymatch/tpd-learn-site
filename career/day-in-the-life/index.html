A Day in the Life of a Poker Dealer

I started in casinos in 2007 and I still pick up dealing shifts in Texas. Here's what a shift actually looks like, from before you clock in to the long days at a tournament series, plus the small habits that make the job easier on your body and your head.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 16, 2026 · 11 min read

People picture a poker dealer as someone who shuffles and pitches cards for eight hours. That's part of it. But a shift has a rhythm of its own: a rotation that moves you from table to table, breaks, a dealer who comes to "push" you off the table, players you get to know, and on a tournament series, long days back to back. Most of that you only learn on the job, and it's a lot of what makes your first week feel intense.

I started in casinos in 2007, and I've left the box and come back to it several times. I still pick up dealing shifts in Texas. I've worked 20 days in a row averaging 11 hours in Dallas. Here's what the day actually looks like.

Before your shift

How your shift goes starts before you sit down. A few things I do:

  • Eat before work, not at the table. I eat a healthy breakfast, eat very little at work (I always have a green apple), drink water, and have my coffee at least an hour before my shift. Being stuck at the table needing the bathroom is the worst. Plan your food.
  • Take care of your breath. You're sitting right next to players for your whole down. I brush and use mouthwash before work so I don't have to think about it.
  • Bring something to sit on. I cut a cheap yoga mat into circles shaped like the room's chairs and carry one from table to table. It gives more grip, and you're not sitting in someone else's sweat (you share those chairs with every dealer in the room).
  • Wear the right shoes. Where the room allows it, I wear inexpensive solid black barefoot shoes. Dealers sit most of the shift, and my feet feel free.

Then the most important one: show up. The number one reason poker dealers get fired isn't mistakes, and it isn't stealing (with cameras everywhere, that's hard to do). It's attendance. Rooms typically give you eight to 10 points: calling out is a point and being late is half a point. A lot of rooms now let you offer your shift to other dealers instead, with no penalty. Use that.

The rotation: downs and breaks

A poker room can't have dealers sitting at one table all day. Dealers don't want to deal 30 minutes and then sit 30 minutes over and over, and they don't want to deal four hours straight without getting up, either. So the room builds a rotation, a string of tables for each dealer.

  • A down is your time at one table, usually 30 minutes. Then you move to the next table on your string.
  • Breaks: poker rooms try to have you do three or four tables and then get a break. On a typical day you deal an hour and a half or two hours in a row, then get a 30-minute break.
  • Rooms vary. Some rooms put you at one table for an hour at a time and break you every three or four hours.
  • A brush down: busy rooms sometimes put a "brush" in the rotation instead of a break. For 15 to 20 minutes you handle lights, fills and player needs instead of dealing. I'd rather take the break.
  • The early out list. Some rooms keep an "EO" list. You tell the supervisors you'd like to leave as early as possible, and when it's time to cut dealers, they go down the list.

Rooms also keep opening games through a busy night. At 6:00 on a Saturday a room might keep opening tables until about 10:00, so it needs extra dealers available to open games without wrecking the rotation. It's also why poker rooms hire so often: at just about every room I've seen, there's a hiring cycle every three to six months.

Tapping in, tapping out and the push

The dealer coming to take your table is "pushing" you. (The rest of the poker room lingo is in poker dealer terms.) Every room has its own details, but here's how it usually goes.

  1. Wait for the hand to end. While the dealer in the box finishes their hand, count the bank over their shoulder if you can. Most poker games have a fixed amount in the bank, so you know what should be there.
  2. Tap in on the room's side. Typically you exit on whichever side the tip box is on. A lot of rooms have the tip box on the right, so dealers come in on the left and leave on the right, grabbing their box on the way out.
  3. The dealer leaving leaves the deck, gets up, clears their hands, and keeps an eye on the bank until the new dealer actually sits down. You're responsible for that bank until someone else is in the seat. Every once in a while the incoming dealer fakes you out by accident (the supervisor tells them they're at the wrong table) and walks off. If you'd already left, that's on you, and you could get fired.
  4. The dealer coming in sits down, sets the tip box in place, swaps in the other deck from the well (there are two decks at a poker table; with a shuffle machine the procedure can be different), greets the table, and gets right to the hand.

Watch the push. When a new table gets added to the rotation, the pushing dealer often skips someone, and that's how you get stuck at a table. Pay attention to who's pushing and where. In rooms that use the PokerAtlas tablet, swiping down shows a small clock only the dealer sees, so you can notice when your time is up and no one is coming. (I stopped wearing a watch to reduce tension in my wrist.)

Once the table's set, get back to the game

Talking with the players is part of the job, and I want your personality at the table. But as soon as the cards are organized and the table's set, get back to the game and prompt the action. You're the dealer. We can never be waiting for you. I point this out in class all the time, because if you're chatting with the person auditioning you when it's time for the players to act, you're probably not getting the job.

In the box

Once you're dealing, the job is running the game. A few things about the day-to-day that surprise new dealers:

  • You never have to argue. State what happened and the rule. If there's any resistance, call the floor. The industry wants you to call, so don't worry about bothering them.
  • You never root for anyone. In table games the house is the opponent, so a dealer can cheer for a blackjack player. In poker the players play each other. You never take a side.
  • The tablet runs a lot of the room. Many rooms use a tablet at the table to call the floor, chip runners and servers. Nobody teaches you that system before your first day.
  • Real money changes hands. In Dallas I push pots of a few thousand dollars nearly every day. Losing that is painful for the player. A dealer they can't follow makes it insult to injury, so count clearly and talk your game.
  • Tips go in the box. When a player tips you, you tap the table (it tells surveillance you're putting a tip in your box), drop it in your tip box, and clear your hands. Follow your room's exact procedure.
  • Deal the cards you're given. Some decks are great and some are old and sticky. A professional dealer figures out how to shuffle and pitch whatever's on the table. You can ask for better equipment, but blaming management won't help you.
  • The microphone. If you ever have to talk on the poker room mic, use your low voice. Sound carries strangely through a casino.

And you get to know people. After years in the Dallas rooms I have a whole world of players I know, some I now see outside the casino. Don't pressure yourself to learn the regulars' names early, though. It takes months.

Your breaks

  • Wash your hands first, every time. I don't touch my face on break until I've washed my hands thoroughly, and I get noticeably less sick.
  • Use the break room to learn. When you're new, the break room is an excellent place to learn about dealing and supervising. Most dealers and floors on break are happy to answer just about anything.
  • Don't soak up the negativity. In some rooms, the break room is endlessly down on the company and the supervisors. I see less of it in Texas. You don't have to join in.

Long days and tournament series

At a tournament series, the days get long. One of my graduates worked 10-hour days for 10 to 12 days straight. When tiredness started causing small, silly mistakes, he took an early out so he could come back fresh. That's a good use of the EO list. Long days also make your body matter.

  • Learn your chair. Dealers never learn what the knobs on the dealer chair do, because players are always waiting. An occupational therapist who came on my show recommends playing with it when you're not dealing, and adjusting it every time you sit down.
  • Sit in a position you can hold. Her target is feet flat, knees and hips at about 90 degrees, shoulders over your hips and elbows relaxed. It's a target, not all or nothing. Another fix we've talked about on my show for lower back pain: lower the chair and sit back against it. It puts a little more on your shoulders and takes about a week to get used to.
  • Turn with the chair, not your neck. Every dealer chair swivels. Go with the chair on the pitch.
  • Keep your mechanics low-tension. Pitch with as little motion as possible and no flick of the wrist. Comfort is longevity, and in a room that allows overtime, longevity is a lot of money. (More on that in how to pitch cards.)

Your first day and first week

Your first day as a dealer will feel intense. It's real people and their real money, with all the rules and procedures coming at you at once, plus things nobody taught you, like the room's tablet system. A recent graduate described it well on one of my livestreams: the first hand is the hardest, and once that rush of nerves passes, it's fine.

  • Don't count on shadowing. Some rooms let a new dealer watch another dealer first. That's more of a table games culture thing. In poker-only rooms, you may be put straight on a table.
  • Do it the room's way. Every room has its own details. If a supervisor wants something done differently than I taught you, do what your supervisor says. Never tell them "that's not how I was taught."
  • The nerves fade. One of my graduates said that once he got comfortable, the players barely noticed he was there, and his anxiety eased after about 100 hours of dealing.

The lifestyle

  • You'll work holidays. Know that before you start. Some tournament rooms close on Thanksgiving and Christmas.
  • Your schedule can be flexible. Many rooms let you give up and pick up shifts with an app, and choose days or nights. When my kids were small, working nights let us cut down on daycare. Days and nights also pay differently, which I cover in poker dealer pay and tips.
  • You don't take it home. As a supervisor I thought about work after my shift. As a dealer, maybe once a month I'll replay something that happened at a table. It's like going to school with zero homework.
  • You can live almost anywhere. I grew up in Massachusetts, broke into the industry in Washington State, and dealt in Florida and Texas. Every move, I could get a job quickly without starting a new career.
  • Card rooms and casinos feel different. In a card room, everything is about poker. In a casino, poker makes far less money than slots and table games, so table games procedures can bleed into the poker room.

Common questions about a poker dealer's day

How long is a poker dealer's shift?

A common shift is about eight hours. At tournament series, dealers can work 10-hour days for 10 days or more in a row. Some rooms keep an early out list for when they're cutting dealers.

How long is a down?

Usually 30 minutes at each table. Some rooms do an hour per table.

How often do poker dealers get breaks?

Typically after three or four downs, so every hour and a half to two hours, you get about a 30-minute break. It depends on the room's rotation.

Do poker dealers sit or stand?

Poker dealers sit. Table games dealers stand. Sitting all shift is why your chair setup and posture matter so much.

What does "push" mean for a poker dealer?

The push is the dealer who comes to take over your table when your down is up. Watch who's pushing, so you don't get skipped and stuck at a table.

Do poker dealers work holidays?

Yes. Know before you start that you'll work holidays. Some tournament rooms close on Thanksgiving and Christmas.

Is being a poker dealer stressful?

Your first days are, because it's real money and everything is new. Once your fundamentals are instinct, it gets much easier. And unlike a lot of jobs, you don't take it home with you.
