Is Being a Poker Dealer a Good Job? The Honest Pros and Cons

I sell a course that teaches this job, so it would be easy to only tell you the good parts. That wouldn't be right. Here's what I love about dealing, what's hard about it, and who I think should and shouldn't do it.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 18, 2026 · 9 min read

I've worked in casinos since 2007, as a dealer, a supervisor and a shift manager, and today I still pick up dealing shifts. My own experience has been mostly positive. But I teach people to do this job, and it wouldn't be right to make it look only positive. You should know the downsides before you put months into practicing.

So here's my honest list. The quick version: it's a great job for the right person, and the biggest costs are your schedule and how much control you have over your day.

The pros

Good money for a skill you can learn in months

Dealing pays well for a job that takes months to learn, not years. Most of the money is tips, and new dealers start toward the low end and get faster. I break the numbers down in poker dealer pay and tips. And if you play poker, here's the part people don't think about: as a dealer, you leave every 30-minute down with more money than you sat down with. No swings, no rake and no losing months.

You don't take the job home

This is a big one. In a lot of jobs you think about work at night and answer emails after hours. Once you're past the early learning, a dealer's job stays at the casino. As a supervisor I thought about the room after my shift. As a dealer, maybe once a month I'll replay something that happened at a table. It's like going to school with zero homework.

Lots of breaks

You deal for an hour or two, then get a break of 20 minutes to an hour, several times a day.

Time for the rest of your life

An experienced dealer can work three or four days a week and pay the bills, then use the rest of the week for interests and passions. One way to do it: three days making your money, a day to recover, and three days on whatever you're building. It takes a certain kind of person to actually use that time, but the opportunity is real. And scheduling has become much more flexible. In 2016 there was no shift-swap software where I worked. Now many rooms let dealers pick up, give up and swap shifts, so you can nearly build your own schedule.

A safety net for life

Once you've had the job and worked it a little, the skill is yours. I've left dealing several times over my career and come back, and it was there every time. That let me take risks as an entrepreneur: try a business, and if it fails, go back and deal. Before I learned to deal, my fallback would have been a retail job. Never being scared that I couldn't make rent is the thing a lot of people go to college for, and dealing gave me that. The one condition: don't burn bridges. The poker world is small.

An orderly workplace, and you're not the one making the decisions

What drew me to dealing was that it's a structured profession. You learn the rules and procedures, perform them well, and learn a little about talking to people and what lines not to cross. No selling yourself, no office politics, no reports. Casinos are procedural and orderly compared to a lot of jobs. It's not glamorous (you'll see a whiskey glass with gum in it, and people spitting tobacco), but the supervisors handle the decisions, so dealing can be relatively low-stress. And it's poker. Nothing at the table is life or death, and the game stays interesting every day.

No degree, and you can move

The casino doesn't care about your degree. You can learn this job in weeks of real grinding instead of years, without the debt. Once you've had the job, you can get one almost anywhere there's a poker room. I've worked in Washington State, Florida and Texas. Some dealers travel on purpose, working two weeks in a new city and then taking time off.

The cons

Nights, weekends and holidays

People work when their customers are off work, and casinos often don't tell you this during hiring. It's not a 9-to-5. You will work holidays, and it takes years before you're not the one coming in. Some dealers rarely see their kids in the evening. My first blackjack instructor told our class on day one: if working holidays isn't for you, leave now.

You don't control much

To me this is the hardest part of dealer life. You don't choose your table, your players or, at first, your hours. You work for people whose decisions about you won't always be perfect: they'll send the wrong person home, or skip you. It's hard to make plans for after a shift. When you take a vacation, you have almost no income, because paid time off, if you get it, pays your base wage, not your tips.

Players will be hard on you

Everyone walks into a poker room to compete, and sometimes players direct that energy at the dealer. If you're uncomfortable around competition, it can be emotionally hard. Players treat you like any dealer, new dealers get put in the box before they're perfect, and you'll take criticism even when you're right. Most players lose over the long run, and dealers hear a lot of the complaining. How to handle it is in talking to players.

You're stuck at the table

A down is usually 30 minutes, and you can be in a string of six of them with no bathroom break. Plan around it. I don't drink coffee right before work. And you're stuck with whoever is at your table, bad breath and all.

It's hard on your body if you do it wrong

You sit in one place for long stretches. I once tested working 55-hour weeks, and it was harder on my body than I wanted. How much pain you go home with depends a lot on how you do the job. Staying healthy as a poker dealer covers what I teach about it, and dealer burnout covers the money and mindset side.

Who it suits, and who it doesn't

It's a great fit if you play poker, if you're tired of traditional jobs, if you want to be earning in months instead of years, or if you're looking for a second career. People start in their 60s. Poker dealer requirements covers age and everything else that can keep you out.

It's probably not for you if:

  • You want easy money. Tables can have a lot of money on them, and you have to stay engaged, not just sling cards.
  • You're only in it for the money. Not everyone does, but people who do it only for the money can burn out within months. People who love it are happy working long weeks.
  • You can't take feedback or won't practice. I make a lot of corrections. In the industry you'll get corrected too.

More on both lists in how to become a poker dealer.

Dealing vs going to college

I got an electrical engineering degree from RPI because it was what I was supposed to do. Then I became a poker dealer anyway, and I paid off my student loans working in casinos. So for anyone going to college "just because," without a direction, here's my pitch: learn to deal, get the stable income college promises, and keep the money and the time to figure out what you really want. College still makes sense for careers like medicine or law.

Will machines replace poker dealers?

I don't think so, not soon. Most of a dealer's income is tips and the base pay is low, so poker dealers aren't where an AI business would start. There isn't enough money in poker to justify building and running it well.

And there's a bigger reason. Years ago on a cruise, I played 1-2 on a touchscreen table with no dealer. The technology worked fine. But with no one policing the game, players talked about their hands, two of them openly shared cards, and one player went wildly out of line, with no supervisor close enough to help. That's when I realized a dealer doesn't just need to exist. A dealer is also the referee, and has to be right there, all the time. I'd welcome tables that take over some of the work, like registering a check. But I think the human dealer, real cards and the social side keep dealers around.

So, is it a good job?

For me, yes. It paid my bills while I built other things, it gave me a social life and friends from the break room, and it's still there for me every time. But go in with your eyes open about the holidays and the lack of control. If you can live with those, the rest is a lot of upside.

Common questions about whether dealing poker is a good job

Is being a poker dealer a good job?

For the right person, yes. It pays well for a skill you can learn in months, you don't take work home, and the skill stays with you for life. The tradeoffs are nights, weekends and holidays, and little control over your schedule early on.

What is the worst part of being a poker dealer?

For me, the lack of control: you don't choose your table, your players or, at first, your hours, and you'll work holidays.

Is being a poker dealer stressful?

The first days are, because it's real money and everything is new. It takes a few months on the job before it's second nature, then it gets much easier. Supervisors handle the decisions, and you don't take the job home.

Is dealing poker a good part-time job?

Yes. Experienced dealers can work three or four days a week, pay the bills and use the rest of the week for something else. Many rooms let you pick up and give up shifts.

Do poker dealers work holidays?

Yes. Casinos are open when other people are off work, and newer dealers usually work the holidays.

Is it better to deal poker or play poker for a living?

For most people, dealing. A dealer leaves every down with more money than they sat down with, with no swings and no rake. I tried playing professionally around 2015 with too small a bankroll and got beat.

Will poker dealers be replaced by machines?

I don't think so, not soon. There isn't enough money in it, and a game with no dealer has no one at the table to enforce the rules.

Is dealing poker a good second career?

Yes. People start in their 60s. A friend of mine started dealing in his 60s, did it for about eight years and loved it.
