Talking to Players: Table Talk, Staying Neutral and Difficult Players

The number one reason dealers get fired isn't their mechanics. It's not knowing how to deal with people. Here's how I teach the human side of the job: what to say, what never to say, and how to handle the player who's making it hard.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 17, 2026 · 13 min read

In my experience, the number one reason poker dealers get fired is not knowing how to interact with people. Dealer schools rarely train it. They teach you to shuffle and pitch, and then you sit down in front of nine strangers who are trying to take each other's money. Players will test you. Part of this job is handling other people's insecurities.

It's also the best part of the job. As a supervisor in South Florida I used to wonder why regulars who lost kept coming back every day. My answer: a poker room guarantees social interaction. The dealer and the table will talk to you. Dealers who connect with people get tipped better and last longer, because once the dealing becomes repetitive, the people are what keep it rewarding.

How much should a dealer talk?

I don't believe in "shut up and deal" as a rule. Bring your personality, without crossing lines. But how much talk a room expects really varies:

  • It depends on the culture. Vegas and the West Coast lean "shut up and deal." In Texas and on the East Coast, players chat with you, and ignoring them is worse customer service. Some rooms want less interaction. Read the room you're in.
  • New dealers: deal first, talk later. Learn to deal very well before you add conversation, and keep your answers short. Let your personality come out a little at a time. If you could make a mistake while you're talking, you're not ready to talk.
  • Never when the action is on someone. Finish the chat and prompt the action right away. Stalling to chat at an audition is a big red flag.
  • Talk and deal. Keep your hands moving while you answer. A little chat to fill the silence during the shuffle is fine. Don't start long conversations.
  • Tournaments are different. Don't be chatty. The stakes are high and players are on a clock, so unless you really know the table, assume every moment is the wrong moment.
  • It's OK to not answer. One of the hardest spots is a touchy question while you're running the game. Sometimes the right answer is that there's too much going on to get into it right now.

When I sit down at a new table, I get right to dealing with "Hey everybody, how's it going?" and a little small talk. After that, my action announcements do most of the talking.

Bring your personality, not a script

When students first deal hands in my class, they instinctively give robotic answers. I teach them to bring their real personality instead. The goal is to resonate with the table: amplify the good vibe of the personalities at the table. Sometimes that means shut up and deal, and sometimes it means laughing at their jokes. You can only do this once your mechanics are automatic.

  • Bring the part of you people like. My style is to be friendly, savvy and good at the job, and to give players a hard time to build camaraderie, without ever crossing the line.
  • Playful only with rapport. Ribbing works with players you have rapport with. Don't do it with serious players.
  • Flair is fine, like a signature way of calling "all in," as long as it never affects the integrity of the game or slows it down.
  • Be authentic. I once found 10 to 20 inauthentic habits in myself as a dealer, including a thank-you that sounded fake. Now I find the place where I'm actually appreciative and say it from there.

Stay neutral: never take sides

This is the biggest change for people coming from table games. In blackjack, the math takes care of the house, so you can cheer for the player. In poker, players play each other. Rooting for one player is rooting against everyone else at the table.

  • Never say "good luck" or "nice hand." Some dealer schools teach "good luck, all in." I hate it.
  • Never predict a winner. No "whoever has a king probably wins."
  • Level out the emotion on a bad beat. Read the hands flat: "I see a set of kings, I see deuce-five for the straight," not "the set loses to the straight."
  • Don't get excited about a big pot. Announcing it with excitement marks you as inexperienced.
  • Keep strong opinions to yourself. I never share a strong sports opinion at the table. Nobody wants to tip the guy with the opinion.
  • Options and etiquette, not strategy. Telling a player they can fold, call or raise is fine. Advice on how to play the hand is not. Blackjack players are used to asking the dealer what to do, and that doesn't carry over to poker.
  • Stay out of side bets about the dealer. If players make up their own game at the table, you can't be the one reminding people of it.

When two players start going at each other, don't take a side either. "Guys, come on, please stop." Defuse it, and don't make it a bigger deal than it is.

Regulars: connection without crossing lines

Care about the players. Listen, and talk about what matters to them, between hands rather than during the action. A lot of players are there for the social side, and the person you've written off is a human being who may be choosing to talk to you.

  • Never name it. Give people connection, but never tell someone they seem lonely.
  • Learn names. The table tablet in a lot of rooms shows them. Greet people by name like you mean it, not like a robot.
  • Running jokes build camaraderie. The more hours I dealt, the more running jokes I built with regulars, and that camaraderie earns more. A player once accused me of a fake laugh, so later I did an obviously fake one and we both laughed.
  • Redirect, don't shut down. Enforce the rules 100%. But if a player brings up politics, gently redirect with a smile instead of creating more distance.
  • Keep boundaries. Experiment with connecting, and don't be afraid to fail at it, but don't get personal, and never at the expense of the game.

When players blame you for the cards

Players credit the dealer when they win, so the flip side is blame when they lose. Every pot has a loser, and the second-best hand will call you unlucky. I choose to go with it. Logic like "the cards are random" doesn't work. What does:

  • Be good at your job first. Polish your skills so they have nothing real to needle you on. The best way to keep players happy is reasonable speed, accuracy and talking your game.
  • Flood them with what you want. A regular at a room I worked in blamed every dealer for every bad card. I started answering her with warmth: "You know you love me." She ended up smiling, playing along and tipping me more than anyone.
  • Have a comeback with a smile. "You can blame me when they're bad, as long as you blame me when they're good."
  • Don't take it personally. Dealers are an easy target because they can't fire back. Picture what the player might be going through. They're losing money. The anger comes from them, not you.
  • Check yourself. If a little venting bothers you for more than a few seconds, ask why. Is it the tips? Your self-image?

Rude, angry and abusive players

Know before you start whether you can handle being insulted. If you can't, you'll lose your cool and lose your job. A player can only be kicked out. A dealer can be fired, so you can't cross the lines a player can. Here's how I handle it:

  • Don't take the bait. When one player says "That's 20, right?" to needle another, don't answer. Just spread the chips so the bet is clear. If they challenge you, clarifying an unclear bet is your job.
  • Never say "calm down." It gets under people's skin.
  • Get ahead of it. When voices and tone rise between players, step in every time: "Guys, you've got to stop it." As a supervisor, it bugged me to walk over to a blow-up and find out the dealer had said nothing.
  • Choose your words. "I think that's over the line" or "tone it down" works. "You can't say that" leaves you exposed if you're wrong about the rule. My line for player-to-player rudeness is "we don't talk to other players like that here."
  • A warning is just words. "We can't have that here" is a warning. Avoid "do that again and I'll call a supervisor," which creates tension for nothing.
  • Some things are always a floor call. Cursing at another player: call the floor, no exceptions. In many rooms, a player cursing at you is an automatic floor call too. Racism, sexism and anything illegal: address it and tell the supervisor, even if the room's culture has been letting it go. A player who flicks cards at you will do it to someone else, so call the floor. For casual cursing, a light "language, language, come on" usually works.
  • Take the high road, then tell someone. After the down, tell a supervisor if a player was making the table uncomfortable. Staff can act on that even without cursing. Never retaliate.
  • A player who's had too much. Quietly tell the floor the seat number. From there it's the supervisor's job.
  • Never refuse a tip to make a point. If someone upset you that much, handle it another way.

And try to win them over. Treat a rude player like a puzzle: what does this person actually want from the dealer? Every upset player is a chance to show them it isn't personal. An older friend who dealt as a second career had a great line for it, played as a joke: "Man, I'm trying to like you, but you're making it real hard." Build your own arsenal of lines like that.

What I correct in class

Too much commentary, too soon

Keep the commentary low at first and let your personality come out a little at a time, as your dealing gets automatic.

Chatting while the action is on someone

Finish the sentence and prompt the action. Stalling the game to talk is a big red flag at an audition.

Stopping your hands to talk

Talk and deal. Keep pitching, stacking and sweeping while you answer, so the game never waits on your conversation.

Apologizing for the cards

When a player says "goddamn dealers" after a bad beat, "yeah, sorry, man" is the wrong answer. You didn't do anything. Don't apologize for things you didn't do.

A lecturing tone

Explaining a rule shouldn't sound like school. Keep it simple and friendly, and move on.

When you make a mistake

You're going to make mistakes. Nobody's perfect, and no server at a restaurant you visit every week would be perfect forever either. What matters is how you talk about it.

  • Just name it. My best habit is simply saying what I did: "Oh, I missed it," or "Sorry, I thought that was a black chip, it's 25." That small bit of communication defuses players instantly.
  • Don't over-apologize. A quick "oh, sorry, I thought it was 150, it's 100" for a misstated amount is fine. For most small mistakes, just fix it. "I'm so sorry" feeds the frustration and suggests the business wronged them. Use appreciation instead: "I appreciate your patience." When you flip someone's ace, shrug it off kindly: "It was never meant to be yours."
  • Be human about being new. "I'm new, let me find out" or "it's my first time doing this" takes the edge off. When I came back to dealing after years away, I told tables I hadn't dealt in a while, and people responded to the honesty.
  • Thank the correction. When a player corrects you and they're right: "Thank you, appreciate that."
  • Don't dig in. If you stick to your guns when a player pushes back, even when you're right, you lose.

Explain the rule. Let the floor make the ruling.

A lot of table talk is explaining rules, and that's part of the job. A ruling, deciding what happens in a dispute, belongs to the floor supervisor. Here's how I keep those separate:

  • Make the rule about the room, not you. "There's a rule called one player per hand" feels inclusive, not scolding. When a player says other dealers let them do something, "this one's not up to me" levels with them honestly.
  • Resistance means floor. As soon as a player pushes back, call the floor and don't argue, even if you're sure you're right. If a player asks for the floor, always call it.
  • Never predict the ruling. Don't tell a player "the supervisor will say the same thing." If the floor goes the other way, you're stuck dealing to that player for the rest of your down. Say "let's find out what the floor says." Years ago I argued a rule with a player who said "I raise 65." Today I'd just get the floor.
  • Describe it neutrally. Tell the supervisor what happened and add nothing. Don't guess at intent ("he didn't mean to"). "I could be wrong, but this is what I remember." Once the floor is there, speak when they ask you to.

Phones, room complaints and other tricky moments

  • Two players on their phones in a hand. Don't accuse anyone. Something like: "I don't know if you know each other, but you're both on your phones at the same time. Our rule is no phones while you're in a hand." Then you've enforced the rule without calling anyone a cheater.
  • Your own phone should never be visible in the poker room, even between downs.
  • Asking a player to make room. Say the true thing, neutrally: "I just pitched where I normally do and your hand was there. The card almost flipped." If they won't move, work with them.
  • Complaints about the room, the promos or the waitress. Agreeing never works, and don't promise to fix anything. Giving them a bit of a hard time with the right energy usually defuses it.
  • The break room. Break rooms are full of venting. Some of that is healthy, but it can train you to be negative, and that shows at the table.

Practice this like you practice your pitch. Think of the situations that throw you, and prepare your line in your downtime, so you have it ready next time.

Common questions about talking to players

Should a poker dealer talk to players?

Yes, within limits. Short, friendly conversation between hands is good customer service in most rooms. Never talk when the action is on someone, keep it short when you're new, and talk less in tournaments and in rooms that prefer quiet dealers.

Can a poker dealer say "good luck"?

No. In poker the players play each other, so wishing one player luck is wishing everyone else bad luck. Stay neutral.

Can a dealer give a player advice?

A dealer can explain a player's options (fold, call or raise) and table etiquette, but not strategy.

What do you say when a player blames the dealer?

Don't argue and don't apologize for the cards. Keep it light, something like "you can blame me when they're bad, as long as you blame me when they're good," and don't take it personally.

What should a dealer do if a player is abusive?

If a player curses at another player, call the floor, and in many rooms the same goes for cursing at you. For lesser things, a simple "we can't have that here" is a warning. Never retaliate, and tell a supervisor if a player is making the table uncomfortable.

Do friendly dealers make more in tips?

In my experience, yes. Dealers who connect with players, without slowing the game or crossing lines, tend to get tipped better and enjoy the job longer.
