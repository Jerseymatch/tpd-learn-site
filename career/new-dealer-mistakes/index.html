Mistakes New Poker Dealers Make, and How to Recover at the Table

Every new dealer makes mistakes. I still make them. These are the ones I correct over and over in every class, why each one matters, the habit that fixes it, and what to do in the moment when something goes wrong.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 17, 2026 · 13 min read

You are going to make mistakes as a dealer. Everybody does. After you get hired there are something like 150 things left to learn, one at a time, and it gets easier with experience. When I came back to dealing after time away, I made about 20 mistakes in three days: I flipped at least three cards, misread two hands, and had the deck knocked out of my hand when a player reached for a chip at the same time I did. Nothing catastrophic. It happens. I still deal regularly, and I still make mistakes. Dealers are human, and poker has procedures for exactly this.

So the goal isn't to be perfect. It's to know which mistakes happen most, build the habits that prevent them, and know what to do in the moment when one slips through. These are the ones I see again and again in my classes, in every cohort.

1. Going too fast before you're ready

This is the big one. A lot of students speed up before they're ready because they believe they have to be fast to pass an audition. New dealers on the job try to match the speed of the experienced dealers around them, and the errors pour out. An audition is about being clean, not about being fast.

Dealing is like learning to drive. At first every step feels overwhelming. With repetition it becomes second nature, and rushing is how you crash. Deal properly and the speed will come.

  • Train one or two habits at a time. Don't try to get faster at everything at once.
  • Slow down in anything unfamiliar: a new game, a side pot, a double board. Slow is fine when it keeps the pot right and lets the players follow you.
  • Speed does matter eventually. Once some of my graduates were working next to experienced dealers, one was freezing up under player pressure. That's the one time I told a student to now focus on speed, and it made a huge difference. But that came after accuracy, not before.

2. Not verifying every bet you bring in

Verifying every amount that goes into the pot is one of the two most important things you do as a dealer. The other is taking the rake correctly. I repeat it in class all the time because students forget it.

Here's why. A raise to 20 becomes 27 because nobody verified it, and that alone can fail an audition. On the job, once an unverified bet is in the pot, it can be impossible to say what it was. I made this mistake myself once: I pushed a pot to the winner without collecting the call from the other player, and the floor couldn't tell how much it had been. I retrained myself to slow down in that exact spot.

  • Build the instinct to look at every bet the moment you bring it in. Not after. As it comes in.
  • Don't overdo it either. Re-checking every stack over and over is overkill and slows the game down. Verify once, correctly.

The mechanics of reading and breaking down bets are in Bets, Calls & Making Change and Counting Down Large Bets.

3. Forgetting the rake

Other than getting a pot wrong, the number one mistake I see in big pots is forgetting the rake. It's one of the hardest habits for new dealers to build. The hardest moment is a big betting round that turns into an all-in quickly: there's so much action that the rake gets skipped. In auditions I see it all the time, and supervisors always notice it, because the rake is how the room makes its money.

  • Touch the main pot, glance at the rake. If there's one thing to practice, it's this: every time you touch the main pot, glance at the rake slide before you move on. That habit can save you in an audition, and it can save your job.
  • Keep the rake current after the flop, turn and river, not only at the end.
  • If you missed it, fix it before the push. Missed it on the flop? Take it on the turn. If it's too late, tell the floor "I missed the rake." Never push the pot and hope nobody notices.

More on how the rake works in Rake, Promo Drop & Tips.

4. Losing track of the button

When I came back to dealing, there was a stretch where I had trouble remembering where the button was. It was embarrassing, and the reason was simple: I hadn't built the habit all the way into my instincts that the very next thing after pushing the pot is moving the button. Once I caught myself forgetting it, I made one airtight habit until I knew where the button was 100% of the time.

The order, every single hand:

  1. Push the pot.
  2. Move the button.
  3. Drop the rake, if there is one.
  4. Drop your tips.
  5. Clean up the board and go to the next hand.

Don't start cleaning up before the pot is pushed and the button has moved. There's so much going on at a poker table that you can't consciously remember everything. You need your instincts to do the remembering for you, and a fixed order is how you train them.

5. Forgetting to give change

Forgetting someone's change is common, even for experienced dealers. I've done it dozens of times. It's embarrassing, and it's normal. The habit that prevents most of it: take the chip you're changing into the bank (or bring it over to you) before you put the change out. A loose chip sitting on the felt is the one that ends up in the pot. The full procedure is in making change.

6. Flipping cards and covering the deck

Flipping cards is a common early habit, and it takes a long time to get rid of. I flipped a card here and there for a year or two. When it happens, don't make a big deal of it. A loud apology just calls attention to it. Follow the procedure in exposed cards and keep dealing.

The bigger issue is the habit underneath. If your cards flip toward seats 2 and 3, turn your palm up. And learn the pitch right the first time, because unlearning is brutal: once your body relies on a bad pitch for speed, fixing it on the job is a huge pain. How to Pitch Cards covers the mechanics.

One more card habit I untrain constantly: covering the top of the deck with your other hand after you pitch or while you square it. Well over half the students in my classes do it. It blocks surveillance's view of the deck, and it's really hard to pass an audition with it.

7. Trying to fix your own mistake

This is the one that gets new dealers in real trouble, and it comes from a good instinct. Everywhere else in life, when you make a mistake, you fix it. So in the moment, your human instincts kick in: "Oh, I made a mistake, let me just fix it." In a poker room, dealers don't fix their own mistakes. You call the floor.

One of the biggest reasons I've seen people fail auditions is not calling the floor: they mess up a side pot, try to fix it themselves, and make it worse. On the job, fixing your own mistakes is the quickest way for a new dealer to get fired. If a player says you made a mistake, someone at the table will probably tell the floor anyway, and hiding it is far more serious than whatever training or paperwork comes from calling.

  • Stop and call the floor. If you push a pot to the wrong player, stop everything as soon as you realize it and call a supervisor. The floor works out what happened, may check with surveillance, and makes the decision.
  • Call, don't "offer." When a player says you made a mistake, don't ask "do you want me to call the floor?" Call it.
  • Call even if you might be wrong. Players will try to wave you off with "don't worry about it." You're the one working. When the table sees you'll check on anything over the line, they stay away from it.
  • Never go into the muck to get a card back or check a hand.
  • Tell the floor only what happened. Facts, in order. Not your interpretation, not anyone's intent, and never a dramatic "I made a huge mistake." Once the floor is there, the decision is theirs; I cover how to make the call in When to Call the Floor.

There is a line here. If you catch it while you're still building that pot (say you skip a player and see it from the stacks right away), correcting it on the spot isn't a floor call. If you'd already moved on, you'd probably call the floor. And if you simply misstate a number out loud, just correct it instantly and keep going.

What I correct in class

Covering the deck

Squaring the deck under your other hand, or resting a hand over it after the pitch. Keep the deck visible. More than half my students need this untrained.

No glance at the rake

Big action, quick all-in, and the rake never gets taken. Touch the main pot, glance at the rake slide, every time.

Cleaning up before the button moves

Push, button, rake, tips, then clean up. In that order, every hand, until you never have to think about it.

8. Talking your way into mistakes

When something goes wrong while a dealer is chatting, the talking gets blamed, and it really does happen that way. Be social if that's you, but never while the action is going on. In one class audition, a student did a two-handed cut while talking, which is an instant problem in an audition. The fix in the moment: shake it off, shuffle the deck again, and say something like "Sorry, guys, one sec, old talking habit kicked in."

That's different from answering a player while you keep dealing, which I want you to do. Talk and deal; don't stop dealing to talk. How to talk about a mistake once it happens (name it, don't over-apologize, never apologize for the cards) is in Talking to Players.

9. Copying other dealers' shortcuts

When you start, you'll watch experienced dealers do things you never learned. If a dealer does something you've never heard of, don't assume it's right, and don't call them out either. Find out whether your room actually wants it.

Here's why. A dealer once mixed the stub, the burn cards and the muck together after the river, but only on boards where no bad beat was possible, because the room needed those cards verifiable to pay a bad beat. Another dealer copied the shortcut without understanding it and did it on boards where a bad beat was possible. That's the danger: a shortcut can undermine something you don't understand yet. It's why I teach no shortcuts for your first 6 to 12 months.

The flip side: when your supervisor wants something done differently from how I taught you, do it their way. Don't tell them "my class said to do it differently." I made that mistake in 2007, as a brand-new table games dealer. Your supervisors are right at your casino. Say "Oh, I'm sorry, I thought I was doing it right," and adjust.

10. Hold'em habits in other games

Once Hold'em is automatic, it follows you into every other game. The number one mistake dealing stud or draw is bringing a flop out of habit. The second is forgetting to burn. In Omaha, the Hold'em brain misreads hands that have to use exactly two hole cards. If you're not fully comfortable in a game, deal it slowly so your Hold'em instincts don't take over. The specifics are in Dealing Omaha and Stud, Draw & Mixed Games.

How to recover when something goes wrong

Mistakes are part of it, and the rules handle them. What separates good dealers is what they do next.

  • Don't snowball. Once you make one mistake, there's a tendency to make more, because you can't think straight. As a supervisor in a table games pit, I've watched a manager walk in and say a dealer was "snowballing out there." It happens to everybody. Get comfortable being uncomfortable, shake it off, and deal the rest of the hand well.
  • Own it. You're going to make mistakes, so humble yourself. An "I didn't make a mistake" attitude upsets players and costs you tips. When a player complains, humbly offer to get a supervisor and thank them for the feedback.
  • Lost track of the pot? Don't bluff. As a professional you should always know what's in the pot, and in an audition you need to. But in a live game, if you truly lose track, it's better to say "I don't know" and let the players help, or count it quickly, than to guess.
  • One thing at a time. Every shift, pick one thing to clean up until it's habit, then pick the next. When you get feedback, work on one or two corrections at a time, not ten.
  • Record yourself. Watching a recording of yourself deal is incredibly valuable. One viewer told me he was horrified by the little habits he saw on his. The best case is fixing your corrections from the recording before you deal again.
  • Rust is real. After time off, expect mistakes and slow down. Even an experienced dealer coming back can count a big all-in correctly and still announce it wrong.

How to practice so these don't follow you to work

Most of the mistakes on this page are habits, so practice the habits, not just the hands:

  • Deal full hands at home in the fixed order: verify each bet, touch the pot and glance at the rake, push, button, rake, tips, clean up.
  • Film yourself and watch for the deck in your hand: is it ever covered or tilted?
  • Practice the floor call out loud: "Floor, table seven," then a one-sentence, facts-only explanation.
  • Pick one correction per session and only one. How to practice dealing at home has the full method.

Common questions about new dealer mistakes

Do new poker dealers make a lot of mistakes?

Yes, every one of them. There are about 150 things left to learn after you're hired, and even experienced dealers forget change or misread a hand now and then. The rules exist because these things happen.

What should a poker dealer do after making a mistake?

If it affects the pot or the hand, stop and call the floor, and explain exactly what happened, facts only. If you just misspoke a number, correct it right away and keep going.

Can a poker dealer fix their own mistake?

No. The instinct is to fix it, but rooms don't let dealers fix their own mistakes; the floor handles it. The exception is catching it instantly while you're still building that pot, like a player you skipped a second ago.

Does one mistake fail a dealer audition?

Not usually. Rooms know when they hire anyone that mistakes might happen, even with experienced dealers, and a floor call in an audition doesn't fail you. A few things are automatic problems, like a two-handed cut or not taking the rake, so drill those. Handled well, it can make you memorable. It's about what you do next. More in How to Pass a Poker Dealer Audition.

What is the most common mistake new poker dealers make?

In my classes: going too fast, not verifying bets, forgetting the rake and losing track of the button. Trying to fix your own mistake instead of calling the floor is the one that causes the most trouble.

What happens if a dealer pushes the pot to the wrong player?

The dealer stops everything and calls a supervisor right away. The floor works out what happened, may confirm it with surveillance, and decides where the pot goes.
