Gambling and the Poker Dealer: EV, the House Edge and What I Teach Every Dealer

You're about to work in a building designed so the players lose. Gambling will be all around you, every shift, and it's compelling. Nobody at orientation explains how the business works, so I do, before my students ever go out to work.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 17, 2026 · 10 min read

Near the end of Remote Dealer School, I stop teaching dealing for a day and teach the math of gambling instead. I'd feel out of integrity training dealers without it. I'm sending people into an industry where gambling is going to be all around you, all the time, and it will be very compelling. I want the numbers to be armor for you.

I'm not anti-casino, and I'm not trying to stop anyone from gambling. That's impossible, and it's not what this is. When I started at a casino, orientation covered the property's history and where the gaming floor starts and stops. There are a thousand things they don't tell you, and one of them is how the business works. Nobody explains it, so I do.

EV in plain terms

"EV" (expected value) is poker talk. It's the average result of a decision if you made it over and over. Positive EV makes you money over time. Negative EV loses it. One reason I love poker is that you can win in the long run: it's a game of skill against other players, and anyone who's tried to beat the game seriously has heard the phrase "positive EV."

Poker is a game of decisions and opportunities. The cards are random, but they even out, so a player wins by maximizing the good spots and minimizing the bad ones. Casino games are different. They're a system you can't beat in the long run.

As a dealer, you don't need to be a great player, but you do need some insight into how poker is played. It helps you know what to prioritize at the table, and it helps you understand the people you're dealing to.

The house edge, in real numbers

In double-zero roulette, almost every bet has a 5.26% house edge. That means every $100 you bet is, in the long run, like handing the casino about $5.26, or about 52 cents every time you bet $10. Strategy in house games can't get you to even, let alone ahead. At the very best, people are figuring out how to lose less money.

Here's what that edge does over time, on roughly even-money roulette bets at about a spin a minute:

  • After about 16 hours, you have a 95% chance of being down for your lifetime.
  • After about 57 hours, a 1 in 1,000 chance of still being ahead.
  • After about 135 hours, 1 in a million.
  • After about 300 hours, a one in a trillion chance of still being ahead.

It doesn't matter if you bet $30 a spin or $100. The amount doesn't change the odds of still being ahead. Slot machines work the same way: at about six spins a minute on a high-variance machine, you're a 95% lifetime loser after about 105 hours, and at about 1,300 hours you have a one in a trillion chance of still being up. I think real play is closer to ten spins a minute.

Now flip it around. With a thousand players in the building, it takes the casino less than an hour to get to a one in a trillion chance of being down. That's the business you're walking into.

The irony: casinos are built on an edge, and the moment a player has one, they ask him to leave. The only way to beat house games is to play them in a way they weren't designed to be played. When I was a pit boss, I learned to count cards so I could spot counters.

How casinos judge every table: drop and hold

Every table game is a money-making machine on limited floor space. Casinos track the drop (the cash that goes in) and the hold (the percentage the table keeps). They keep the games that earn and replace the ones that don't.

Table games dealers are part of that system. When I dealt table games, I was trained to sell the games, make them fun and get excited when players won. The excitement makes players chase the feeling and bet bigger, and the better they feel, the more they tip. Nobody mentions the house edge. That's one reason I prefer poker: after about two and a half years in table games, it was hard for me to watch people lose all the time.

Why poker is different

In table games, you slowly lose to the house edge. In poker, the house doesn't play against you. It facilitates the game and takes a rake or a time charge, and the skilled players are the ones with the edge. In poker, the pros are the house.

  • Where the money goes. In a poker room, money goes three places: the room (rake or time), the employees (tips) and the winning players.
  • You have to beat the rake. The rake comes out of every pot, so to win in the long run a player has to beat the other players and the rake. It's hard to beat the rake. My estimate is maybe 10% of players, maybe 15%, maybe even less.
  • Poker makes the least money of anything in the casino. Casinos aren't motivated to turn slot or table games players into poker players.
  • The business is keeping the losing players happy. Gamblers lose in the long run, and the skilled players are the ones who might win. Keeping the recreational players happy is what brings everyone to the room.

That last point is your job description, too. More on how the rake is taken in Rake, Promo Drop & Tips.

Dealing in a room full of gambling

A viewer once asked me how to handle being around "the degeneracy." It's a real question, and I've sat with it for years. As a dealer, you facilitate a game where some people play for skill and many play for the quick fix. A lot of regulars treat poker like blackjack and hope to get lucky. Most of them don't win in the long run, especially against the rake.

  • You're part of the vibe. Players talk about their sports bets and baccarat stories, and in cash games you roll with things like a player going all in blind. Being stuffy hurts your tips.
  • The person you see as a degenerate is also human. A poker table guarantees social interaction, and some players are lonely. They may be choosing to talk to you.
  • Protect your own energy. If it's wearing on you, the answers are about hours and recharging. I cover them in Dealer Burnout.

Dealers who gamble

One of the toughest things about working in casinos has been watching coworkers gamble. When I worked table games, employees were allowed to play at the same casino. I watched some of my favorite coworkers lose their entire paycheck. Being around chips and cards all day had desensitized them to the bet sizes, and nobody educates anyone. I believe problem gambling is more common among table games dealers.

So if you play anything for money, ask yourself one question: do I have a statistical advantage over the other person? Someone said it to me recently as "always be the house." If you're a poker player who understands how to be the house, I think it's OK. A few more ways I think about it:

  • Poker has a bankroll. Gambling has a budget. A bankroll exists because poker can be beaten in the long run. A gambling budget is just the most you're willing to lose.
  • Why are you at the table? Poker is the only game in the casino you can treat like a competitive sport. Ask yourself whether you're there as a trained assassin or to escape.
  • Don't live like the players who tip you big. Some players lose thousands without noticing. That's recreation money for them, not a lifestyle for you to copy.

Can poker dealers play poker?

Yes, and dealers are great for games. They play all the time. But most rooms don't want you playing where you work, because of the risk of collusion. Some jurisdictions bar you from playing anywhere in the state. Some rooms do let employees play and hold them to a standard, and in rooms that allow it, I think it makes dealers care more about well-run games. Ask your room what its policy is.

Either way, how you act off duty matters. Some rooms let employees play and drink there, and I've seen dealers let go for causing trouble while playing at another property.

Me? I've played since about 2002, and I consider myself a winning player, not a pro. I rarely play after a dealing shift, because I hate losing more than I just made, so I'd need a separate bankroll for it.

The dealer's seat: earning inside a negative-EV building

Dealing is an underestimated way to have positive expected value inside a system built to make people broke. If you love poker rooms, consider dealing instead of playing: a dealer can't lose. You earn more or less in tips depending on the table, and that's the swing. Great tables, okay tables and occasionally bad ones, but you don't have a player's swings.

  • It makes you a better player without the risk. Learning 2-5 pot-limit Omaha as a player means risking real money before you know what you're doing. As a dealer, you sit in that game several times a day and watch how the pots are won.
  • Tips vary every day. If your expenses rise, you get emotional on the low days and start believing your best day is what you need. The dealers who get mad about tips are usually the ones who let their expenses match their income.

The money habits side of this, budgeting and the pull to spend as your income rises, is in Dealer Burnout. What tips look like is in Poker Dealer Pay & Tips.

Positive EV beyond the casino

Here's the bigger picture I give my students. Society gamifies negative-EV systems: casinos, sports betting, apps. We don't gamify the positive ones. We don't make it fun to put $5 into a long-term investment account. High-interest debt, digital addiction and spending work the same way, and time spent in those loops is time not spent building something.

If you're going to get addicted to something, get addicted to something that makes you money. The best way to build a healthy habit is to work with a professional in that area, which is why I bring a financial planner into every cohort of my school.

Common questions about dealers and gambling

Can poker dealers play poker where they work?

Usually not. Most rooms don't want you playing where you work because of collusion risk, and some jurisdictions bar it statewide. Some rooms allow it and hold employees to a standard. Ask your room.

Do casino dealers gamble?

Some do. I've watched table games coworkers lose whole paychecks at the casino we worked in. Being around chips all day desensitizes you, and nobody explains the business side, which is why I teach the math before my students start.

What does EV mean in poker?

Expected value: the average result of a decision if you repeated it many times. Positive EV makes you money over time. Negative EV loses it. Casino games are negative EV for the player by design.

Can you beat roulette or slots in the long run?

No. On even-money roulette bets, after about 300 hours you have about a one in a trillion chance of still being ahead. For slots it's about 1,300 hours. Strategy only slows down the losing.

How do poker rooms make money?

The room doesn't play against you. It takes a rake or a time charge. Money in a poker room goes to the room, the employees and the winning players. Poker makes the casino less than anything else on the floor.

Is being a poker dealer gambling?

No. A dealer can't lose. Your tips go up and down with the table, but you leave every down with more than you sat down with, and you're working in the one game in the casino that's built on skill.
