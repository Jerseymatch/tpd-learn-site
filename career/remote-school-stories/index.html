Remote Dealer School Student Stories: From Class to Dealing Real Games

People ask me all the time whether you can really learn to deal poker over Zoom. I'd rather let my graduates answer. Here's what they've told me on my channel, and what I've seen, about class, their auditions, their first shifts and dealing the WSOP Circuit.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 16, 2026 · 13 min read

When I started Remote Dealer School, a lot of people doubted that you could learn to deal poker from home. The idea came from a member of my self-paced course who told me they were renting an Airbnb for six weeks to attend an in-person dealer school. I'd been teaching people one-on-one, remotely, on YouTube for years. So why not run a whole school that way?

The best answer to whether it works is the people who've been through it. Several of my students and graduates have come on my channel to talk about it, unscripted. I'm not naming them here, but their stories are on my channel, in videos like the ones on this page. This one is two graduates of my summer class, recorded right after they dealt 11 to 12 straight days of the WSOP Circuit in Austin.

Who takes Remote Dealer School

Here's who has come through my classes:

  • People leaving corporate jobs. One graduate spent 22 years in the credit union industry, ending up in IT, with no dealing experience. Another spent most of his life in a corporate job he didn't enjoy and decided to deal because he loves the game. Two students this year came from corporate finance and accounting.
  • People who wanted a better work environment. One graduate was a meat cutter and motorcycle mechanic. He switched for more money and less wear on his body.
  • College students. One accounting graduate chose dealing instead. When his parents asked whether you can really live off it, working the job answered the question.
  • Second careers. One of my students got the idea at a home game full of dealers who were raising families on the job. I urged him to go to the WSOP, and he went in his 60s. I was blown away by how good he was compared to the other dealers there. He says it's the first job he's actually liked. I know a lot of people who love dealing as a second career.
  • Table games dealers. One graduate came from table games and had never played poker at all.
  • Working dealers. My July class included two dealers with three years' experience, including the WSOP, who kept making mistakes at the table that nobody had ever corrected.

Every class is recorded, so you can rewatch every hand and fix the corrections you'd never remember live. In Remote Dealer School you deal on camera and I correct you live.

Why they chose remote

For a lot of people the first reason is simple: in-person dealer schools only exist in a few cities. One student's nearest school was two and a half hours away. Without getting an apartment there for two months, remote was his option. By the end, he'd decided it was the best option.

Another reason: one graduate thinks an in-person instructor can only bounce around a room so much, and you'd leave with more questions. Remotely, with me dictating the hands, there was no disconnect. It's more one-on-one, not less.

And some people come back after trying it alone. One graduate learned from my material years ago, dealt a little without being fully prepared. He found it traumatizing, because he didn't know what he didn't know. This time he wanted an instructor telling him exactly what to work on.

What class is actually like

Here's how my graduates describe it.

  • Being on camera feels like being in the box. Both of the graduates in the video above said dealing on camera with me dictating the action was terrifying, and it never fully got easier. That's exactly why it translated so well to a real table.
  • You rewatch every hand. Later in the course, class is three hours of students dealing hands on Zoom, and I critique each one. One graduate rewatched every recording afterward, stopped at every mistake and drilled it until it was instinct. In a live school you can't remember and fix 15 corrections from one hand. With the video, you can.
  • You learn from everyone's hands. Watching classmates deal shows you situations that didn't come up in your own hands. Students deal along with each other's recorded hands, so they don't have to invent the action at home.
  • Missing a class is OK. Everything's recorded. One graduate listened in on his lunch break from another job and practiced classmates' hands at home later.
  • The pressure is on purpose. If you deal a hand cleanly under my dictated action, you get to keep that clip forever. Who wants a certificate that says you can deal, when you can have a video that shows you can? Students in my first class started asking me for "the hard one."

Around week four, I start shifting the responsibility onto the students. After you're hired there's usually a 90-day evaluation period and a lot to learn. If you rely on your boss to teach you, you might get fired. If you own it, you catch up and get better faster. I tell every class: be your own teacher. One graduate summed it up as his advice to new students: I'm your mentor, and you're your own teacher.

The story I tell every class

"I'm the worst one here"

One of my graduates from the very first class got hired as a tournament dealer, and the room's "training day" turned out to be them watching the new hires deal. He was nervous, went first and did poorly. Then he watched the others, who came from a local dealer school, and realized he was better than all of them. On his next turn he cruised, and the trainer had him teach everyone else how to cut chips with the index finger instead of counting them one at a time. I tell that story to every class so students stop thinking of themselves as the person making mistakes.

Their auditions

Every student auditions over and over as they learn, not just in a final week. I've run over 1,000 auditions, and the hands I give in class are in the top 1% of difficulty. That's why my students are often surprised at how easy the real audition is. The graduate in that training-day story said my mock auditions were significantly tougher than any real audition he's had.

Real auditions don't go perfectly, and they don't have to. One graduate went straight from the airport to an audition where five floor people sat in as players. He dealt no-limit, PLO and stud, including stud he'd never dealt before. He forgot to take the rake (a habit he'd lost dealing only tournaments) but caught it before pushing the pot. He nailed the PLO pot math and got hired. Because class had taught him he'd never be perfect, the mistakes didn't rattle him.

Another graduate expected her audition to be on a live table. Instead they took her to a training room, where she dealt 1/3 and 2/5 no-limit and limit games for roughly 40 minutes, with a hair tie standing in for the button. When I asked whether dealing on camera in class helped, she said 100%. For how to prepare, read how to pass a poker dealer audition.

Getting hired

  • An hour after the last class. In my first class, a college student who had never played live poker left the final class, went to a poker room, talked to the manager and got hired on the spot. He never got his perfect-hand clip in class. He was hired within the hour and texted me.
  • At the WSOP, with no experience. One graduate applied and tested but never heard back. A friend and I pushed him to go anyway. He went to the employment office, asked for someone from the WSOP and got hired by HR on the spot. He dealt four weeks of tournaments, including a main event day and a $5K six-max full of pros.
  • Off a bad-feeling audition. One graduate auditioned for a WSOP Circuit event and thought it went badly. At the end of the event, HR offered him a 90-day temporary tournament dealer job. A few months later he was being moved up to floor special events.

Every student from my first Remote School class ended up working as a poker dealer. After one later class, three weeks after the last session, everyone but one or two already had jobs. Getting hired depends a lot on where you live and how many rooms near you are hiring, so I can't promise anyone a job. But near the end of class, every student gets a plan for getting hired in the area where they want to work. For the bigger picture, read how to become a poker dealer.

Their first shifts

Every hired student so far has told me the same thing: dealing to real players is easier than dealing alone at home. In class the action moves faster than a real game, with no tanking. At a real PLO table, two of my graduates found they had the pot figured out before the player even acted. And they didn't have to restack all the chips after every hand.

That doesn't mean the first week is easy. Here's what they told me:

  • The first hand is the hardest. One graduate wanted to watch for half an hour before her first down. Once she was on a table, the first hand was hard, and after the rush of nerves it was fine.
  • There's a lot to follow at first. One graduate realized on day two that he was so focused on the rake and the bank that he wasn't following the action. Day three was better than day one. Another made two $5 bank mistakes in her first two days and none since, because now she counts the bank every time she taps in.
  • Class prepares you for floor calls. One graduate's real floor calls included a string bet and a player who put out a call, pulled his hand back and said he meant to raise. She ruled it a call and called the floor, and the floor agreed. She said practicing floor calls in class helped.
  • For one graduate, the nerves lasted about 100 hours. He started each day anxious and eased up by midday. About two-thirds of the way through the series he stopped panicking, and by day 12 he was excited to go in. He stopped sweating, and his hands stopped sticking to the cards.
  • Keep practicing after class. One graduate had a few weeks between school and her first shift and wishes she'd kept practicing every day, because her first two weeks would have been easier. After 6 to 12 months in the industry your skills stay with you, but a brand-new graduate's skills fade without practice. Now coworkers tell her she went from super fresh to a pro.

One more graduate said it went more smoothly than he expected. Once the mechanics are second nature, the job is keeping people happy and moving chips. More on what the job is like in a day in the life of a poker dealer.

The WSOP Circuit in Austin

My two summer classes, in June and July, were timed for the WSOP Circuit at Texas Card House Austin. Eight of those students dealt the circuit, and I dealt it with them. It was the first time I'd trained dealers over Zoom and then watched them deal in a real room, on both day and night shift. Austin was one of the hardest places in the country to start dealing.

That's why I use hard hands in class. A lot of new dealers start at the summer WSOP, dealing hold'em tournaments all day. At the Austin circuit, the room pushes dealers to wherever they're needed. A new dealer can land in a complicated PLO cash game, about one time in five. One graduate's first table on day one was the wildest game in the room: fast pot-limit Omaha with straddles and short stacks going all in. He slowed down and got through it, and players won't remember you once you leave the table. Without the pressure of class, he says, he would have made a catastrophic mistake. Still, he says that first day was hell: he couldn't keep up with the pot while short stacks went all in and pots got quartered. I went with him to the dealer coordinator to get him off pot-limit games for a while, and by the end of the series he was looking forward to those tables. A few students struggled like that for a couple of days, and then it was smooth.

The other graduate in the video above knew pressure was his weakness, so he drilled multiplication flash cards on every break, especially before a PLO table: 12, 15, 25 and so on up to 95, times 2 through 9. Other dealers started using his app. That's a perfect example of being your own teacher. For the math itself, read how to build side pots and split pots and quartering.

What graduates tell new students (and what I add)

  • Be coachable and ready to make mistakes. That's my graduates' advice. One put it this way: don't chase perfection in class. Let your flaws come out so you can fix them. I tell every class the same: class is the place to find your mistakes, not to be perfect on camera, and you're not competing with your classmates. The intensity is intentional, so you have a reference point when real games get intense.
  • Ask yourself whether it feels like poker, and where the action is.
  • Sleep on it. One graduate found the PLO math much easier after a break. I always stress sleep: fine motor skills smooth out while you sleep, so you're often better the next day.
  • Get a practice table. I tell everyone this, and it doesn't cost much. One graduate's table from Amazon was under $200, and mine was around $100 to $150 and took a couple of hours to put together. Some students use a dining table with a felt, a little padding underneath and towels around the edge.

Common questions about Remote Dealer School

Can you really learn to deal poker remotely?

Yes. My students deal at home on camera while I dictate the action and correct them live, and every class is recorded so they can rewatch their hands. Every hired student so far has said dealing to real players is easier than class.

How long is Remote Dealer School?

8 weeks of live classes on Zoom. Near the end, you get a plan for getting hired in the area where you want to work.

Do Remote Dealer School graduates get hired?

Every student from my first class ended up working as a poker dealer, and after a later class everyone but one or two had jobs within three weeks. It depends on how many rooms near you are hiring, so I can't promise anyone a job.

Do I need poker experience to take Remote Dealer School?

No. Graduates have included a college student who had never played live poker and a table games dealer who had never played poker at all.

What if I miss a class?

Every class is recorded. Students who miss one catch up on the recording, and graduates keep access to the class recordings after the course.

Is remote dealer school better than an in-person school?

My graduates think so: it's more one-on-one, you get video of every hand you deal, and you don't have to move to one of the few cities with an in-person school.
