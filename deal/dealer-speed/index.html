How Fast Should a Poker Dealer Be? Hands per Hour and How to Get Faster

Every poker room wants fast dealers, and every new dealer feels the pressure to be one. Here's what fast actually means, how rooms measure it, and where speed really comes from. Hint: it isn't only about moving your hands faster.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 20, 2026 · 13 min read

"How fast should I be?" is one of the questions I get asked the most. It makes sense. In a raked cash game, more hands means more money for the house, more poker for the players and more chances for you to get tipped. Speed matters.

But speed is the last thing a new dealer should chase. The dealers who try to go fast before they're ready are the ones who make mistakes. It's better to be a little slow and do it right. So this guide covers both sides: what fast looks like, and how you actually get there.

The short answer

  • About 15 hands per 30-minute down is what a room hopes for at low-limit no-limit hold'em, like 1-2. 18 or more is fast. 20 or more is excellent.
  • A "hand" means a flop. The industry measures speed by flops dealt, so a hand that ends preflop doesn't really count.
  • It depends on the game and the players. A limit game like 2-4 can get a lot more hands out than pot-limit Omaha.
  • Accuracy comes first. An audition is about being clean, not fast. Your speed builds over your first months on the job.
  • Speed is hand skills plus cutting downtime. Clean, efficient mechanics matter, but so does the gap between pushing a pot and starting the next shuffle.

How many hands per down is good

Dealers usually work 30-minute downs. At 1-2 no-limit, a casino is hoping for about 15 hands in that down. That's average. A fast dealer gets 18 or more, and 20 or more is excellent. A lot of that depends on the players, though.

The way the industry counts it is flops. If you're dealing hand after hand where everyone folds to the big blind, you're shuffling and pitching a lot, but those don't count the same as a hand that goes to the flop and gets raked.

I once had a dealer tell me he got 30-plus hands a down. So I counted him. He got 21. That's still really impressive.

How rooms count your hands

When I was a shift manager, we had a system where the dealer swiped in at the table and a sensor in the rake slide counted every hand that got raked. So the room knew exactly how many hands each dealer got out. The highest I ever saw a dealer sustain was around 46 or 47 hands an hour, and getting there takes every shortcut in the book.

I've also counted dealers' hands by hand, for evaluations. It's fun to ask a dealer to guess their number first. Most people have no idea.

Why speed matters (and where it matters less)

In a raked cash game, the number of hands decides how much the room makes. That's why cash games demand that you crank out hands. When you learn the pitch correctly, you get to deal more hands per hour, and that's good for you, good for the house and good for the players. Everybody's interests line up.

For you, it's simple: you're tipped hand by hand, so every hand you get out is another chance at a tip. Players also want a pace that keeps them in the flow of the game.

The hand you squeeze in right before your break can matter too. If you start the next hand ten seconds earlier because you didn't waste any motion, sometimes that's the difference between dealing one more hand before the next dealer taps you out, and that one extra hand adds up over a year.

Tournaments are different. The blinds keep going up no matter how fast the dealer is, so even slow dealers finish on time. In a tournament, accuracy and focus matter more than squeezing out one more hand. Players wait hours to play a hand, and if you ruin it, it's brutal for them.

Accuracy first, speed second

A lot of students make the mistake of going too fast before they're ready, because they believe they have to be fast to pass an audition. That's a false perception. You can't be insanely slow, but there's leeway. What you want is to do everything correctly, so that becomes your foundation, and then speed yourself up within your means.

When I'm evaluating somebody, it's about being clean, not about being fast. There's a baseline that's just good enough to get in the door and start learning, and poker rooms understand that baseline pretty well. If you passed an audition, you're at that baseline speed.

Then expect to be slower than the veterans for a while. Getting hands out fast is the dream, but it's going to be hard in your first six months. New dealers on their first job try to match the speed of the experienced dealers around them, and the errors pour out. Learn the job, learn it well, and the speed will come.

When to slow down on purpose

Even experienced dealers slow down for certain moments, because some parts of the game have to happen at a pace players can follow:

  • Side pots and multi-way all-ins. If there are two or three players and it isn't complicated, you can go a little faster. But when I'm building side pots, I slow down so the communication is clear and everybody has a chance to follow what's happening, without ever running them over. See side pots.
  • A disputed showdown. If a player questions the result, slow down, frame the hands clearly and make sure they 100% understand what happened. See the showdown.
  • A game you're new to. When you're new to a game, go slow. In draw games, forgetting the burn is one of the easiest mistakes to make, and rushing is how it happens.
  • Pot math under pressure. If the numbers aren't adding up, stop and recalculate step by step. Guessing fast is worse than counting right.

The one time I told a student to focus on speed

In class, my rule is get everything right and the speed will come. But once some of my graduates were working next to experienced dealers, one of them was freezing up when players put pressure on him. That's the one time I told a student that now it was time to focus on speed. It made a huge difference. But that came after he was accurate, not before.

Where speed really comes from: killing downtime

Being fast is a combination of two things: your hand skills, like the shuffle and the pitch, and knowing how to cut downtime during the game. From the moment you push the pot to the moment you start the next shuffle should be very quick, a few seconds, and that includes dropping the rake and clearing the board.

A good dealer school teaches you where your focus should be at every moment. What I see in students is that they stop after the last riffle, or after pitching a couple of cards, to chat. I would never stop to talk with two cards out unless it was vital. Whenever there's downtime, you kill it.

One exception: if a player is thinking, or might be thinking, don't tell them it's their turn. That's needling them. Prompt a player who doesn't realize the action is on them ("action's on you"), but never one who's thinking.

Habits that save time every hand

  1. Bring the next card, then fix the pot. If you bring the bets in, tidy the pot a couple of times and then burn and turn, the players are waiting on you. Bring the card first so the action can continue, and fix the pot after.
  2. Make change during downtime. While players are deciding, you can anticipate the change you'll need, organize the pot and pre-stack chips, instead of waiting until it's needed. See making change.
  3. Never straighten your bank between hands. Organize it during the hand, while you're still following the action, never in the gap before the next deal. See working the bank.
  4. Count bets as you collect them. Don't pause to count the players first and then bring the chips in.
  5. Pull the burn cards into the muck while you push the pot. Two jobs, one motion.
  6. In a cash game, start pitching. Don't wait for the blinds to post before you deal. In a tournament, wait for the antes.
  7. Use the math shortcuts. Some rooms, and some supervisors especially, don't want dealers adding up a pot one spot at a time. Say it out loud as multiplication: three, four, five times 150. Sometimes just looking at the stacked chips is faster than doing any math. See counting bets.
  8. Read hands instantly in hold'em. In Omaha, players understand it can take a second. In hold'em, dealers can't be slow at figuring out what beats what.
  9. Talk without slowing down. When dealers talk, they almost always get choppy and slow with their hands. If you can multitask and keep the pace up, great. If not, the conversation waits. See talking to players.

Experienced dealers find even more shortcuts than these. The best way to find them is to watch good dealers, then watch yourself.

Cut the wasted motions

The other half is mechanics. Most of it is tiny motions that cost a second or two each, every single hand:

  • The wash should be quick from day one. Unlike the shuffle and the pitch, the wash is something you train to do quickly right from the start. It should feel like poker, with some energy, not like a slow classroom motion. And pick the cards up in one motion at the end, not in sections. Gathering them in sections costs five or six seconds every time.
  • Keep the deck in your hands. Putting the deck down and picking it back up between shuffle steps is a speed cost over time.
  • Get the deck to your hand in one motion. When you cut, square the deck with your fingers as you take it, so it goes to your deck hand in one motion. Squaring it after the cut should be so fast nobody would notice you did it.
  • Bring your deck hand to where you're going. When you burn and bring the flop, it's quicker to take cards off a deck that's down near the board than one that's up in the air.
  • Cut chips toward your elbow, with your index finger. The chips should move in the same direction as your arm. Thumb cutting is how poker players do it, and it limits your speed. See how to cut chips.

What I correct in class

A slow wash

I tell students to wash briskly, because I've learned that if I don't say it, people just keep washing slowly. A slow wash makes the whole table wait, and a slow dealer can hurt an audition.

The extra scoop on the cut

Two students in one class were both cutting the deck and then scooping it with an extra motion. It's a second and a half to two seconds each time. Over a 30-minute down, that's 12 to 15 seconds, and 12 to 15 seconds can be the difference between getting one more hand or not.

Fixing the pot before the turn

A student brought the bets in and fixed up the pot a couple of times before bringing the turn. Bring the turn, then fix the pot. Do your required job and keep the action moving.

Speeding up before you're ready

The opposite problem is just as common. Students rush because they think they have to, and the mistakes come with it. When a student's shuffle looks rigid and jerky, like they're rushing, I'd rather see it smooth, even if it's slightly slower. Get the motion smooth first, then work on going faster.

How to get faster: a practice plan

  1. Work on one thing at a time. Every shift, pick one thing to speed up or clean up. When I went back to dealing, I sometimes forgot to move the button. So I built an airtight habit until I knew where it was 100% of the time. Then I moved on to the next thing.
  2. Watch where you slow down. Each down, notice the spots where you lose time, and build small habits to fix them. A lot of these are specific to your room: its rake, its bank, its layout.
  3. Time yourself. From the wash to getting the cards out to every seat, time yourself, then try to get about 20% faster. For hold'em, under 30 seconds to get all the cards out would be really good. The fastest dealer I ever challenged took about 26 seconds to shuffle and get four cards to nine seats in Omaha.
  4. Push a little past comfortable. Once your mechanics are clean, practice going slightly faster than feels comfortable, without giving up accuracy. That's how you build speed that holds up under pressure.
  5. Shorten your motions. Practice the pitch at home and make the motion smaller. Your technique sets the ceiling on your speed, so if something in your mechanics is capping you, sometimes you have to slow down to fix it first. See how to pitch cards.
  6. Memorize the math. Speed comes from memorization. The first time I had to work out 15 times 9 for a bomb pot, it felt hard. After dealing a bunch of them, 135 is just memorized. The less you have to think, the more energy you have for speed.

Rooms that can pick and choose their dealers may set a speed test, like washing, shuffling and pitching to nine seats in 25 seconds. It's doable with practice, but it's fast. My normal pace is fine for most rooms.

Common questions about dealer speed

How many hands per hour does a poker dealer deal?

At low-limit no-limit hold'em, about 15 hands per 30-minute down is average, so roughly 30 an hour. A fast dealer gets 18 or more per down, and 20 or more is excellent. The most I ever saw a dealer sustain was about 46 or 47 an hour.

Do you have to be fast to pass a poker dealer audition?

No. An audition is about being clean, not fast. You can't be insanely slow, but there's leeway. Rooms know a new dealer is at a baseline speed. See how to pass a poker dealer audition.

How long does it take to get faster as a poker dealer?

Expect your first six months on the job to be slower than the experienced dealers around you. Learn the job well first, and the speed comes with it. See how long it takes to learn to deal.

Do casinos track how many hands a dealer deals?

Some do. When I was a shift manager, a sensor in the rake slide counted every raked hand for each dealer. Supervisors also count hands in a down for evaluations.

Do faster poker dealers make more money?

In a cash game, usually yes, because you're tipped hand by hand, so every extra hand is another chance at a tip. But speed that comes at the cost of mistakes isn't okay. See poker dealer pay and tips.

How fast should a poker dealer shuffle and pitch?

For hold'em, under 30 seconds to get all the cards out would be really good. A room that can be picky might want something like 25 seconds. A normal, clean pace is fine for most rooms.

Do shuffle machines speed up a poker game?

Manufacturers claim a couple more hands an hour. When the machine is down, you lose some of that, but it's still faster overall. Their biggest value is integrity: the dealer isn't shuffling, so manipulation is very hard.
