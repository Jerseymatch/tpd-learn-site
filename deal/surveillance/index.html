How Casinos Catch Cheating at Poker (and What Surveillance Watches Dealers For)

As a shift manager I sat in on a lot of surveillance reviews, and I've done two livestreams with magicians on whether they could beat the cameras. Here's how cheating at poker gets caught, why dealing seconds doesn't work in a casino, and what surveillance is watching you for every shift.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 18, 2026 · 13 min read

I love casino surveillance. It protects the casino, it protects the players, and it protects you as the dealer. If a player assaults a dealer, it's on tape. If a player wins big, the win can be verified. And because the people in surveillance understand the procedure, a dealer has a very hard time doing anything shady.

This page is about the cheating side: how players try to cheat at poker, how it gets caught, why a dealer can't get away with it, and what surveillance is watching dealers for on every shift. The habits you use to protect the game yourself are in game protection.

The short answer

  • The cameras. Most poker tables have about three HD cameras on them, and most places require at least three. Surveillance can rewind, slow the footage down and zoom way in.
  • The procedure. Everything a dealer does is done in plain view, so if something goes wrong, the footage can show exactly what happened.
  • The numbers. Supervisors know what every dealer tips out. A dealer far outside the norm gets looked at.
  • The players. Cheating often gets reported by another player. Then surveillance reviews it.
  • The consequences. A player caught cheating can be banned for 24 hours or for life. A dealer caught cheating can face charges and never deal again.

What the cameras can see

Most poker tables have about three cameras with HD zoom and slow motion. Once surveillance has a reason to look, it can rewind, slow a hand down and zoom in on your hands from more than one angle. A move that looks perfect at full speed can be obvious at a quarter speed.

That's also why casino procedure looks the way it does. The biggest difference between a home game and a card room is that in a card room, everything is done transparently. Players who have no friends at the table need that security. If anything goes wrong, the footage has to be able to show it.

Even the cards are built for it. Casino cards have a white border on the back, and one of my magician guests showed me why. Without a border, even a cheat who hasn't practiced very long can make a second deal or a bottom deal look professional. With a border, the eye (or the camera) can catch a split-second flash of white where it shouldn't be.

How players try to cheat, and how it gets caught

Marked and bent cards

Players have tried marking cards forever. Casino cards are now coated to make marking hard, and the cameras catch it. A burn card also protects against someone who has marked the top card. Bent cards are the more common problem: players bend cards when they squeeze them, and a crimped corner can be visible from across the table and tell someone what the next card is.

Here's how I'd handle it as a dealer. Keep two decks of different colors in rotation, and check the backs when you switch. A lot of cards with the same fingernail mark aren't really distinguishable from each other. But if one card is identifiable, take the whole deck out of play, replace it and call the floor. The supervisor checks the rest of the deck, surveillance reviews the time window, and someone damaging casino property is asked to leave.

When you get a new deck, spread it face down and check every back, in the glare of the light, before it goes into play. Old shuffle machines sometimes left a half-moon mark that looked like a nail mark. If every card has it, it's no concern. More on deck changes is in game protection.

Swapping in folded cards

One cheat I reviewed as a supervisor wasn't sophisticated at all. A player folded and left. The player next to him took those folded cards, mixed them with his own and kept the best two. The new dealer hadn't noticed. Another player reported it, I reviewed it in surveillance, and the room banned the player for life. The dealer got into significant trouble for not noticing.

That's why protecting the muck and the folded cards matters so much. (How to do it is in protecting the muck.)

Adding chips in the middle of a hand

As a shift manager in Florida, I investigated on surveillance many times and confirmed players adding chips to their stack in the middle of a hand. In one case, a player with a strong hand added chips from a pocket mid-hand. The room recovered the winnings, returned them and banned the player.

Collusion

Cameras, removing marked cards and experienced players all make cheating much harder in a card room than in a home game. In more than 10 years, I kicked players out for collusion once, in Florida: two players working together to build pots against a third. The pot was given to the player they were cheating.

Then there are high-hand promotions. As a supervisor I disqualified at least 10 high hands, using surveillance review. The usual reason was apparent collusion, and in one room the most common version was players stalling on every street so their high hand would land in the next period. As a dealer, you tell them they can't stall, even if it costs you the tip.

Phones are the modern version. I've never seen players meaningfully cheat with phones, but about 15 times I suspected two players were texting each other during hands. What to say when that happens is in protecting the game at the table.

Are dealers trained to spot cheating?

Yes, especially in blackjack, where it's the house's money. In poker it's the players' money, but you're still responsible for everything on the table. If you see someone add chips mid-hand or mark a card, you report it. You call the floor, tell the supervisor what you saw, and let the supervisor and surveillance take it from there. (How to make that call is in when to call the floor.)

Can a poker dealer cheat?

Dealing seconds is real. I've shown a slow-motion clip of a magician friend of mine, who also dealt, pitching the second card off the deck. I can't do it convincingly myself. But surveillance can watch it in slow motion too, the tape gets reviewed on any complaint, and getting caught means charges and never dealing again. You only get one shot at a career. Cheating as a dealer is a terrible risk.

I did two livestreams with magicians, and my magician guests showed me move after move. Asked whether a skilled magician could beat casino surveillance, one of them said he probably couldn't, between the nerves, players watching from every direction and the cameras. I framed the series as a tool for supervisors, to learn what to look for. And almost every move they showed is something casino procedure already stops:

  • Peeking the top card. While saying "two to call" and moving a chip, a dealer can tilt the deck slightly and see the top card from his own angle. At speed it happens in an instant. Keep the deck flat and close to the felt.
  • An unsquared cut. A one-handed cut that leaves one card sticking out a little looks like the normal sloppy cut dealers do all the time. That jogged card marks where the bottom cards went, so a cheat can hold a break and deal known cards from the middle. Cut and square the deck completely.
  • Squaring the deck facing you. If a dealer sees cards on the bottom while scooping and squaring, a crooked dealer who knows where good cards are can use sleight of hand to change the outcome. That's why you square the deck facing away from yourself.
  • Lapping. Magicians hide cards in their lap, just under the edge of the table. The casino rule that answers it: no card ever goes below the visible height of the table rail. Dealers wear flat aprons, too.
  • The second deal. On a fair deal, the thumb lifts between cards. A second dealer's thumb stays on the deck.

The deck-handling rules behind these are in protecting the deck and how to shuffle cards.

What surveillance watches dealers for

Surveillance isn't only watching players. It watches you, every shift, and most of what it's looking for is whether a chip could have gone somewhere it shouldn't.

Your tips

When a player tips you, take it in your fingertips, tap it on the table or the corner of the bank, and drop it in the tip box. Nothing else. The tip box is locked, and at the end of the shift it's counted out in a clear bin. Only what's in the box gets tipped out. (The full procedure is in rake, promo drop and tips.)

Supervisors know what every dealer tips out. A dealer whose numbers are far outside the norm gets loosely watched, and then surveillance can zoom in on that dealer's whole shift. Complaints can trigger it too. I know of a dealer who was caught taking a red chip out of every pot. I also once reviewed a dealer with an unusual number of $2 chips, and he turned out to be completely legitimate.

The best example I know of is a dealer I know who used to be a magician. Every time a player tipped him, he showed the chip to the camera like he was supposed to, said thank you, then made it vanish or appear in his other hand before tapping it and dropping it in the box. Players loved it. Surveillance handed him 40 write-ups in one week, one for every time they couldn't follow where the tip went. They had clearly been watching him for a while to build that packet. In most casinos, that would probably have been the end of his career. Tip handling has to be visible to the camera every single time.

Your chips

Chips stay in your fingertips, never your palm. Chips moving toward your palm get you told to stop, or investigated. This matters most with $100 chips, because those are the most likely chips to be palmed into a tip box or a pocket. Splash bets and change so the amount can be read, and clear your hands after you deliver chips. All of it is in how to handle chips and how to cut chips.

Your hands and the deck

Keep your hands above the rail, don't cover the top of the deck, and don't cross your hands or arms over it, because surveillance can't see the deck through them. If you have to touch your face or reach below the rail, clear your hands before and after. Spread the cards at showdown so surveillance can see the pips and tell what they are. These are in clear your hands.

What I correct in class

  • Picking up chips in the palm. I have to say this in every class. Fingertips, always, and especially with black chips.
  • Tapping at the wrong time. Tapping the table tells surveillance you're putting a tip in your box. A student once asked if you tap before dropping the rake. No. If you tap for the rake, surveillance is going to be very suspicious.
  • Forgetting to tap at all. The tap on the corner of the bank is a visual and audio cue for surveillance that money is going into the tip box.
  • Chips the camera can't read. Looking down from above, there's no depth perception. If you stack red chips on top of a green one, the board can look clear, but nobody can tell how many chips are there. Keep a spacer on top of any short stack so surveillance and the next dealer know it's a short stack, and don't restack a splashed all-in into something that looks like a full stack.

How a surveillance review works

As a supervisor and shift manager I reviewed footage many times. Here's how it worked for me:

  1. Call surveillance first with what you need: the table and the time window.
  2. You're signed in and walked to a separate viewing room. Never the real surveillance room.
  3. A surveillance employee replays it from the different angles, with the footage already pulled up.

The reason they bring in a poker person is that surveillance staff often don't know poker. I've talked to someone in surveillance who didn't know what a flop is. They need someone who understands what's supposed to happen to tell them what actually happened. Surveillance also isn't supposed to be friends with the people it watches, so it's kept separate from the poker department.

And the review isn't only about cheating. Say a dealer grabbed a hand that wasn't protected because he thought it was folded, and the player notices a street later. Sometimes you go to the cameras, because with people's money you have to get it right. As the dealer, your job is to call the floor and describe what happened. The supervisor decides what to do with it. (More in when a dealer should call the floor and showdown disputes.)

Why a casino game is safer than a home game

Between the cameras, the procedure and experienced players at the table, a casino is a terrible place to try it. Shuffle machines make card cheating harder too. Home games are the scary place. In a home game, nobody is reviewing the footage, and friends just sort it out among themselves.

Gaming regulations are written with the players in mind, too. I once read through a state's roughly 300 pages of gaming regulations, and the intent includes making sure games are fair for the players, not just protecting the casino's money.

Common questions about poker cheating and surveillance

How do casinos catch poker cheaters?

With cameras that can rewind, slow down and zoom in, procedure that keeps everything in view, tip-out numbers that show when something's off, and players who report what they see. Once there's a reason to look, surveillance reviews the footage.

How many cameras are on a poker table?

Usually about three HD cameras per table, and most places require at least three.

Can a poker dealer cheat?

A dealer can learn moves like dealing seconds, but surveillance can watch it in slow motion, and getting caught means charges and never dealing again. Casino procedure is built to stop those moves.

Are poker dealers trained to spot cheating?

Yes. In poker it's not the house's money, but the dealer is responsible for the integrity of the game and reports anyone adding chips mid-hand or marking cards.

What happens if you get caught cheating at a casino poker table?

A player can be asked to leave and banned, from 24 hours up to life, and the room can take back what was won. A dealer can lose the job, face charges and never deal again.

Why do poker dealers tap the table before dropping a tip?

The tap is a visual and audio cue to surveillance that money is going into the tip box. That's why you never tap when you drop the rake.

Do poker dealers steal chips?

It's very hard to get away with. Chips stay in your fingertips, tips are tapped and dropped in view, and a dealer whose tip-outs are far outside the norm gets reviewed.

Can players mark cards in a casino?

They try, but casino cards are coated to make it hard, and cameras catch it. If a card is identifiable, the dealer takes the deck out of play and calls the floor.
