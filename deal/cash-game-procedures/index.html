Cash Game Procedures

In a cash game, each hand stands alone. That one idea explains almost every cash game rule: buy-in limits, who gets dealt in, missed blinds, why you can't take chips off the table, and why tips work the way they do.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 15, 2026 · 20 min read

Cash games have their own logic. In a cash game, every chip on the table represents real money. A player gives the cashier $300, brings $300 in chips to the table, and plays. If they win a hand and they're up to $600, they can leave. We advise people to play longer than that for etiquette reasons, but that's how it works. Nobody is ever committed to play any hand other than the one they're in.

None of this is going to be on an audition, and it isn't usually taught at this stage of dealer school. But I can't tell you how many dealers I've seen go through school without hearing any of it. They don't realize when they're going outside the etiquette, and they say things that confuse players. Dealers who understand these concepts show up with a savvy and awareness that casinos really appreciate.

The big idea: each hand stands alone

From the dealer's point of view, every hand of a cash game is its own game. When a hand is over, anybody who doesn't want to play anymore can leave. A new player who hasn't had anything to do with the table can sit down and join. Players can change their minds about leaving whenever they want, without penalty. Because of that, we don't force anyone to be dealt in.

The hands are still connected, though. The button moves, so every player has a different position every hand. And some rules exist specifically to tie the hands together so the game stays fair. You'll see both sides of that in everything below.

A word on terminology. I'm explaining a concept here. Don't walk into a casino and say "each hand is a new game." To them, a new game means something else, like a new table opening. Think of it as "each hand stands alone."

Compare it to a tournament. A tournament is one large event: many hands that lead to the result of one game. That's why so many rules are different there. A tournament player who walks away still gets dealt in and still pays their blinds. A cash game player who walks away just doesn't play.

Buy-ins and adding to your stack

Every cash game has a minimum and a maximum buy-in. (I used to see a fixed buy-in at Foxwoods, but for the most part, it's a range.) Common 1/2 no-limit buy-ins are $50 to $200, or more commonly nowadays, $100 to $300. There's usually something on the table that shows it. When a player sits down, they have to put chips in front of them anywhere in that range, and you're the one who makes sure they do.

  • Too little: a player comes to a $50 to $200 game with $40. You can't let them play until they get up to $50, even if the whole table says "we don't mind." You're the dealer. You enforce the integrity of the game.
  • Too much: a player comes to a $100 to $300 game with $400. You need to catch that and get them to buy in for the correct amount.

Between hands, never during one, a player can add to their stack up to the maximum buy-in. If they have $92 in a $50 to $200 game and want to add $125, that would put them over, so you tell them to take the extra off the table (in their pocket is the safest way to think about it). If they get above the maximum by winning, that's fine. That's what they came for.

What they can't do is take money off the table and keep playing. The button is the best position at the table. If a player put $200 in front of them every time they were on the button and dropped to $50 for the blinds, they'd be limiting their risk in bad position, and that isn't fair to everyone else. So in a cash game, money in play remains in play. The only way to take it out is to leave the game.

That's also what table stakes means: you can bet as much as you have on the table at the start of the hand, and no more. Nobody adds chips in the middle of a hand.

(How to actually do a buy-in at the table, including bills, chip runners and the well, is in buy-ins, cheque change and color-ups.)

Leaving, coming back, and changing tables

  • Leaving: at the end of any hand, any player can leave, as long as they don't owe money to the pot.
  • Coming back to the same table: a player can't cash out $600 and come back ten minutes later to buy in for $50. In most rooms, for about an hour, they have to come back with at least what they left with. It's hard for the casino to enforce, because dealers rotate every 30 minutes or so and the next dealer has no way to know the player was just there. That's when the players speak up. If there's an issue, call a supervisor.
  • Changing to another table at the same stakes: say a player runs their stack up to $700 and doesn't feel comfortable risking that much every hand. In a lot of rooms, they can go through the staff to move to another 1/2 table and buy in fresh within the range. There are exceptions: smaller rooms with must-move games treat it as one big table, and some large casinos require you to bring everything when you change tables.
  • A broken table: late at night, when tables get combined because players are leaving, it isn't the player's fault, and they get a choice. They can bring their whole stack, or start over with a buy-in between the minimum and maximum. Those are the only two options. With $620 in a $50 to $200 game, they bring $620 or buy in for $50 to $200. They can't go down to $400.

Who gets dealt in: the defaults

In a cash game, players can leave the table whenever they want. So as a dealer, you go by certain defaults all the time. If a player wants something different from the default, it's on them to speak up.

  • Sitting in their seat: deal them in. Even if their blind isn't out, even if they're facing the other way. You don't ask "are you playing?" Once they get a card, they're responsible for it.
  • Standing up at their seat: by default, don't. This is the borderline case, and it's okay to ask.
  • Away from the table: absolutely don't deal them in, and don't ask. If it's their blind, they get a missed blind button (more on that below).

If a player who's sitting there wants to be dealt out, they have to say so, and you have to acknowledge it so they know you heard them. And use a little guest service: if a regular is clearly walking back to the table as you're pitching, you can be on top of that and deal them in. But if you don't notice them, that's why we have the default. They should have said something or gotten there quicker, and you don't have a misdeal on your hands.

If you've pitched a card to a blind and they say "oh, I didn't want to play," you can slide the card along only if you know with 100% certainty nobody has seen it. Once a player has looked at a card, or you can't tell anymore, they're in, and they have to pay the blind. If they argue, explain that when you're sitting at the table, you get dealt in. If they still argue, call the floor. In my experience, the floor is going to make them pay.

What I correct in class

Stopping to wait for the blinds

This is one I think poker schools get wrong: dealers learn to stop and sit there waiting for a player to post the blind. If they're sitting at the table, you deal them in. Say "blinds, guys," let them know who the blinds are, and keep pitching. The one time you do stop is when the big blind has both cards and still hasn't posted: a quick "Big blind." I promise you that's what casinos want. (There's more on this in how to deal a hand of Texas hold'em.)

Adding chips or cash before the hand

  • Chips: a player can put more chips on their stack between hands without saying anything, as long as they stay under the maximum.
  • Cash: by default, if you didn't know about it, it doesn't play. The player has to put it out in front or hand it over for a buy-in before you start dealing. Bills tucked behind their stack don't automatically play. If you were a little distracted and realize on the second card that they had money out all along, you can make a judgment call. If there's any resistance, call the floor.
  • Cash that plays "behind" or "on top": if you can't get the chips right away (not enough in the well, or the room has a chip runner do buy-ins), announce it so the player and everyone else knows they're playing their chips plus that amount. The terminology varies by state and room.

Some rooms let cash play on the table. Foxwoods, years ago, let all cash play, and later changed it to hundreds only. Even there, it can't be hidden. Everyone needs to be able to tell how much it is.

Seeing other players' chips

Because stacks can change between hands, every player has the right during a hand to an unimpeded view of every other player's chips. If a player covers their stack with their hands and someone asks how much they have, the player can't say "tough." You ask them to lift their hands so everyone can see. If they give you a hard time, call the floor.

What I correct in class

Counting down a player's stack for an opponent

Students ask me whether a dealer should count out an opponent's stack when a player in a cash game asks for a chip count. Typically, a cash game player is entitled to a full view of their opponent's chips, but not to have them actually counted out, unless that particular room has decided to do it that way. (Tournaments are different: there, a player can be entitled to an exact count in some situations.) If I'm not sure what my room's policy is, I make sure the player can see the chips, and I call the floor.

Missed blinds, walking and straddles

Missed blinds

If you have to go to the restroom, you don't get dealt in and you don't pay your blinds while you're gone. The last time I played a cash game, I was playing 2/5 no-limit and took about a 30-minute walk around the pool at the Hard Rock in Hollywood, Florida. No penalty.

But a player who's away from the table when their blind comes around gets a missed blind button (a missed small blind or missed big blind button, or a spacer if your room doesn't have them, as long as you remember what it means and tell the next dealer). They don't play that hand, but they owe it when they come back. In 1/2 no-limit, when you see a missed big blind button, you can almost always assume the player posts $3 to come back: $2 in front of them as a big blind, and $1 in the middle as a dead small blind. The full rules for posting, buying the button and moving the button around empty seats are their own topic, and they'll get their own guide.

The "walking" rules

Players can get up whenever they want, but a poker room still wants its seats full. So most rooms limit how long players can be away. Once two people are up, they generally get something like 45 minutes. Some rooms count it in dealer downs instead, and the details are different at every casino. Most rooms also have a third man walking rule: the third player to get up gets less time, often 15 minutes or so, and a fourth player who gets up is often picked up right away. As the dealer, you're responsible for noticing when the third player gets up. If someone's chips do have to be picked up, you call a supervisor to handle it.

Straddles

A straddle is only possible in a cash game, never in a tournament. It's like a third blind. In 2/5 no-limit, the player under the gun could straddle for $10, usually double the big blind. (In 1/2, a lot of casinos use $5 just so you're not making as much change.) The player has to put it out or announce it before the cards are dealt. Most casinos say before the first card is pitched to anyone, and some places allow it until that player gets their first card. Once they've looked at their cards, they can't straddle.

The action then starts to the left of the straddle, and the straddler gets the last action preflop instead of the big blind. That's the difference between a straddle and a preflop raise: if everyone just calls a straddle, the straddler can still raise. If everyone just calls a raise, the betting round is over.

Rooms vary a lot. Some allow a straddle only under the gun, some under the gun or on the button, some from anywhere. If two people try to straddle the same hand, the one in the best position gets it, so the button always gets priority. I'll cover straddles in much more detail in their own guide.

Showdowns, loose change and chopped pots

Who has to show

In a cash game, when players are all in, as soon as one player shows their hand, nobody else needs to show. In a tournament, that's not the case. But someone does have to show to win a pot that's contested, and if a player is still holding their cards, they're still deciding, so you don't push the pot yet. (Why a hand has to be tabled to win is in cards speak and one player per hand.)

What I correct in class

Making the last player show to win

A lot of new dealers assume a player always has to show their cards to win the pot. Most of the time nowadays in a cash game, if it gets down to two players and one of them mucks, the other one just gets the pot. They don't have to show, even at the showdown, and generally speaking that's true even in a tournament. There might be a room here or there with a rule that they do.

Loose change doesn't play

In a lot of games, bets have to be made in increments of the chip used for the big blind. In 2/5 no-limit, the big blind is a $5 chip, so everything goes in increments of five, and $1 chips don't count. That's what "loose change doesn't play" means. If a player in 2/5 pushes $92 forward and says all in, their all in is $90. You toss the $2 back and the $90 is the bet. You don't have to lecture them about it. In a 10/25 game, everything goes in increments of 25. In a 5/10 game, it's still increments of five.

In 1/2 no-limit, the big blind is two $1 chips, so the $1 is the denomination used for the big blind, and every bet amount plays.

It's pretty common in casinos, but not universal. It's pretty rare for a room to let loose change play in a game where the big blind is a $5 chip, because it creates problems.

What I correct in class

Not knowing what to do with a $12 raise in 2/5

When I set up a player making it $12 in 2/5 in class, a student wasn't sure what to do. In any game where the blind is in increments of five, we don't allow loose change, so it rounds down to $10. It's not a huge deal which way your room chooses to round, as long as you're logical and consistent about it. Believe it or not, this happens once or twice every time I deal in Dallas: someone in 2/5 tries to make it $12 or $17, and I just toss the two bucks back and say, "That's going to round down, it's increments of five."

Chopping a pot: who gets the odd chip

When two players chop, the loose change doesn't get split in half. Anything smaller than the game's betting increment counts as one chip, and it goes to the player who acts first after the flop. That's the worst position. Some rooms do break the loose change down in a chop (the WSOP rules count it, because the small blind uses $1 chips). I like it better when you don't worry about it.

What I correct in class

"Closest to the button"

Students ask me what "the odd chip goes to the player closest to the button" means, or read it backwards. It's horrible terminology. What it means is whoever is going to receive the button next as more hands are dealt. That turns out to be the first player to act after the flop. The player on the button right now is actually the furthest from the button, because they'll be the last to get it again. I prefer to say worst position gets the extra chip.

Rake and tips in a cash game

The rake is how the house gets paid

A tournament has an entry fee. In a cash game, there's no entry fee, so the only way the casino makes money is by taking money out of each pot. That's the rake. It's revenue the house pays taxes on, and it usually ends up in a box under the table. It's also why the casino is happy to facilitate a game where players play against each other, with you as the official facilitator.

Think about the math. If you deal 10 hands every 30 minutes, and getting closer to 15 is better, and you average $3 in rake, that's $30 to $45 every 30 minutes. At $4 or $5 a hand, it adds up fast. Poker tables make pretty good money.

When a table is short (say three players are up), the players are allowed to ask the dealer to reduce the rake. You can never make that decision as a dealer. It's a floor call, and your casino's policy decides it. You only reduce the rake if someone asks and a supervisor approves it. When I was a supervisor, we taught our dealers to say our name and hold up the number of fingers, so we didn't have to walk all the way across the room every time a player left or came back.

Tips

Tipping is a culture we as dealers absolutely love, and in a cash game, it's a big part of what you're there for. Because each hand stands alone, when a player wins a $1,000 pot, that hand really is the thing that won them the money, so it makes sense for you to keep the tip.

  • Whenever someone tips you, you accept it, and you say thank you. Most tips come at the end of a hand, from the pot. Push the pot, move the button, drop the rake, then drop your tip.
  • In a cash game, you can accept a tip any time, even in the middle of a hand. If a player from another table comes over to tip you, have them put it on the table, so it doesn't seem sketchy.
  • Some players tip in cash. I've had a player I dealt a $1,000 high hand to come over and hand me $100. I said thank you, very appreciatively, and put it in my dealer box. Some places make you use chips.

Tournaments are different. Players there get paid by where they finish, not by any one hand. So most of the time, a tip you get in a tournament goes into the tournament pool and is split between all the dealers.

Players in a cash game can also usually use the chips in play to pay and tip a waitress for a drink, or pay for food. What they can't do is take chips off the stack for something like betting a horse at a racetrack that has poker, or the room may limit how much they can take off. In a tournament, you can never remove chips from play.

Seat changes, the bank and changing stakes

Seat changes

In a cash game, a player can change from one seat to another. Sometimes you have to manage it. Who gets an open seat:

  1. Whoever has a seat change button.
  2. A player already in the game over a new player. If a new player sits down but hasn't played a hand yet, and someone who's been in the game wanted that seat, the existing player takes it and the new player sits in the seat they left.

A player can't keep moving away from the blinds for free, either. If they're about to be the big blind and move to a seat that skips it, they have to post a big blind if they skip more than a certain number of players. Sometimes it's more than three, sometimes it's two or more. I prefer running a room where it's more than three.

Protecting the bank

You're responsible for everything on the table, including the cards and the chips in your bank. You don't really need a bank to run a tournament. A cash game needs one for buy-ins and for making change for the rake and the drop. In 1/2 no-limit, I sometimes call dealers change-making machines.

  • Never turn your back to the bank, and never let anyone take anything from it.
  • Keep yourself square to it. Dealer chairs usually have wheels. If you have to do something to one side, angle your chair that way instead of reaching.
  • Your arms never cross over the bank. In table games, they call keeping yourself centered "walking your game."

Some casinos don't have a locked-in bank. You carry your own tray from table to table, and you're responsible if it's up or down. (Everything about working the bank is in working the bank.)

Changing the stakes

In most poker rooms I know of, if every single player at a table agrees, the table can raise its limits, from 1/2 to 1/3, say. That's not something the dealer does. Let a supervisor know ("these guys want to play 1/3, can they?"), and the supervisor will come over and verify that every single person agrees.

Why rooms enforce these rules the way they do

In a tournament, a penalty means something: the player misses hands, and there's a limited number of them. In a cash game, the hands are unlimited and each one stands alone. You can't give someone a penalty and have it mean much to them. So in a cash game, the room's weapon to get people to follow the rules is not letting them play.

How to practice cash game procedures

  • Deal full hands to an empty table and make up the action as you go, including the situations on this page: a player who's away on their blind, a $92 all in at 2/5, a chopped pot.
  • Deal the same hand over and over. If you know the right answer but still have to stop and think about it, deal that exact hand ten times. After that, it's in your instincts, and it looks completely different.
  • Learn the rules of the room you're going to. Buy-in range, whether cash plays, straddle rules, seat change posting and walking limits are all different from room to room. A lot of the time the casino will teach you these when you get there, but you'll be more comfortable if they aren't brand new.

Common questions about cash game procedures

What is the difference between a cash game and a tournament?

In a cash game, chips are real money and each hand stands alone: players can leave after any hand, skip hands, and buy more chips between hands. A tournament is one long event, so players get dealt in and pay blinds even when they're away, and they can't leave with their chips.

Can you add chips during a hand in a cash game?

No. A player can only add chips between hands, and only up to the maximum buy-in. That's what table stakes means: you can only bet what was on the table when the hand started.

Can you take chips off the table in a cash game?

Not and keep playing. Money in play remains in play. The only way to take it out is to leave the game. In a lot of rooms, if you come back to the same table within about an hour, you have to bring at least what you left with.

What does "loose change doesn't play" mean?

In games where the big blind is a $5 chip, like 2/5, bets go in increments of five and $1 chips don't count. A $92 all-in plays as $90. In 1/2, every chip plays.

Who gets the odd chip in a split pot?

The player in the worst position, meaning the first player to act after the flop. Rule books call this "closest to the button," meaning the next player to receive the button.

Does the dealer deal in a player who's away from the table?

Not in a cash game. If the player is sitting in their seat, you deal them in. If they're away, you don't, and if it's their blind, they get a missed blind button.

Do players have to show their cards to win a cash game pot?

Not if everyone else folds. In most cash games today, if it gets down to two players and one mucks, the other wins without showing. When players are all in, once one player shows, nobody else has to.
