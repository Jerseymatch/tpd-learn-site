The Showdown

The whole hand builds up to this moment. The showdown is where cards speak comes into play, and your job is to award the pot to the best hand that was properly tabled, without influencing anybody along the way.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 15, 2026 · 20 min read

The entire game of poker is designed around the idea that whoever has the best cards at the end of the hand wins. Because of that, we need a procedure for how someone actually wins. That's the showdown. It's the moment the second fundamental rule of poker, cards speak, comes into play.

At the showdown you're a nonpartisan, impartial official. You don't have a favorite. (Realistically, you might want the player who tips the most to win, but you don't do anything about it.) Just like a basketball referee or a baseball umpire, your job is to fairly officiate. That means making sure each person gets their fair shot to win, and making sure you never violate the one player per hand rule while you do it.

When it's a showdown

A showdown happens when more than one player is competing for the pot and the betting is done. Usually that's after the river. It can also happen earlier, when there's been an all-in and a call and the action is complete.

When the river action is over, bring the last bets in and announce "showdown." If I had the music, I'd play it. By now the deck is out of your hand, which is nice, because you get to use two hands.

Tabling a hand

There's exactly one way to table a hand that qualifies to win: cards face up on the table, flat against the felt, where everybody can see both cards. That's the only way a player can be entitled to win any part of a pot. A player who doesn't table their hand can't win at the showdown when someone else did.

  • A player can leave their hand on their tabled cards. Nothing wrong with that. They do it so the dealer doesn't take their hand if they think it's the winner.
  • Holding cards up isn't tabling. Even if everybody at the table can see them, if the player hasn't put them face up on the felt, they don't play. And if a player who's holding their cards throws them in face down, you don't ask questions, even though you saw them. They've taken it upon themselves to muck.
  • You don't spread a player's cards. If one card is directly on top of the other, tell them, "I can't spread those." If it's already clear what the second card is, that's different. If they don't care and they muck it, their hand is dead.

What I correct in class

Spreading the cards for the player

When a player tables their hand, you're actually not supposed to touch it or spread it yourself. That's true in Omaha, too. It's a little hardcore, but it is a rule. It violates one player per hand, and we're not trying to booby trap anybody. Read the hand, and if you can't, ask the player to table it better.

Asking a player to turn their hand back over

In a multi-way all-in, everybody sometimes just flips their cards up at once. You do want to encourage only the hands eligible for the outermost pot to show first. But you never ask a player who tabled their hand at the showdown to flip it back over. Some dealers make that mistake. Once they've tabled it, it's tabled. Don't try to over-control the situation.

Mucking at the showdown

Not everybody has to show. The way a player mucks at the showdown is by tossing their cards forward, face down. When they do:

  • Take them immediately. Don't say "are you sure?" and don't give them a second. You don't want them to realize, from someone's facial reaction or anything else, that they made a mistake. They chose to muck. You're just facilitating what they chose to do.
  • Get them into the middle of the muck. Not on the top, not on the bottom. Slide them through so they're mixed in, not identifiable and not retrievable. If they sit on top, there's a small chance something crazy happens and a supervisor rules them live, and you don't want to be the facilitator of that.

Here's why it matters. Say a player was all in on the flop with two cards to come, and the river gives them a chop with the other player's aces. They're not thinking about it, they say "oh man, I lose," and toss their cards in. You take them and muck them, even though they would have chopped. You don't indicate to anyone what they have.

What I correct in class

Waiting to pick up mucked cards

At the showdown, if a player mucks, before you do anything else, take those cards, unless something truly imperative is happening in that moment. It's not good for the poker room when a player mucks and then 30 seconds later wins the hand because they realize, "Oh, I can beat that." And put them in the middle of the muck.

When it gets tricky (call the floor)

  • A player tosses their cards forward, you haven't gotten to them yet, and they grab them back because they realize they had a club. In most rooms, those cards are live. A player can show down their hand up until it's gone. Call the floor.
  • Somebody else says "wait, didn't you have a club?" and then the player snatches their cards back. They might not win, because that can violate one player per hand. Call the floor.
  • A player tables their hand, then picks it back up and mucks it because they didn't realize they made a flush on the river. That's a floor call, and it's entirely possible they gave up their right to the pot.

Cards speak, but there are caveats. You're not going to have to worry about these in an audition, and you shouldn't be the one making the ruling. Call the floor. It protects you and it protects the room, and good supervisors see enough of this to come to consistently good decisions.

Reading and framing hands

From the perspective of a five-card poker hand, your job is to read what the hand is. You're not deciding the powerful part of the hand. You're reading it.

  • Read the hand, not the high card. If a player has an ace and the board pairs nines, you don't say "ace high." You say "pair of nines with an ace," or "nines with an ace." Somebody else might have pocket eights and not realize they have nines and eights for two pair. If you'd said "ace high," you'd have communicated the wrong thing about what's on the board.
  • Keep it short. "Sevens with a queen" is good, and it's slightly more professional than just "a pair of sevens." "Sevens with a queen, eight, five" is too much. Two pair: "tens and nines with a king," or just "tens and nines."
  • Frame the winning hand. Push up three board cards or pull down two. Sometimes it's five, four or one. If the board plays, you still frame it. You're not influencing anything. You're doing your job.
  • Kill losing hands. When you see a hand that's beaten, take it, tap it twice, and put it in the muck. The two taps tell the player there's a hand beating them. That's their moment to say, "No, my hand's good," before you push the pot to the wrong person.

Practice this. Deal out multiple boards and multiple hands to yourself, read them quickly, and frame them properly.

What I correct in class

Saying "no flush" out loud

When the first two players show down and there's no flush between them, students sometimes say "no flush" out loud. You can't say that while other people are still in the hand. If one of them doesn't realize a flush would win, you can't be the one to tell them. That violates the one player per hand rule.

What I correct in class

Reading every hand like it's an audition

I don't like having dealers read every single hand. In class, I want you to treat it like a real game every time, because I want your habits to be the right habits. When a player turns their hand over, you're only looking for something that beats what's currently there. You're not reading the whole hand. If it doesn't beat it, kill it. In an actual audition, though, you'll get a lot further by announcing every hand smoothly, and if the auditioner asks you to read every hand, definitely do it.

What I correct in class

Calling a chop too early

Two players with king-jack high: one has king, jack, ten, eight, seven, and the other has king, jack, nine, eight, six. A student called it a chop. When you compare poker hands, you compare all five cards, all the way to the end. Only when both players have the exact same five-card hand do they chop.

What a player says doesn't change the cards

A hand is ranked by the cards actually in it, not by the player's opinion of it. If a player tables their hand and says the wrong thing, or thinks they're losing, their cards can still win. And if the dealer announces "both players are playing the board" when that's not true, that announcement doesn't stop the better hand from winning. It's unethical to deliberately miscall a hand, but it's quite possible to do it by honest mistake, so players are responsible for confirming their hand reads the way it was called. (There's more on this in cards speak and one player per hand.)

Who shows first

Anybody can show their hand at any time. If someone tables a hand and everybody else mucks, that player wins, even if their hand is terrible. In a cash game, once one person shows down, nobody else is required to show. Everyone else can show or muck.

The problem comes when two or more players are still eligible and nobody wants to go first. Give it a reasonable amount of time first. I'll give it around five seconds, and you don't want to make it much more than ten. Then you go to the showdown order.

The last aggressor

The most common rule is that the last aggressor (the player who bet or raised most recently) has to act first. If there was a bet and a raise and some calls on the river, it's the player who raised. Politely say something like, "You were the last aggressor. You need to show or muck."

That player has two options, not one. They can show, or they can forfeit and muck. They might know they have no chance and not want anyone to see what they were doing. If they muck, it goes to their left and around the table from there. Once a hand is shown, everybody else has to either fold or show to win.

When the river checks through

This is the biggest difference I've seen between casinos. Say a player bet the turn, everybody called, and then the river checks around. Some casinos stick with the last aggressor, and you have to remember who that was, even if it was back on the turn or earlier. Many casinos nowadays only count aggression on the river: if it checks around, worst position shows first. You have to know the rule where you work. When you're new at a room, it's totally okay to call a supervisor: "It's the showdown. It checked the river. This player was the last aggressor on the turn. Who shows first?"

Watch your language

If somebody is just sitting there holding their cards, you can nudge them, but don't say "are you folding?" (that might make them fold) or "are you showing?" (that might make them show). Give all the options, or use neutral language: "Sir, it's a showdown. I need you to finish up here." If anybody gives you a hard time, call the floor.

And don't jump straight to the showdown order to get people moving. Do your normal routine and allow them to choose to show down first. Manipulating the rules to get people to do things isn't a healthy way to approach it.

What I correct in class

"You show first"

When nobody's showing, students tell the last aggressor, "You show first." The right language is "show or muck." Give them both options. If a player is playing the board and knows for sure someone else beats it, just let them muck, and go to the next person.

When nobody shows at all

In a lot of cash games nowadays, if it gets down to one player because everyone else mucked, that player doesn't have to show to win. Other casinos require somebody to show down to win a pot, period. That gets tricky if everyone mucks. If you ever get a showdown where everyone mucks, don't push the pot. Call the floor. It's an uncomfortable situation, because it isn't just about following the rules. When nobody wants anyone to see what they were playing, there's a possibility it has something to do with cheating.

The showdown with side pots

When there are side pots, you show down the outermost side pot first, among only the players eligible for it. Treat it like a mini game without the others. The showdown order applies only to those players. Then you work your way in to the main pot. (Building the pots themselves is in how to build side pots.)

  • The last aggressor is always someone in the final pot. If one player goes all in for 60 and another player then goes all in for 125 over the top, the player who went in for 125 is the last aggressor, because they raised. The only way the last aggressor isn't in the final pot is if they leave the table or something like that.
  • An all-in player can show any time they want, but they can't win the pots they aren't in.
  • On the river, you don't always need to build every pot first. You can tell the players who are in for the full amount, "I need to see these hands first. They're for the outer pot." Award that one, then work inward: "You're going to win the outer pot. Now it's between you two for the next pot in." If you need to build the pots to be safe, especially when you're new, have the players wait and do it. You never want to make a mistake.
  • A tabled losing hand can be tapped and put on top of the muck, since it's already been seen.

"Showdown for the main pot"

Picture a river where one player bets into a side pot and everyone else folds. That player is so excited they won the side pot that they forget the player who was all in for less on the flop, and they toss their cards forward. Poker rooms handle this differently. Some will protect the player who forgot. Some want one player per hand to be so strong that they say nothing. Either way, it would be kind of wrong to touch those cards before you've done the one thing you're supposed to do: announce "showdown for the main pot." A lot of dealers won't say it, but you're supposed to. Whenever there's a pot to show down, you announce it, and you don't grab anyone's cards before you've said it. If the all-in player is hiding their cards back behind their hands, it's okay to say, "That player has cards over there."

What I correct in class

Who shows first when there's a side pot

A player goes all in for 50, another raises to 150, and a third calls. Who shows first? You could argue the player who went all in started the action, so they should show first. But there's a side pot. Before we look at the main pot, we look at the side pot, and the player who raised has to show (or muck) first between the two players in it. And when a player wins a side pot because everyone else folded, tell them "showdown for the main" as you push it. You'd be surprised how many times that player just tries to muck, thinking everyone folded.

Tournament showdowns

In a tournament, when a player is all in, all hands must be shown at the showdown. I haven't heard any exceptions to that anywhere in the poker world. That includes players who aren't all in themselves: if two players kept betting into a side pot and made it to the river, they still have to show down their hands. This is a time you protect your muck. It's there to prevent collusion.

What I correct in class

Forgetting the tournament show-all rule

When there's an all-in and you're at the showdown, everybody who made it to the river has to show, whether or not they put more chips in. You cover your muck, the hands are shown, and you read them no matter what they are. People forget this, and there's very likely going to be a day when you forget it, too.

"I want to see that hand"

Rooms have different rules about who can ask to see a hand that made it to the showdown. Some say anybody dealt into the hand can ask. Some say only the players who paid to get to the showdown. Some say only the player who called the last bet.

If a player is entitled to ask, then the moment they say "I want to see that hand," you protect the muck. The player shouldn't be able to get their cards in anymore. Then:

  • If someone who's not winning the pot asks, take the cards, tap them on the muck to show everybody they're not live, flip them over, and quickly put them back in the muck. They're dead.
  • If the player showing the current winning hand asks, that hand is live, and you announce it.
  • If nobody has shown yet and someone asks, it's live. You're making that player show first.

Once the cards touch the muck, they're no longer shown. In many rooms, the muck is treated like a sacred dead-hand zone. In most casinos, the board isn't an extension of the muck, and neither is the dealer's hand. If a player insists, call the floor. You don't enforce rules when players resist. That's what supervisors are for.

This right isn't supposed to be abused. At the room I worked at, a supervisor had to be present before a called hand was shown, and they'd ask the player why they wanted to see it. If the answer was "because I paid to see it," the supervisor said no. A player who uses it as a way to irritate people can be warned, and if they keep doing it, management can take the right away.

What I correct in class

Going into the muck

As a dealer, you never go into the muck, not even to answer a player's question about what a hand was. Doing it would fail an audition. Returning a card to a player off the muck is a floor decision, every time, no matter how certain you are.

Other showdown situations

Every room's rule book is a little different. These come from the procedure manual I've used in a real casino, and every one of them is a floor call. Your job is to recognize it and get a supervisor. (One card shown, mucked winners, slow rolls and show one, show all are in showdown disputes.)

  • Too many cards: a hand with too many cards for that form of poker is dead and can't win any part of the pot, even if there's a straight on the board that everybody plays.
  • Too few cards: a hand with too few cards at the showdown may be ruled live, as long as the player still has one or more cards. If I'm the supervisor and nobody can find the missing card, I award the pot, and then we suit the deck to make sure all the cards are there.
  • Discarding a card: it's improper for a player to discard any of their cards, even ones they don't need. A player who deliberately does that is subject to having their hand ruled dead.
  • A tabled hand the dealer mucks by mistake: a hand placed face up on the table and properly identified is live, and the player is entitled to the pot, even if the dealer mucks it in error.
  • A hand that's thrown away but hasn't touched the muck may be retrieved and ruled live, provided throwing it away didn't get another player to muck their hand.
  • A card goes flying off the table: call the floor. If one card lands face down, the player can turn it over, provided it didn't hit the muck.
  • You push the pot to the wrong person and it's called to your attention right away: call the floor.

And a player can never reach out and grab the pot on their own. An inexperienced player will sometimes try it. You have to put a stop to it.

The room's rules win. If your poker room's management says to do something differently from what I teach, do what they say. It's their poker room. You'll find that not every supervisor and shift manager is on the same page, and it's easy to assume one of them has it wrong. You never tell a supervisor they're wrong, and you never tell them "True Poker Dealer said this." At the very most important moment, you might quietly say, "I thought it was this," in a way where you're the one who might be wrong.

Pushing the pot

After all the losing hands are in the muck, push the pot to the winner. Then move the button, drop the rake, and drop your tip.

  • Kill the losing hands before you push. Not after.
  • Leave the winning hand and the board where they are until the pot is pushed. Leave the player's hand in front of them. Some dealers clean up the board and the cards first and then push the pot. It's not wrong, but it's a shortcut. If something weird happens, it's easy to see exactly what was what. Clean up after.
  • Never put the pot on top of your muck. That's a big no-no. Back in 2011, I failed Victor in his audition because his muck was underneath his pot. I didn't know how that happens, but it scared me, so I couldn't give him a dealer job. Over time, he became COO of Texas Card House.

What I correct in class

Cleaning up before moving the button

After students push the pot, their instinct is to clean up the board. Train yourself to move the button the instant the pot is pushed, every single time, no exceptions. Then drop the rake. There was a stretch of about two years at Texas Card House where I had trouble remembering where the button was, because I didn't have that habit built all the way into my instincts. Do it the same way every time and it becomes automatic.

How to practice the showdown

  • Deal out several hands and a board, then read them as fast as you can and frame the winner. Do it over and over with different boards.
  • Practice killing hands: tap a tabled losing hand twice and put it in the muck. And practice taking face-down mucks immediately and sliding them into the middle.
  • Set up multi-way all-ins with two or three side pots and run the showdown from the outermost pot in, saying "showdown for the main pot" out loud.
  • Learn your room's rules: who shows first when the river checks through, whether the last player has to show to win, and who can ask to see a called hand.

Common questions about the poker showdown

Who has to show their cards first at a poker showdown?

If nobody shows voluntarily, the last aggressor (the last player to bet or raise) has to show or muck first. If the river checks around, some rooms go back to the last aggressor on an earlier street, and many go to worst position, the first player to act.

Do you have to show your cards to win in poker?

In a lot of cash games, if everyone else mucks, the last player wins without showing. Some rooms require a hand to be shown to win. If anyone has tabled a hand, you have to table yours to beat it. In a tournament with an all-in, every hand that reaches the showdown must be shown.

What counts as tabling a hand?

Both cards face up, flat on the felt, where everyone can see them. Holding them up, even where everyone can see them, doesn't count.

Can a player get their cards back after mucking?

Usually not, and a dealer never hands cards back off the muck. Some rooms let the floor retrieve cards that are 100% identifiable on top of the muck. If the player tossed them but they haven't reached the muck, many rooms rule them live, as long as tossing them didn't make another player muck. It's always a floor call.

Can you ask to see a mucked hand at showdown?

Depends on the room. Some let anyone dealt in ask, some only the players at the showdown, and some only the player who called. If a player who isn't winning asks, the hand is dead. The right can be taken away if it's abused.

Why does the dealer tap the losing hand twice?

To tell the player there's a hand beating theirs. It gives them a last moment to say their hand is good before the dealer pushes the pot.
