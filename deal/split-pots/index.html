Split Pots & Quartering

Players get real nervous when their money is at stake and the dealer is fumbling through a chop. Here's how to split a pot cleanly, who gets the extra chip, and the quartering shortcut that saves you from bringing every chip into the middle.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 16, 2026 · 15 min read

For the first ten years or so I worked in this industry, I didn't really need to know how to quarter somebody. Now that there are all these double-board PLO bomb pots, it comes up a lot more, and even in hold'em double-board bomb pots you'll occasionally have to do it. And even though I'm True Poker Dealer on YouTube, it wasn't that long ago that I had to figure it out on the fly, and even the players didn't have confidence in me. That's okay. I didn't know how to do it.

I want you to see how, because poker players get real nervous when a dealer has never seen this before, their money's at stake, and the dealer is fumbling their way through it. This guide starts with the basics of chopping any pot, then gets into high-low games and quartering.

How to chop a pot

When two players tie, they chop: each gets half. You don't count out the exact amount of the pot and then calculate. You stack it up and break it in half, because that's so much simpler.

  1. Pull the largest chips back toward you. Larger denominations go closer to you.
  2. Make even stacks of each color, side by side. As long as two stacks are the same denomination, all greens or all reds, you can tell they're even, and as the dealer you have the best angle to see it from the side. If there's an odd chip in a stack, you're the one who can see it. That's a big part of why we use this procedure. Even stacks, not counting each one.
  3. Work down the denominations. Say the pot has blacks, greens and reds. The greens go in the next row in even stacks. Leftover green goes on one side, and you match it with 25 in red. Then all the red gets stacked up, so there's only one stack of red. If there's a loose chip, there's a loose chip. We never make an exception to this.
  4. Even left to right is what matters. The stacks don't have to be the same height as each other, as long as each side has the same amount.
  5. Push with two hands. Every single time you chop a pot, it's two hands, each hand pushing one half to its player. Zero exceptions. That's what makes it feel like poker and look like a dealer. When it's simple, push both at once. When the amounts are so large it's too much to move all at once, you don't have to.

A few more details:

  • Lay chop amounts side by side, not on top of each other. If you're chopping several ways, traditionally you put them next to each other, sideways. If you really don't have room, stack them across evenly.
  • Don't add lone chips at the end. If you finish and then put another red chip on top of each side, it makes people uncomfortable. Get it right in the stacks.
  • Leave the board where it is. I do not like pushing the board aside when you're chopping a pot. I understand it when you're pushing a whole pot and need the space. When you're chopping, there's plenty of room right where the pot already is.
  • Don't forget the rake. Split pots get raked too, and students forget this. You can't take it from just one player when it should come out of the whole pot. See rake, promo drop and tips.
  • Push chips toward the player. Never pull a player's chip stack toward you when you're giving chips back to them.

Chopping before everyone's bet is back

In a multiway all-in that gets chopped, a student starts chopping the main pot while everyone's bets are still combined in the middle. As the dealer, you're responsible for everything on the table. If everyone's money is in the middle like that, I'm terrified someone's going to end up taking something that doesn't belong to them. First make sure everyone gets their own money back cleanly. Then chop.

Who gets the odd chip

When a pot doesn't split evenly, usually the extra chip doesn't get broken down and goes to one player. Some rooms do break it down, so know where you deal. Where they don't, you don't make change for it.

  • In most cash games, worst position gets it. That's the player who's going to get the button next, the first player to act after the flop. "Closest to the button" is the horrible terminology we use for it, and it confuses everyone. I explain it in cash game procedures.
  • It's room specific. The first thing you do when splitting a pot is establish who gets the extra chip.
  • Don't break down to the dollar. If the leftover is a $5 chip and a few $1 chips, generally you're not going to break that down to the nearest dollar. Treat it as increments of five.
  • Keep it visible. While you're waiting to find out who gets it, the extra chip sits centered on top between the two stacks. When you pay it, toss it to that player first and separately, then give them the rest. Don't put it on top of their half where the two amounts look the same. Leave it next to the stack, so surveillance and the players can all see.
  • In a tournament, chop each pot separately. Some cash rooms are fine with you combining a main pot and side pots and chopping them together. In a tournament, you're supposed to be technical and chop each pot separately, because every chip impacts the rest of the tournament. If there's an extra chip in every pot, the same player gets every one.

High-low split pots

In high-low games like Omaha eight or better, the high hand gets half the pot and the best qualifying low gets the other half. These games are the most commonly mistaken games for awarding a pot incorrectly, so a few things change.

  • Read the high first. One of the biggest giveaways that a dealer is inexperienced with high-low is that they start with the low. Every experienced dealer reads the high hand first.
  • Announce it clearly. "Three tens wins the high, four-deuce chops the low."
  • The extra chip goes to the high hand first. There's an order of priority. The high hand gets the extra chip. After the high has been paid, if two players chop the low, position decides who gets the extra chip there.
  • Kill hands that lose everything. When a player loses both the high and the low, kill their hand.
  • Leave the board visible. If you hide the board, accidentally or by moving it aside, nobody at the table can tell if you made a mistake. And this is when players are catching up on what happened.

Stack the pot as it builds

In a limit high-low game, whether or not the board allows a split, we stack the pot. That's what we do. It doesn't have to be stacked right away; it depends on the room. I like what the WSOP manual says: stack it by the river. In limit, the bets are fixed, so you can keep adding to even stacks as you go. In pot-limit eight or better, the bet amounts get weird and the chips get large, so I make stacks of each color as the pot builds, and after the river I'm just organizing, not stacking from scratch.

Double-board bomb pots are split pots too, because each board gets halved, so you try to stack those as you go. But in a regular, single-board game, you don't. In four-card high-only pot-limit Omaha, you don't stack the chips as you build the pot.

What quartering means

Getting quartered means winning a quarter of the pot. It happens whenever a half of the pot is chopped. Take a PLO double-board bomb pot. Each board gets exactly half of the pot. Say one player wins the top board outright with tens full of deuces. On the bottom board, that same player and one other player both have an ace-high straight. So:

  • The first player wins half the pot for the top board, plus half of the bottom half. That's three quarters.
  • The other player wins half of the bottom half. That's a quarter. They got quartered.

High-low works the same way. If one player wins the high and also ties another player for the low, the high hand gets three quarters and the other low gets a quarter. If the high hand doesn't share the low, it's half, a quarter and a quarter.

With no bets on the end, it's simple: give a half first, then split the other half in half. Half the pot goes to the top-board winner, and you chop the rest between the two straights.

The quartering shortcut

When there are big bets on the end, bringing every chip into the middle and then distributing it all back out takes a long time. Players in these games are used to a shortcut. It works when two players put money in on the river, and one of them gets three quarters and the other gets one quarter.

Why it works

Picture $20 in the main pot. One player is all in for 500 on the river, and the other calls 500. If you brought it all in and split it, the quarter player gets $5 of the main pot and the three-quarter player gets $15. Of the 1,000 in river bets, the quarter player gets 250 and the three-quarter player gets 750. The money in the middle and the money out front each get split at the same ratio. So you don't have to bring the bets in at all.

How to do it

  1. Make sure you have the correct amount from each player. This is the fundamental rule. "Pretty sure" one player has the other covered isn't good enough. Count down the all-in and get the same amount from the other player.
  2. The quarter player gives half of their bet to the three-quarter player. The three-quarter player started with 500 out front. Add 250 and they have 750. The quarter player keeps 250. That's exactly right.
  3. Tell the quarter player to take the rest of their bet back. That's 50% of their river bet, so they get 50% back.
  4. Then split the main pot the normal way: a half, then the other half in half.

There's an even simpler way to do step 2. You don't need to do any math: just stack up the quarter player's bet, cut it in half, and hand half to the three-quarter player. In the 500 example, it barely saves any time. With bigger amounts, like an all-in of 2,050, it helps more. Half is 1,025, and you can say the amount if that's quicker, or just cut the stack and slide it over.

When the bet doesn't split evenly

Say it's 2,055 each. You can't cut a $5 chip in half. Because the money in the middle and the money out front split at the same ratio, you can just take the same amount from each player and bring it to the main pot. Take 5 from each, and now you have 2,050 each, which cuts in half easily. Use whatever denomination makes it easiest, as long as you take an equal amount from each. In one lesson, I ran into an amount that was odd by a dollar, but taking a dollar meant I'd still have to make change, so it was easier to take five from each player instead.

If the three-quarter player is the one who called by dumping a ton of chips in, you don't need to break the whole thing down to find their extra $5. Take the quarter player's $5 chip into the middle and ask the three-quarter player for any $5 chip. They're taking their bet back anyway.

Covered stacks

A player pushes their whole stack in a lot of the time when they're all in or calling an all-in. You can't just take the whole thing and cut it in half, because you don't know the other player has enough to cover it. You need to make sure they have the same amount.

Once you've established that the three-quarter player clearly has the quarter player covered, the quarter player's stack is the active bet amount. Then you can cut that whole stack in half and hand it off, without counting it.

When you hand over an amount you've announced, cut it out for the player first. Don't give it to them and push the rest through without cutting it out. That's really scary and uncomfortable for the player.

The odd chip in a quartered pot

Some games specify where the extra chip goes. In the room I deal in, worst position gets it, but it isn't tied to the top or bottom board. Personally, I put the extra chip with the half of the pot that I'm quartering. Is that correct? Not always, but it's fine. If your poker room manager tells you to deal it a certain way, always listen to them over me.

When there's no shortcut

  • A player who loses everything. Say three players are in, one wins three quarters, one gets a quarter, and the third wins nothing. Take the loser's cards to the muck and bring their bet into the main. Leave the other two bets out and use the shortcut, then chop the main pot including that money.
  • Half, a quarter and a quarter. One player wins one board, and two other players chop the other board. Each puts 2,000 in on the river, so 6,000 total: 3,000 to one player, 1,500 to each of the others. The shortcut doesn't work here. There's a way to do the math, but players aren't used to it and aren't comfortable with it. It doesn't come up enough. So bring all the money into the middle and chop it up. You can even say it: "We're going to bring it in. There's no shortcut here."
  • Three or more players putting money in on the river. The shortcut doesn't always work exactly the same way. Unless it's the case above, where one player loses everything, bring it in.
  • A side pot. One mistake that happens a lot: the player who gets a quarter, or three quarters, isn't actually eligible for all the money, because they were all in for less. Someone counts down 2,000 and tries to take it from everybody, but there's a side pot between those players that has to be handled first. To be eligible for a pot, a player has to have put the correct amount in on that betting round. See how to build side pots.

When you're not sure

This stuff can be a little intimidating the first time you're in that situation. It's okay if the first few times, even right after reading this, you have to stop and ask yourself, "Wait, am I right about this?" There are variations that make it complicated, and you figure them out over time. Don't be afraid to go slow and double check that the shortcut applies completely to the spot you're in.

My experience is that the players in games where people get quartered are very familiar with all this, and they'll talk you through it a lot of the time. Some players, in some rooms, won't be so nice about it. Remember: as the dealer, if you're not 100% sure, you can take information from anybody.

Dealers are taught never to say they're sorry. But what you don't apologize for is mistakes, because you don't want a player to think they've been wronged by the business. You can say "I'm sorry" when you're being human, and it's very defusing. If you're taking a second to figure it out and a player is telling you what to do, and not nicely, try: "Oh, I'm sorry. I've learned how to do this. This is just my first time actually doing it." Most of the time, they understand. Occasionally somebody will say you should have practiced it on your own, and we don't worry about them.

How to practice split pots

  • Chop the same pot over and over. Blacks back, even stacks by color, match the leftover, one stack of red, two hands out. Repeat the same situation until it's instinct.
  • Set up the quartering examples. Put out $20 in the middle and 500 from each player, and do it the long way once, bringing everything in. Then do it with the shortcut and check that you end up with 255 and 765 both ways.
  • Practice the variations. An all-in for an odd amount, a player who loses both boards, and half-quarter-quarter, where there's no shortcut.

Common questions about split pots

Who gets the odd chip in a split pot?

In most cash games, the player in the worst position: the first player to act after the flop, who's the next to get the button. In high-low games, the high hand gets it first. It's room specific, so find out where you deal.

What does it mean to get quartered in poker?

Winning a quarter of the pot, because you tied for half of it. It happens in high-low games when two players chop the low, and in double-board games when two players tie on one board.

How does a dealer split a pot into quarters?

Give a half first, then split the other half in half. With two players betting on the river, the quarter player gives half of their bet to the three-quarter player and takes the rest back, and the main pot gets split the normal way.

How is a double-board bomb pot split?

Each board gets exactly half the pot. The best hand on each board wins that half. If two players tie on a board, they split that half, and each gets a quarter.

Do dealers make change for the odd chip?

Usually not. The extra chip goes to one player, though some rooms do break it down. Small leftover amounts usually aren't broken down to the dollar.

Should the dealer stack the pot in high-low games?

Yes. In limit high-low, you stack the pot as it builds, at the latest by the river. In pot-limit eight or better, make stacks of each color as you go. Double-board pots get stacked too. In a regular single-board pot-limit game, you don't.
