The Technical Rules of Betting

Betting rules exist to read the minds of the players who are acting: to let a player who's acting with integrity do it comfortably, and to stop anyone from using words, gestures or chips to take advantage of everyone else. Here's how they work, and which ones you enforce yourself.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 15, 2026 · 23 min read

The two fundamental rules of poker are one player per hand and cards speak, and every other rule is built on them. The betting rules are what make cards speak meaningful: the betting rounds are where players decide whether the best cards make it to the showdown. So poker needs rules for how chips, words and gestures turn into actions, the same way a kid has to learn how to hold a bottle before they can drink from it.

A lot of this is different from poker room to poker room. I'm going to give you the way that's most common, and I'll tell you where rooms differ, because dealer schools usually teach one way and you'll run into the others. I'm teaching what I know from the United States. I've never dealt outside it. And the most important rule on this page: when in doubt, call the floor. "Floor, table three." A supervisor would much rather come over and get it right than have you guess.

Words and gestures

Statements in turn are binding

When it's a player's turn, saying fold, check, call, raise, or a specific bet amount is binding.

  • A player facing a bet who says "fold" has folded. You don't snatch the cards. You say, "You announced fold. I need you to give me your cards." If they resist and say they didn't mean it, you call the floor. You never decide a hand is dead and act on it yourself (unless they toss you the cards, and then you muck them). But you can't ignore it either.
  • Folding when it's been checked to them is different. It's bad etiquette, and it's more circumstantial. Say it's the big blind, who didn't realize they were the big blind because they hadn't put it out yet. It happens, especially when a player is distracted. You still want to grab the cards most of the time, but you can let them sit there for a second instead of grabbing them right away. If they take them back and someone complains, call the floor. Facing a bet, though, you grab them immediately, every time. Giving cards back after the bettor may have given away information about their hand seems very violating to one player per hand.
  • A player who says "call" has to put the chips in. Even if they then toss their hand into the muck, they still owe that money. And if you see them get scared by the card that just came and start trying to get their hand into the muck before paying, protect the muck. I don't usually want your hand on top of the muck touching those cards, but in that extreme moment, truly protecting it is the better choice.

Before you take any of this to a supervisor, be sure of what you heard.

"I raise 20" means a raise to 20

Heads up, someone bets 10, and the other player says, "I raise 20." Does that mean the bet is now 20, or 20 more? I can't tell you what every poker room says, but the enormous majority treat it as a raise to 20 total. Any time a player says raise and an amount, that's the total. Because there are two possible meanings, the poker world picked one, and a player who meant 20 more learns the lesson quickly.

Words also beat chips. A player who says "I raise to 25" and throws out a different amount gets their bet corrected to the amount they said.

Irregular words and gestures

Players get creative. People don't only say hello; they say what's up, how you doing. At a poker table, that kind of creativity is dangerous, because it opens the door to manipulating the rules. The standard is that an irregular statement or gesture is as binding as the regular one if the intent is obvious, or it could easily and justifiably be read that way.

  • Rapping the table or waving the cards is generally a check.
  • Pushing both hands forward is often read as all in. If a player does that and then tries to check, stop the action: "Hold on. It seemed like you were making a gesture to go all in. Now you're trying to check. I need a floor." You're not making the decision, but you're the one at the table upholding the integrity.
  • Consistency counts. If a player's gesture meant all in last time, tell the supervisor: "The last time you did that, it meant all in." That makes a huge difference in getting a ruling with the right kind of integrity.
  • "You win." Heads up, facing a bet, a player says "you win." That can get the other player to show their hand. I've seen that ruled a fold.

Here's language you can use with players, and it helps you by helping them: "Whenever you say or do anything at the table that could be interpreted to mean something, you put yourself at risk." That puts the responsibility on them to stay out of risk.

The TDA, in the rules it uses for its tournaments, says players use unofficial terms and gestures at their own risk. It even rules on "I bet five" at 200/400: if the pot is under 5,000, it's 500. If it's 5,000 or more, it's 5,000. I show you that for the thinking behind it, not so you rule it. That's for a supervisor.

Conditional statements

Heads up, it's not their turn, and a player says, "If you bet, I call." The other player bets. Is the first player committed? Rooms handle it differently. Some enforce it as binding. Some say only statements in turn are binding. Some require a manager to make that decision, and I like that very much too. The TDA (Rule 59) calls conditional statements non-standard and strongly discouraged, and says they may be binding and may be penalized. If you hear one, especially if a player then tries to get out of it, call the floor.

When the supervisor comes, only say what happened. Don't give your opinion on what it means. "This player was facing this bet. They said 'you win' and held their cards like this. That player showed their hand, and as soon as they saw it, this player called." Dealers don't usually get trained on how to talk to the floor, and it's one of the most helpful things you can do for the supervisor and the table.

String bets

A player isn't allowed to make a bet, gauge an opponent's reaction, and then add more chips. If they want to add more, they have to say so when they make the bet. So a bet is one forward motion. If a player goes forward with chips, comes back, and puts more out without having said anything, you immediately say, "No, you can't do that. That's a string bet. You're only allowed one motion." A lot of people just don't know. Explain it.

Ways a player can do it legally:

  • Announce the amount first. "300." Then they can bring it out however they need to. We have to let people bet the amount they want, and pushing a lot of chips together is hard for some people.
  • Stage it, then push it. They can assemble chips in front of them and push them forward together.
  • Say "raise" first. Then they can put out the call and go back for more, and the second motion is the raise. It has to be exactly the call amount the first time. Once they've said raise, they're required to raise.
  • Come out with a stack and cut off part of it in one smooth motion. They don't have to bet everything in their hand. What they can't do is put 25 out, keep their hand there, watch the other player, and decide to add more.

"I call, and I raise you a hundred" is not allowed. You hear it on TV and in movies, and it's annoying to the whole poker industry, because in a real casino, once you say "I call," that's your action. It's a verbal string bet. "I see your 25 and raise you 100" is the same problem: "I see your 25" means call.

Some motions are borderline. A player who's just bad with chips might take a while to get one motion out. If it really was one motion, I'm going to let it stand, and maybe tell the player after the hand, "When you bet 65 on the turn, that was close to a string bet. I'd announce your amount or come out with the right chips." That's preventative dealing. If it wasn't one motion, call it, and if the player pushes back, get a supervisor. And watch your wording. "I viewed that as a string bet" lands a lot better than "that's a string bet," and it gives the supervisor room. When a player ends up looking stupid, they resent you even though you were right.

What I correct in class

"Raise" said mid-motion

In a class hand, a player said "raise to 50" while the chips were still on their way out, and the question came up whether it was a string bet, because it looked like two motions. I allowed the raise. In most poker rooms, if the player says raise (or an amount) before the chips are actually out there, that's allowed. Some rooms say it depends how much they have in their hand. I think the default is to allow it. Once the chips have landed and they go back for more without having said anything, that's a string bet.

The wrong amount: the 50% rule

A player puts out more than the call but less than a legal raise, without saying anything. Your room will have a rule for this, and your job is to correct the action. In no-limit, the general rule is:

  • 50% of a raise or more: the player has to put in a full minimum raise, even if that puts them all in.
  • Less than 50%: it's a call. Give the extra back.

Examples. On the turn, a player bets 25. A full raise would be to 50, so half a raise is 12.50.

  • The next player silently puts out 40. That's 15 more than the call, which is more than 12.50. You say, "I need 10 more," and it becomes a raise to 50.
  • They put out 35 instead. That's 10 more, less than half a raise. You toss the 10 back to them (not onto their stack) and say, "That's a call."
  • It's 85 to call, and a player pushes out a stack that turns out to be 130. That's 45 more, and half of 85 is 42.50. So it's a raise, and a minimum raise is to 170. You say, "I need 170." You don't ask them what they think.

It's always a minimum raise. A player who says, "Fine, if you're going to make me raise, I'll make it 300," can't. If it takes you a second to do the math, it takes a second. If the chips need to be broken down first, stop the action: "Hold on, I need to see what this is." And always say clearly what the action became: "That's a call." "That's a raise to 50."

The same thing happens with words. If it's 25 and the player says "35," that's a call, just like 35 in chips. And if a player says "raise" and puts out too little, you still make them raise, to the minimum. Facing a 45 bet, a player says raise and puts out 60: "I need 30 more. The raise has to be 90."

This is one you handle yourself. You don't need the floor unless the player resists, and you won't get much resistance, because a player who put out 35 into a 25 bet knows it wasn't clear. When you do need the floor, just call them and give the information. Never tell a player what the supervisor is going to say when they get there. I can't tell you how many times I've seen a dealer do that out of ego, and then the supervisor comes and the dealer is the one who's wrong. It's one of the worst situations you can put yourself in.

And never touch a player's stack to get the chips. Ever. Tell them to put the rest out.

Rooms vary at exactly 50%. Some say exactly half is a call, some say it's a raise. I like 50% or more for no-limit. Limit is a little different: some rooms force a call when a player puts out half a raise, some force the raise. I'd force the full raise. I once worked in a room whose dealer manual said that a raise even one chip short was a call. I think that's a terrible rule, and we didn't follow it. We used the 50% rule.

Offering the player their options

When a player says "raise," don't announce "a minimum of 4, up to your whole stack." We don't offer that to players unless they ask.

The minimum raise

In no-limit, the minimum raise is the call amount plus the previous full raise. You'll hear people say a raise has to be double the bet. It's not true. It just works out that way when the only action so far is one bet.

Here's how it builds in 1/2 no-limit:

  1. The big blind is 2, so the first raise can be to 4 (a raise of 2).
  2. The next minimum raise is to 6, then 8. Anyone can raise more, up to their whole stack.
  3. If it goes 4, 6, and then someone makes it 9, that's a raise of 3. Now the minimum raise is to 12.
  4. Someone makes it 12, and the next player makes it 20, a raise of 8. The minimum is now 28.
  5. Someone makes it 28, and the next player makes it 37, a raise of 9. The minimum is now 46.

And after a bet: someone bets 45 on the flop, and the next player says raise. The minimum is 90, because the last raise went from 0 to 45. If it goes 15, 30, 45, 60, the next minimum is 75. If someone makes it 100 instead, that's a raise of 40, so the next minimum is 140.

Why does a dealer need to know this so precisely? Because of the 50% rule above. If the bet is 9 and a player puts out 11, you need to know the last raise was 3, so 2 more is over half a raise, and you need to know the minimum raise is 12.

What I correct in class

Doubling instead of adding the last raise

A large majority of regular poker players don't understand the minimum raise, and students come in with the same idea: double the bet. It's the call amount plus the current raise amount, not the raise amount alone and not double. Always know the current raise amount, because you can't apply the 50% rule without it.

All-in for less and reopening the action

A betting round is complete when every player has had a chance to act on the same amount. The question is what happens when a player goes all in for more than the current bet, but less than a full raise.

The example I use: one player bets 20. The next raises to 70, a raise of 50. The next calls 70. Then a player goes all in for 100. That's 30 more, which is more than half of the 50 raise. Does it count as a raise?

In most poker rooms, no. To reopen the betting, the all-in has to be a full raise. Being more than 50% of a raise doesn't matter here. So the player who raised to 70 and the player who called 70 can only call or fold. They've already acted on 70, and they're not facing a full raise. If the all-in had been for 120, that's a full raise of 50, and everyone could act on the new amount.

But the all-in for less only affects players who already acted on the current bet. A player who has only acted on the 20 so far hasn't acted on the 70 yet, so they can raise. Their minimum raise is to 150: the call of 100 plus the last full raise of 50. The same thing confuses people the other way. It goes check, bet 50, all in for 60. People think nobody can raise now. That's not true. Everyone who hasn't acted on the 50 yet can do whatever they want.

That's what the TDA says, too (Rule 47): an all-in, or several short all-ins that add up to less than a full raise, won't reopen the betting for players who already acted and aren't facing at least a full raise. Several short all-ins can add up, though. In the same example, all in for 100 and then another all in for 120 means the player who's been sitting at 70 is now facing 50 more, a full raise, so the betting is open for them. I can't think of a single time I've actually seen that happen. If it happens and you're not sure, call the floor.

Some rooms do use the other rule, that more than 50% of a raise reopens the action. That's not wrong. It's just less common.

What I correct in class

Two different rules that get mixed up

This is one of the most confusing rules in poker, and even hired dealers in real poker rooms get it wrong all the time. Students mix up two separate rules. Rule one: when a player puts out 50% of a raise or more, we force them to put in a full raise, and that makes it a raise. Rule two: when a player goes all in, did a raise take place, so that the action is reopened? The simple way to think about it: we force players to raise when they put in more than 50%, but if they don't have enough to raise, it's not a raise. What makes it a raise is getting the rest of the money for it, and an all-in player has no more money.

The minimum raise after a short all-in: east coast and west coast

Here's where there are two real schools of thought, and I've gotten answers from people at the top of the industry on both coasts. Someone bets 100. The next player goes all in for 140. The next player wants to raise. What's the minimum?

  • The way I teach (and what I've seen on the east coast, in the middle states I've asked about, and in most online poker): the 40 wasn't a full raise, so the raise amount is still 100. The minimum raise is the call of 140 plus 100: 240. An all-in for less than a full raise never changes the raise amount.
  • What I've seen on the west coast: the all-in wasn't half a raise, so the next player "completes" to 200.

If the all-in had been 180 instead, more than half a raise, I believe both sides land on 280. My personal preference is to never complete in no-limit. Somebody raising is trying to make a significant impact on the pot, and making it 240 does more than 200. But it doesn't come up much, and whatever your room does is the answer. Find out where you work. The same rule generally applies to tournaments and cash in the same room.

In limit, half a raise counts

Limit is different. In limit, a player who goes all in for half a raise or more has made a raise.

  • 4/8, on the flop: bet 4, call 4, call 4, and a player goes all in for 6. That counts as a raise, so the next player can fold, call 6, or raise to 10.
  • Same spot, all in for 5 instead. That's less than half a raise, so the next player can fold, call 5, or complete to 8. A player who already acted on the 4 can only call the extra 1 or fold.
  • With three or more players, there's usually a bet and three raises, and then it's capped. A short all-in that counts as a raise counts toward the cap (4, 6, 10, 14, capped). Heads up, most casinos allow unlimited raises.

Because the same dealers and supervisors deal and run both kinds of games, these rules get mixed up a lot. Know which game you're in.

The oversized chip rule

We don't want to force a player to raise who didn't mean to. A player at 1/2 might sit down with three black chips because they don't want to go to the cashier. They know if they throw one in to call, the dealer will make change. So the rules lean toward calling.

  • Facing a bet or a blind, a single chip with no declaration is a call, no matter how big it is. Announce it: "Call." That includes a player's last chip. If they meant to go all in with it, they had to say so.
  • To raise with an oversized chip, the player has to say "raise" before the chip hits the table. If they say raise and no amount, the raise is the full value of the chip. They can't decide later after seeing how people react.
  • Not facing a bet, the chip's value plays. Check, check, and a player silently throws out a $25 chip: that's a $25 bet. There's nothing to call, so the oversized chip rule doesn't apply. It's a rule for calling.
  • More than one chip: under the TDA rule, if every chip is needed to make the call (take any one away and it's not enough), it's a call. At 1/2, facing a 6 bet, two red chips is a call. If one chip could be removed and it would still be a call, it's not an oversized chip situation, and the 50% rule decides it.

This is one of the few times you'll see a player trying to raise and the casino not letting them. The small blind wants to make it 26 and throws out a green chip. It's a call. Some casinos allow clarification there, and I have mixed feelings about that. I think it's fine to enforce the call, and an articulate dealer can help an upset player understand that it's pretty universal and that it exists so players without the right chips aren't forced to raise.

The TDA goes further for tournaments: if a player facing a raise clearly pulls back their prior bet chips, they're bound to at least call. I love that, because it puts the responsibility on the player. But a lot of casinos aren't sophisticated enough to have that rule in their cash games, and it takes a lot of manpower to get everyone on the same page.

Answering by chip color with no bet out

I'll have it checked around and then a player throws out a single chip, and a student treats it like the oversized chip rule and says it's the minimum bet. But there's no bet they're facing. Some rooms even have that confused in their rules. When there's no bet, the amount of the chip plays, unless they say something. A $100 chip with "two" is a $2 bet. A $100 chip with nothing said is a $100 bet.

Undercalls

Someone raises to 10, and the next player puts out 5 because they didn't see the raise. Now they say, "Oh, I didn't see it. I meant to fold." Can they take it back?

Not without a supervisor. That bet was on the table right in front of them. Universally, the poker world says it's on them to pay attention. Tell them, "It's been raised to 10, and you have to call 10." If they give you a hard time, let a supervisor decide. Usually they're stuck with the 5. Some rooms make them put in the full 10. Some let them choose to call or forfeit what they put in. Either way, they can't take it back and fold unless your room specifically allows that, and even then it's the supervisor's call. If they just put the rest in, you don't need a supervisor at all.

The TDA (Rule 51) says a verbal "call" in turn commits a player to the full call. A silent undercall is a mandatory full call heads up, and on the opening bet of a betting round multiway (the big blind counts as the opener preflop). In other spots, the tournament director can let the player forfeit the undercall and fold.

  • Announce every raise. One of the biggest reasons you say "raise" out loud every single time is this situation. Maybe the player has headphones on. Maybe they were talking to the person next to them.
  • Use the all-in button. It's a very powerful aid for clarity, and it cuts down undercalls.
  • Protect the muck. You know they might try to throw their hand away to get their money back. If the ruling is that they owe the call, mucking just takes away their chance to win it.

Betting lines and working space

When does a pile of chips become a bet? Players have the right to assemble chips in front of them before they act. Some rooms have a betting line, and anything over it is a bet, but I've noticed the betting line has become less enforced than it used to be. It's turned into more of a guideline. So rooms use other rules:

  • Working space. Within about 10 hands, you get a sense of where every player puts their chips. Wherever a player normally bets is their betting area. If a player who always bets right in front of their stack suddenly counts out chips in that same spot, goes back and forth, and then pushes them farther out, that's a red flag. That's basically a string bet. Call the floor and tell them exactly what you saw: "Every time they bet, they bet right here. This time they cut out a bunch of chips in that same spot, went back and forth, and then pushed them out here."
  • The cards as a betting line. Some rooms say a player can stage chips all they want behind their cards, and anything past the cards is a bet.

One manual I've used says a player has made a bet if they push chips forward and release them, or release chips far enough out that it's obvious they meant to bet, and that a player who lets the dealer pull the chips in without objecting has made a wager. That language is for supervisors. As a dealer, any unclear situation is a floor call. Your part is knowing how it works where you deal, and being able to say what happened.

Heads-up exceptions

Some cash games allow things heads up that they wouldn't allow with three or more players, like a player showing one card, or talking about the hand, because it only affects the two players in it. Some rooms still allow that, and I'm fine with a casino that does. In a tournament, it's never allowed. Every hand affects every other hand, so a tournament is one big game, and exposing cards usually gets at least a one-round penalty.

A lot of rooms have stopped allowing it in cash games too. Players see someone do it heads up, think it's cool, and do it multiway, and they don't know the difference. With a third player in the hand, it's a violation of one player per hand.

How to learn these rules

  • Learn the common way first, and know the others exist. Once you pass an audition, the room will still hand you a manual and expect you to read it, because they know dealing isn't universal.
  • Always know the current raise amount. You can't apply the 50% rule or the minimum raise without it.
  • Rewatch the parts that confuse you. It took me years as a dealer, supervisor and shift manager, and teaching it to dealers and supervisors, to get this into my head. The first time it comes up for real, the panic might make you learn it for life. Until then, if you forget, call the floor.

Common questions about poker betting rules

What is the minimum raise in no-limit hold'em?

The call amount plus the previous full raise. If the bet is 9 and the last raise was 3, the minimum raise is to 12. It's not "double the bet," except when there's only been one bet.

Does an all-in for less than a full raise reopen the betting?

In most rooms, no. It doesn't reopen the betting for players who already acted on the current bet. Players who haven't acted on it yet can still raise. In limit, an all-in for half a raise or more counts as a raise.

What is a string bet?

Putting chips out, then going back for more without having announced the amount or said "raise" first. A bet is one forward motion. "I call and raise" is a verbal string bet.

What happens if a player puts in more than a call but less than a raise?

In no-limit, if it's 50% of a raise or more, they have to make a full minimum raise. If it's less, it's a call and they get the extra back. Rooms differ at exactly 50%.

What is the oversized chip rule?

Facing a bet, a single chip thrown in without saying anything is a call, no matter its value. To raise with it, the player has to say "raise" before it hits the table. When there's no bet, the chip's value is the bet.

If a player says "I raise 20," is that 20 more?

In the enormous majority of rooms, no. "Raise" with an amount means the total bet is that amount.

Can a player take back chips if they didn't see a raise?

Not without a supervisor. It's on the player to see what's on the table. Depending on the room, they'll have to call the full amount, or call or forfeit what they put in.
