Straddles, Missed Blinds & the Button

How the button moves is one of the hardest parts of dealing, and a lot of dealers never learn it all the way. Here's where the button goes, what a player owes after missing blinds, how buying the button works, and how straddles change the hand.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 16, 2026 · 16 min read

How the button moves is one of the hardest parts of dealing. It doesn't really matter for the audition. A lot of dealers learn it on the job, and even more of them never end up learning it all the way. It's confusing because you have to think about the next hand, not just the one you're dealing.

Most of this only happens in cash games, because in a cash game each hand stands alone and players can get up and skip their blinds (that idea is explained in cash game procedures). Every room has its own version of these rules. I'll tell you what's most common, where rooms differ, and what I'd do. When your poker room tells you something different, do what they say.

Moving the button

At the end of every hand: push the pot, move the button, drop the rake. In that order, right away. I personally struggled for about two years with forgetting where the button was, because I never built the habit of moving it right after the push. Build it from day one.

  • The button follows the blinds. It moves one seat, to where the small blind was.
  • A dead button. If the player who was the small blind busts or leaves, the button still goes to that empty seat. That's a dead button. The small blind and big blind go to the next players in order.
  • The button can't skip ahead. Moving it past an empty seat to the next player would skip somebody's big blind.
  • The same player can have the button twice. It happens, and it confuses players. A bomb pot is a separate game, so the button doesn't move for it (see bomb pots). I've seen one player get the button three hands in a row, and years later he still argues with me about it.
  • Tournaments going heads-up. When it goes from three-handed to heads-up, let the big blind be the correct big blind, and the button goes to the other player.

What I correct in class

Forgetting to move the button

This is one of the corrections I make most: a student pushes the pot and goes straight to cleaning up the board, or to the rake, and the button sits there. Push, button, rake. Every single hand.

Skipping the button ahead

When I set up a missed blind situation, a student will move the button past the empty seat to the next player. It looks tidy, but it skips a big blind. I let students try to figure these out first, because then it makes more sense to them later.

Missed blind buttons

In a raked cash game, you don't have to play your blinds. If you get up, or you tell the dealer "deal me out," you don't get dealt in. When your blind comes around, you get a missed blind button.

  • Who gets one. If a player is sitting in their seat, you deal them in, even if they're facing the other way. You don't ask. If they're clearly away from the table, you don't deal them in, and you don't ask. If it's their blind, they get a missed blind button. Only in the in-between case, like a player standing at their seat, do you clarify. And you don't stop and wait for blinds. You say "Blinds, guys," and you deal. That's not necessarily how dealer schools teach it, but I promise you that's what the casinos want.
  • Missed small and missed big. Rooms have a missed small blind button and a missed big blind button. If yours doesn't, you've always got a spacer. Use a clear spacer, remember what it means, and tell the next dealer.
  • When to give it. As you deal past their seat, or right before the hand. Either one's fine.
  • Put it right in front of their seat. I keep missed blind buttons right there, so it's a real easy cue not to deal them in.
  • Both blinds empty? If both seats that would be the blinds are empty, both players get a missed blind button.

A missed small blind means a big blind only

Anytime you give out a missed small blind button, that same hand you're going to have just one blind: a big blind, and no small blind. Then the button has to go somewhere next hand. It's more common to move the button onto that empty seat, where it was supposed to go, so the button is where the dead small blind was. Some places just leave the button where it is. I don't really care which one you do. What you can't do is skip the button ahead.

What a player owes when they come back

Missed blind buttons don't mean the player pays a penalty. They mean the player has to make up what they skipped to get back into the game. First, two words you'll hear: live money goes in front of the player and counts as their bet. Dead money goes in the middle and isn't part of their bet.

  • Missed big blind: post both. Whenever you see a missed big blind button in 1/2, you can assume it's $3: $2 in front of them as a live big blind, and $1 in the middle as a dead small. In 2/5 PLO it's $7: $5 live and $2 dead. Because it's live, if it comes back to them unraised, they get the option to check or raise, just like a big blind.
  • The regular blinds still post. This isn't replacing the blind. This is them getting back into the hand.
  • Missed small blind only: post the small, dead. "I need a dollar." It's dead money in the middle, not part of their bet.
  • Or wait. A player with a missed blind button can wait for their big blind to come around and just come in for $2.
  • Coming back on the button. Traditionally, and that's about 95% of the poker world, a player who paid the big blind but not the small can't get a hand on the button. In order to get the button, you have to pay the blinds. Some rooms, including the ones in Texas, let them post the dead small and play.
  • Straddling in. In some rooms, a player with a missed blind can straddle instead of posting, and you don't make them pay the dead small. If the button also wants to straddle, the button has priority, and the returning player can't.

Dead money and the pot in pot-limit

In pot-limit games, most of the industry doesn't count a dead small posted for missed blinds when you figure the pot preflop. It's the same way tournaments don't count the ante. I think you should count it, but you don't. (Money a player put in and then folded is different. That does count.)

Mixing up $2 and $3

Students confuse a player who owes a big blind after a seat change ($2) with a player who missed their big blind ($3). They're different situations. The seat change is just a big blind, with no dead small. It's covered below.

Buying the button

Here's the situation: a player is sitting between the button and the small blind. Maybe they just came back, maybe they're new. They haven't paid any blinds, so normally they'd sit out one hand. Most rooms, not all, let them buy the button instead.

  1. They post a live big blind and a dead small. In 2/5, that's "I need $7."
  2. The blinds take their bets back. The buyer is the only blind that hand.
  3. Next hand, the button goes to them and the blinds continue just like they were going to. No interruption.

Normally, only a player with the button on their right and the small blind on their left can buy the button.

What I say to a new player sitting there: "Would you like to buy the button?" If they look confused: "Or you can wait one hand and play for free." For this one, have them post before you deal, so it's clear to the whole table. And once the cards are pitched, it's too late. If they ask after the cards are around: "At that point, no."

The dead button and last position

There's a rule in poker where you can't come in in last position. A new player can't sit down behind a dead button and just get dealt in, because they'd get the button without paying the blinds. People get this one wrong all the time.

Some rooms handle it by moving the button back to the empty seat, where it technically sort of is anyway, and letting the new player buy the button from there. If my poker room allows it, I'd rather give the player that opportunity instead of forcing them to wait. Ask if they'd like to buy the button, or wait one.

Other rooms do other things. One option is to let the new player be the big blind only. The button stays put, and the same player has it twice, which is fine, though some rooms don't like it because players find it confusing. At Texas Card House, if you want to buy the button, you buy the button: two big blinds go out and both get an option, and the next hand the buyer pays the small blind. They made that up. Do I care? Not even a little.

Two players coming back at once

If two players with missed blinds come back at the same time, the first chance to buy the button goes to the one closest to where the button goes next. If they say no, offer it to the second. If the first player buys it, a lot of rooms make the second one sit out that hand. (Offer it in the wrong order and the first player ends up posting behind the button, which isn't fair to them.) In the room I'm at, the second player can straddle in, then post the next hand.

Tournaments

You can't buy the button in a tournament. A player assigned to the button seat gets dealt in nowadays. If they can get unlucky and draw the big blind, they should be able to get lucky and draw the button. A player who arrives between the button and the blinds waits: "I'll get you the next hand."

Seat changes and posting

A player can change seats in a cash game, but they can't use it to dodge their big blind. If they move away from the big blind and skip over three or more active players, they have to post a big blind or wait for their natural big blind. Usually, if they skip over two active players or less, they don't. General rule of thumb: three or more, you do. Two or less, you don't. Rooms vary. I've seen a room that made players post at two, which I think is overkill, and some rooms only make you post when you skip more than three.

  • It's not a missed blind. A big blind posted after a seat change is just a big blind. There's no dead small. If the player doesn't want to post, they still get a missed blind button, but it only means they owe a big blind.
  • Telling the player. I used to warn players before they moved. I stopped doing that. They're all grown-ups. Now I just say, "Hey, I need $2," before they get a card.
  • As a new dealer, always make them post. Me, I might not make a big player late at night post for $2. You're not there yet.
  • A small blind who moves before the cards are dealt. The blinds aren't locked in until a card is dealt, so the move is allowed. The most professional way is a live small blind in their new seat. Less sophisticated rooms make it dead. My reasoning for live: they're already giving up the benefit of the button. The button goes in the seat they left.

Missed blinds are a little bit silly in my opinion. Private games don't care about them. The rule exists so nobody can keep skipping their blinds and coming back after the button for free.

The seat-change posting rule is like killing an ant with a sledgehammer. Somebody once figured out they could keep moving away from the big blind, so now there's a rule for it. But drop the rule, and after about a year, people will start taking advantage.

Straddles

A straddle is a voluntary blind, only in cash games. It's like a third blind, generally double the big blind. In 2/5, that's a $10 straddle. In 1/2, a lot of rooms use $5 instead of $4, just so you're not making as much change.

  • How the action works. The action starts to the left of the straddle, and the straddler gets the last action preflop instead of the big blind. If nobody raises, the straddler can still raise. That's the difference between a straddle and a raise: a player who raises after looking doesn't get to raise again if everyone just calls.
  • When it has to be announced. The player puts the chips out or says they're straddling. Most casinos say it has to be before the first card is pitched to anyone at the table. Some places let it go until that player receives their first card. Some rooms allow it any time before the action, which is scary. At Texas Card House, it's until the last card comes off the deck, as long as the player hasn't looked, and the table polices it.
  • Err on the side of caution. As a new dealer, if you can't verify the straddle was in time, deny it: "I don't think you did, so I can't let you straddle." Requests after the cards are out get "No straddles allowed."
  • Where you can straddle. Depending on the room, under the gun only, under the gun or the button, or from anywhere. It's probably more common nowadays to allow it under the gun or on the button. The Texas Card House rooms only allow it under the gun.
  • Priority. If two players try to straddle the same hand, the one in the best position gets it, so the button always gets priority.
  • Button straddle. When the button straddles, the action starts in the small blind.
  • Re-straddles. Some rooms allow them and some don't. Find out where you deal.

Straddles and raise sizes

In no-limit and pot-limit, the straddle works like a new big blind for raising. With a $5 straddle, the minimum raise is to 10. A player who silently puts out 7 has called, because it's less than half a raise. A player who puts out 8 has to complete the raise to 10. The full rules are in the technical rules of betting. In a limit game, the straddle isn't a new betting increment and doesn't count toward the bet and three raises. In 2/4 with a straddle, the cap goes to 10.

One more thing you'll see: a player who misses the chance to straddle often just raises to the straddle amount. And if a player asks another player, "Do you want me to raise so you can straddle?" that's talking about the action. One player per hand.

Announce anything unusual as you pitch

When the blinds are anything other than normal, say it while you deal: "We've got a dead small blind here, the live small blind moved over here, you're first to act." Or, "Seat six is buying the button, seat seven is straddling, it'll be five bucks." Otherwise a player bets 7 thinking it's legal, and it feels bad to be you. And if a player says they're the big blind and posts by mistake, most of the time you give it back. Some rooms make them call.

Kill pots in limit games

How do you make limit games more fun? Kill pots. In a 10/20 kill game, when one player wins a pot over a set amount, say $200 (some places $100), the kill is on, and the next hand is played at 20/40. A half kill makes it 15/30. Chopped pots usually don't count. The winner gets the kill button and posts a blind of $20, the big blind for the 20/40 hand. The action usually stays in the normal order, though some rooms start it to the left of the kill. Some hold'em games use a "leg up," where you have to win two pots in a row. In split games like Omaha eight or better, it's usually scooping a pot over a set amount. It rarely comes up on auditions. A kill pot is also one of the few exceptions to a player being able to leave after any hand.

When you're not sure what the room does

Missed blinds aren't really on auditions, and every room does them a little differently. The good news is that players often know the house policy. It's okay to ask the table or call the floor: "Guys, I actually forget what we do here." And if a room does something you weren't taught, don't tell them their way is wrong. Say, "That's another way to do it."

How to practice button movement

  • Move the button every hand you practice. Push, button, rake, out loud, until you don't have to think about it.
  • Set up the empty seats. Take a player out of the small blind, out of the big blind, and out of both, and decide where the button goes and who owes what next hand.
  • Bring players back. A missed big blind ($3), a missed small blind ($1 dead), a player waiting for their big blind, a new player between the button and the small blind, and two players coming back at once.
  • Add a straddle. Practice where the action starts and what a min raise is.

Common questions about blinds and the button

What is a dead button in poker?

When the player who would get the button has left or busted, the button still moves to that empty seat. Nobody has it that hand, and the blinds go to the next players in order.

What does a player post after missing the big blind?

Usually a live big blind in front of them and a dead small blind in the middle. In 1/2 that's $3 total. Or they can wait for their big blind to come around and come in for $2.

What does it mean to buy the button?

A player sitting between the button and the small blind posts a live big blind and a dead small blind so they can play right away. The blinds take their bets back that hand, and the button goes to the buyer next hand. Not every room allows it.

Can you be dealt in behind the dead button?

No. You can't come into last position without paying the blinds. Depending on the room, the player waits, buys the button, or the room has its own workaround.

What is a straddle in poker?

A voluntary blind, usually double the big blind, posted before the cards are dealt. Action starts to the left of it, and the straddler acts last preflop and can raise if nobody else does.

Can you straddle on the button?

In some rooms. Rooms allow straddles under the gun only, under the gun or the button, or anywhere. When the button straddles, the action starts in the small blind.

Do you have to post after changing seats?

If you move away from the big blind and skip three or more active players, usually yes: post a big blind or wait for it. Two or fewer, usually no. Rooms vary.
