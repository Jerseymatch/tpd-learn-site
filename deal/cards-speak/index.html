Cards Speak and One Player Per Hand

Every rule in poker ties back to two ideas. Each person plays on their own, and the only thing that wins a pot is the cards. Learn these two and you'll make better decisions (and explain them better) in situations nobody trained you for.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 15, 2026 · 20 min read

Poker has a ton of rules. Every single one of them, at the end of the day, is tied to two fundamental rules: one player per hand and cards speak. Even the way we keep the bank exists to facilitate a game built on those two ideas.

Here's why that matters to you as a dealer. Dealing well takes a lot of interpretation. It's not just telling a player what the rule is. It's deciding whether the rule applies at all, in a situation that doesn't look exactly like anything you learned. No procedure manual exists that doesn't need to be interpreted. When you get out there, you're going to run into spots where you don't know exactly how to apply a rule. If you anchor your thinking to these two rules, you'll make better decisions, and you'll explain them in a way players accept.

What "one player per hand" means

Every person who comes to the poker table comes on their own behalf. The rule says "player," but it really means one person per hand. Each person at the table has to play, as far as the game can enforce it, without getting information or help with a decision from anybody else at the table, or from anybody else in the world. A player can learn whatever they want away from the table. Once they're in a hand, they play it completely on their own. (That's why cell phone use is such a tricky topic: whoever made the website a player is reading is, in effect, helping them.)

And it's on each player to uphold it. Poker gives everyone rules to follow, but every individual at the table is responsible for making sure they're playing on their own. When they do something that violates it, they put themselves at risk.

A player who flashes their cards

A great example, and one I've seen over and over: a player looks at their hand lazily, lifting the cards so the people next to them can see. It's often someone who played a lot at home, or who plays games like bridge where you hold your cards up. In their mind, they're just looking at their cards. In a casino, it matters. It violates the rule in two directions. Anyone next to them who's still in the hand is now getting information that the rest of the table isn't. And if somebody who's seen the cards says something to help, the player's hand can be killed.

What you do depends on who could have seen them:

  • Someone still in the hand may have seen the cards. "May have" is the standard. You don't guess whether they actually did. You expose the hand right away, whether or not anyone says anything, so everyone in the hand is making decisions with the same information. You don't need to call the floor for this. Tell the player something like, "You showed your cards to people who are in the hand, so we're going to make it so everyone can see them."
  • Only people who aren't in the hand saw them (a folded player or a spectator, which is the same thing). You don't expose the hand automatically, because it doesn't affect the hand being played. But if anyone at the table asks to see those cards, put them aside and expose them after the hand is over. That's what "show one, show all" means. It's on request.

That's the way I believe is most common. Some rooms will have you expose them right away. If that's where you work, you do what they say.

Players who show a neighbor and then fold often aren't happy when someone asks to see the cards, and they'll try to slide them into the muck fast. As soon as someone asks, protect your muck (hand right on it) and get those cards from the player so they can be shown. And if you get resistance on any of this, call a supervisor. Don't force your way through it.

Also make sure cards stay on the table. If they come up above the rail, get the player to bring them back down. If they go below the rail, that's a floor call. In many places it kills the hand, because there's no way to know they're the same cards.

What I correct in class

Handling a new player who flashes their hand

A new player is going to do all kinds of things without realizing what they're doing, and holding their cards up is one of the first. Address it the first time you see it: "Can you put those down so no one else can see them?" The terminology is that every player has to protect their hand. If they're right next to other players, you may even need to show them how to look at their cards without revealing them, and I've plenty of times called a supervisor over between hands to help with that. It should be handled within a hand or two. If it keeps happening, let a supervisor know, because it slows the game down and hurts its integrity. (Sometimes the player is a little spacey, or a little drunk, and that's how you find out.)

Someone else helps a player in the hand

A player who shows their live hand to the person behind them is putting themselves at real risk. Picture a big pot on the river: the player is facing an all-in, they're hemming and hawing like they're about to fold, and the person behind them, who's seen their cards, says "You should call, they don't have it." That's a floor call. And because the player chose to show their hand and give that person the ability to help them, there's a pretty good chance the supervisor kills the hand, even if they try to call. It's not universal, but it's a common ruling. It's a totally different situation if the player wasn't interacting with that person at all. You can't penalize someone acting in good faith because somebody near them spoke up.

If one player at the table starts advising another during a hand, you step in and say, "One player per hand." But if the person giving advice has seen the player's cards (they showed a neighbor, and the neighbor says "you should bet"), it's not a simple reprimand. That's a floor call.

Talking about the board

Four clubs on the board, and someone at the table says, "Ooh, does anyone have the last club?" That's not okay. Nowadays it's universally accepted that no one at the table, and no one who isn't in the hand, can say anything that gives away information about what the hand might be. Maybe a player in the hand didn't notice the four clubs. That's on them. They're supposed to figure it out on their own. People love announcing a possible straight flush. That excitement comes from table games, but in poker it gives away information.

All you can do is tell them, "Hey, you can't talk about the board." If they get the message, you're in great shape. If they keep doing it, you get a supervisor involved, either right away or by telling one after you leave the table so it causes less confrontation. Rooms differ in how strictly they enforce this, and the culture can be lenient. But it's a better approach to enforce the rules first and learn that your room is lenient than the other way around. And if it's innocent, remember you're there to educate people too. Don't create unnecessary tension.

What I correct in class

How to address board talk

When I set up this situation in class, a player comes back to the table and asks, "Who's got the spade over there?" The one player per hand rule includes not talking about the board in a way that brings attention to it, because someone who didn't realize a fourth spade came out might now realize they need to check their hand for one spade. The way a poker player makes money in the long run is not by winning pots where somebody didn't realize they had a single spade. The one thing we don't want is for nothing to be said.

Questions you can and can't answer

  • "Who bet?" Every player has the right to get clarification about the action in front of them while it's still on the table. If the chips are out there, tell them. But once the bets have been brought in and the next card is out, that information is no longer available. Answering would be helping that player, so you say, "I can't answer that, we're on the next street." If they give you a hard time, call the floor.
  • "How much is in the pot?" In no-limit hold'em, you're not allowed to answer that. It's only in pot limit that you do. You say, "I'm not allowed to tell you that."
  • "What's on the board?" If a player can't read the board, you're not supposed to tell them what the cards are. That one can be touchy. If it's a player with vision problems, and it feels like a sensitive situation, call the floor.

When the dealer flashes a card

Some dealers, if a card flashes on the pitch, just keep going because they don't want to deal with it. That drives me crazy. If a card flashed enough that you, or anybody, could have seen some of it, then by ignoring it you're violating one player per hand yourself. Go the integrity route even when it's unpopular, even if there's a minute left in the high hand period and the table wants you to hurry. You're there to be the integrity of the game. If you don't maintain it, nobody will. (How to handle an exposed card on the deal is covered in how to pitch cards.)

Cash games vs. tournaments

Everything above is for cash games, where each hand is unique and separate, and a player can leave whenever they want. A tournament is one ongoing event. So if a player shows anybody their cards in a tournament, even a neighbor while they're folding, it can get a penalty in a lot of rooms. Know whether your room penalizes any exposed hand in a tournament. If it does, you handle it aggressively: announce the cards were exposed, call a supervisor, and expose them at the point the rules call for.

What "cards speak" means

One player per hand means each player plays completely on their own. Cards speak means the only thing that entitles a player to win is their cards. Nothing else in the game does. Without cards, you can't win at poker.

When the betting is over, all a player has to do to win, if their cards are best, is put them face up on the table. Once they do that, the cards speak for themselves. The dealer can't kill that hand. Another player can't kill that hand. And just like everyone is responsible for one player per hand, everyone at the table has the right, and the responsibility, to speak up if a tabled hand is supposed to win. If the dealer reads a hand as a flush when one of the cards is actually a different suit, and the player who should win isn't paying attention, anyone who notices is supposed to say, "That's not a flush. That's a pair of aces, and that hand should win."

Think of it as the cards speaking as if they were their own people. We do that for integrity. The last thing you want in a casino is to give the dealer the power to make somebody win who didn't actually win.

What "tabling" a hand means

Cards only speak when they're face up, flat on the felt. The game has to define what "speaking" is. A player who holds their cards up to show them, and then mucks them, never tabled their hand, so their cards never spoke.

That creates a tricky spot for you. Say two players have tabled their hands and a third is just sitting there holding their cards. You can't tell them to table their hand, because that would be helping them and it violates one player per hand. But as long as they're holding live cards, you can't award the pot either. You're stuck waiting. If nothing happens, you can say, "It's the showdown." If they ask what you mean, then you can explain: to show a hand at the showdown, you lay it face up on the table.

The same thinking explains why players who toss their cards in at the showdown are taking a chance. I've seen it more than 30 times: a card bounces off the edge and goes right off the table. That's a floor call. Some rooms rule the card dead because it never got tabled. Others try to find out whether it's 100% retrievable. Either way, the surveillance cameras are over the table, not under it. When a player does this, I'll tell them with a smile, "Totally get it. But look, you can just put them down right here." It's hard for them to be mad at you for that.

What I correct in class

Touching a tabled hand

Two things I correct. First, if a player flips their hand over when you didn't ask them to, don't turn it back over. Some dealers make that mistake, and it violates cards speak. Focus on the hands you asked to see. Second, you're not really supposed to touch a player's tabled cards or spread them yourself, unless you need to for some reason, which is pretty rare. That's true in Omaha too. It's a little hardcore, but it is a rule, because it violates one player per hand. Read the hand, and if you can't, ask the player to table it better.

When the pot goes to the wrong player

Cards speak is so strong that even after the pot's been pushed, if someone speaks up that a tabled hand was actually the winner, you call the floor. Even if it was three hands later. Even if it was ten hands later. It's up to the floor to decide how to handle it. There is a limit, set by each casino. Some say it can be brought up until the first card of the next hand is pitched. Some allow it later. Nobody's going to allow it 400 days later.

If everyone agrees, including the player with the chips in front of them, the supervisor will just give the pot to the right player. If there's any disagreement, it probably goes to surveillance, and the money is put aside until they figure it out. (Most poker rooms have three cameras on every table.) If the pot is already mixed into the wrong player's stack, they'll work out how much was bet on every street. The money comes from the player who wasn't supposed to win it, not from the house, because that player was never entitled to it.

Two more situations:

  • The player tables their hand, then mucks it on their own. In some casinos, tabled is tabled and the cards speak. In others, by mucking at the showdown they may lose their right to the pot. Learn which way your room goes.
  • The dealer misreads the hand, and then the player mucks. Now the player got bad information from the dealer, which is its own violation of one player per hand. They tabled in good faith, so there's a reasonable chance the hand ends up live. Call the floor.

Your cards only win what you put in

Cards speak, but not so loudly that they win more than your fair share. A player who's all in for less is only entitled to the money they were able to match. Everything beyond that is a side pot between the other players, and that player's cards can't win it. (The full procedure is in how to build side pots.)

Protecting your hand

Because cards are the only thing that entitles a player to win, every player has to protect their right for their cards to speak. That's what "protect your hand" really means. There are three ways to do it:

  • A chip on top of the cards.
  • A card cover, which can be anything that goes on top of the cards. Greg Raymer, the 2004 WSOP Main Event champion, was known as FossilMan because he used to sell fossils as card covers.
  • Your hand on top of them. If you go all in and don't have a card cover, leave your hand on your cards.

When a hand is protected, you can tell which cards are which 100% of the time, even if another player's folded cards fly across them. When it isn't, and folded cards land on it, nobody can be sure. What if those two players know each other, and one folded into the other on purpose to give them a new card? Very unlikely, but possible. So the floor has to decide whether the cards are live or dead.

When I play poker, as soon as I decide I'm going to participate in a hand, I cover my cards. I do it 100% of the time. Even in the World Series Main Event, I can't tell you how many times I've seen a player go all in for big money without protecting their hand, and then a dealer takes it. Why? Because it was unprotected, and that's what dealers do. They take cards. At a nine-handed table, a dealer takes eight sets of two cards to the muck every hand. They're trained to do it over and over.

When a dealer mucks a live hand

As the dealer, you don't decide anything here. Protected or unprotected, you stop the hand and call the floor. And know the answer to the first question the supervisor is going to ask you: was the hand protected? When I came to a table as a supervisor, that's what I asked first, and it was very frustrating when the dealer didn't know.

For anyone running a home game, here's what's typical nowadays:

  • Unprotected hand: a lot of poker rooms give back the bet the player has in front of them on that betting round, and nothing from earlier streets. If it's the river and they've put $300 in the pot and their $15 call gets mucked, they get their $15 back. For a long time the rule was they got nothing back, because it was on them to protect their hand. It's less common nowadays, because unprotected hands are such a booby trap for players.
  • Protected hand: the player gets back every dollar they put into the pot. It's very rare. In my ten-plus years working in poker, supervising and shift managing, I've seen a protected hand get mucked maybe once or twice.

Once cards are in the muck, they're gone. That's part of why the muck is built the way it is: the cards are irretrievable. If you ever see a supervisor go into the muck to fish out a player's cards, don't tell me about it. It's one of the worst practices I've ever heard of, because there's no way to guarantee it's done with integrity.

What I correct in class

Beating yourself up over a mucked hand, or guessing on a fouled one

When a student tells me they mucked an unprotected hand at work, I ask them how many hands they think I've folded in my career. I actually calculated it once on a livestream: something like seven to ten million hands, two cards at a time. When a hand is unprotected, emotionally, I don't feel anything if I take it. You can't feel bad about it, because dealers are programmed to take face-down cards sitting on the table. (The one place I'd be more careful is a private home game, where there aren't always new people coming through.) What you do is call the floor. The player's going to be annoyed. Stay level.

The other half: when I set up folded cards landing on an unprotected hand in class, students said they'd call the floor, but that if they knew for sure which cards were which, they could just muck them. As a new dealer, for your first six months to a year, every single time cards land on another player's hand like that, call the floor.

How to explain a ruling to a player

Compare two ways to tell a player you have to expose the cards they showed their neighbor. If you just state the rule ("You showed it to him, so I have to expose it"), they can come right back at you: "He's not in the hand, it shouldn't matter." Now they feel singled out, or like the other player is needling them.

If you tie it back to the fundamental rule ("You showed another player your cards, and that violates the one player per hand rule, which is fundamental in poker, so we have a way to handle that"), it gives them perspective. They can't really argue that it doesn't violate one player per hand. Same with board talk: "Why can't I talk about the board?" Because every person has to play on their own and make decisions without help from anybody else, and our rules protect that.

This matters more than it seems, for a few reasons:

  • You have a huge guest service responsibility. Your job is to make sure the players want to come back next time. If you upset a player and it was avoidable, the whole casino might lose that player because of you. Sometimes people just don't know any better.
  • Other dealers won't always enforce things the same way, even in well-run rooms. A player may have seen nothing happen at another dealer's table and feel you're being unfair. Tying it to the rule means you never have to talk about the other dealer: "I understand it's not intentional, but I do need to enforce the rule."
  • It keeps things impersonal. If you notice players who might be playing together, you have to handle it delicately. People take a lot of offense when you call them out, especially if they're not actually doing it. A fundamental rule is not personal.

What I correct in class

Explaining the reason, not just the rule

The reason I talk about one player per hand so much early on is that it helps you communicate in difficult spots. When a player pushes back on a ruling, you say, "Okay, let me break it down for you," and you explain how the ruling keeps things fair for everybody. Tying your explanation back to these rules is what makes it so powerful. It can be incredibly effective, and a lot of situations call for it.

When you're not sure: call the floor

These rules can also help you decide whether to do something. If you're not sure, ask yourself whether what just happened might violate one player per hand or cards speak.

And when you're new and still not sure, the absolute safest way to handle it is to call the floor. Integrity is always there when you call the floor. Some players might give you a hard time. The supervisor might tell you that you did a great job by calling, or that you didn't need to call for that one but you protected the game. Either way, nothing bad happens. It's not like every table you sit at needs a floor call, either. A decent dealer calls the floor zero to four times over the course of a day. When I was dealing eight-hour shifts, I averaged maybe once or twice a day.

In almost every case, when you call the floor, you stop the hand in progress.

Learn how your room handles them

I teach what's more universal, but several of these rulings vary from casino to casino, and you need to know what they are where you work:

  • Whether cards shown to a player who isn't in the hand get exposed right away, or only on request after the hand.
  • Whether a player who tables their hand and then mucks it still has a live hand.
  • How long after a pot is pushed someone can say the wrong player got it.
  • Whether a card that goes off the table at the showdown is dead.
  • Whether any exposed hand in a tournament gets a penalty.
  • How strictly the room enforces talking about the board.

And remember, whatever the people you work for tell you supersedes anything I say.

Common questions about cards speak and one player per hand

What does "cards speak" mean in poker?

The only thing that entitles a player to win is their cards. Once a hand is face up on the table at the showdown, it speaks for itself. The dealer can't kill it, and anyone at the table who sees the winning hand being misread should speak up.

What does "one player per hand" mean?

Every person at the table plays on their own, with no information or help from anyone else, at the table or away from it. That covers other players, people standing behind them, and phones.

What does "show one, show all" mean?

If a player shows their cards to someone at the table who isn't in the hand, and another player asks to see them, the dealer puts those cards aside and exposes them to everyone after the hand. Some rooms expose them right away.

Can players talk about the board?

No. Nobody, in the hand or not, can say anything that gives away what the hand might be, like asking who has the last club. Tell them they can't talk about the board. If it keeps happening, involve a supervisor.

Does a hand have to be tabled to win?

Yes. Cards speak when they're face up, flat on the felt. A player who holds their cards up and then mucks them never tabled their hand.

What happens if the dealer mucks my hand?

The dealer calls the floor. In a lot of rooms today, if your hand wasn't protected, you get back the bet you had out on that street. If it was protected with a chip, card cover or your hand, you get back every dollar you put in the pot.

Can the dealer tell me how much is in the pot?

Not in no-limit hold'em, because it violates one player per hand. Pot-limit games are the exception.
