How to Deal a Hand of Texas Hold'em

A full hand of 1/2 no-limit, from tapping in to the mini wash. The order things happen in, what you're watching at every moment, and the habits a casino expects from a new dealer on day one.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 15, 2026 · 18 min read

This is where everything comes together: the shuffle, the pitch, chips, change and the board, in the order they actually happen in a hand of 1/2 no-limit hold'em. The goal is simple. You should be able to sit down and deal a whole hand smoothly, without stopping to think about what comes next.

I'm teaching this from the casino perspective. For about ten years I spent most of my time as a supervisor and shift manager, bringing in new dealers and training them to deal the way my casino expected. Dealer schools don't always teach that, because they're focused on passing an audition. I want you to pass your audition too, and I also want you to know what it's like to be received into a real poker room.

One rule above all of this. If you work at a casino, whatever the people you work for tell you is right, and it supersedes anything I say. I can think of so many dealers who got in trouble for telling a supervisor that what they learned in dealer school was different. Supervisor, shift manager, poker director: always right. School: always wrong, if it comes to that.

Getting to the table: tapping in and out

A down is usually about 30 minutes. When it's your turn to take over a table:

  • Tap the dealer gently on the shoulder, almost like a check motion: two light taps. Don't startle them, don't hit them hard, and please don't massage people as your way of tapping in. Most places tap in from the left, and the outgoing dealer exits to the right.
  • Lock in from the moment you get to the table. While you wait for the hand to finish, look at the chip stacks (once in a while a player takes advantage of the dealer change to add money to their stack), see where the button is, and follow the hand. If the casino wants you to count the bank when you sit down, count it over the dealer's shoulder while you wait, so that job is already done.
  • The tap means "last hand." In most casinos I've worked in, if you get tapped before you've started the riffle, the hand you're on is your last one. If you reach the riffle, or the deck goes into the shuffle machine, before the tap, you deal that next hand too.
  • The outgoing dealer owns the bank until the new dealer is sitting down. Getting tapped doesn't mean you're free to go. Sometimes the person who tapped you gets called away to do something else and doesn't tell you. Keep your eyes on the bank until you actually see a dealer sit in. I've seen someone get fired for this.
  • Greet the table and get right to dealing. "Hey guys, how's it going?" in whatever way fits your personality. You might ask if the table has seen a high hand. Then swap in the other deck and start, with no waiting. Time is money. You'll chat between hands, not while a hand is being played.

The blinds, and why you don't wait for them

In 1/2 no-limit, the small blind is $1 and the big blind is $2. White (or gray) chips are $1 and red chips are $5. The blinds rotate around the table every hand, and they exist to create action. If nobody ever had to put money in blind, every player would just wait for aces. Because the small blind and big blind have money in the pot with a random hand, there's a reason to play, and the first betting round has an amount to call that's more than zero.

If the blinds aren't out when it's time to deal, say "Blinds, guys," but keep pitching. In a 1/2 no-limit cash game, you can just start dealing. When you come around the second time and give the big blind their second card, that's when you deal preventatively: if the blind still isn't out, a quick "Big blind" lets them know. If the big blind doesn't know they're the big blind, they're very likely to act when it's someone else's turn.

What I correct in class

Stopping to wait for the blinds

This is one I think poker schools get wrong. Students stop the pitch and sit there waiting for a player to post the blind, when they could be pitching cards out the whole time. In a cash game you don't need to wait every time. Keep the cards moving, and only stop for the big blind once they have both cards.

One more thing while you're pitching: you never put the deck down during a hand. I can only think of one situation where you'd ever have to, and it has to do with something going on with your hand. (For the mechanics of the deal itself, see how to pitch cards.)

Following the action

Watch every card land, and make sure the player you're pitching to is the one who secures it. You're responsible for everything on the table.

The moment the player under the gun has their second card, they can act, even though nobody else has two cards yet. Your focus shifts. Their action window, the zone where their chips, their cards and their hands are, becomes your number one priority. You keep pitching to the rest of the table with your peripheral vision, darting your eyes back and forth to make sure players secure their cards, but your main attention is on whoever's turn it is. That's called following the action, and it's tied for the most important thing you do as a dealer.

When you pitch the last card to the button, you engage with the first player fully. If they don't seem aware it's their turn (their back is turned, say), let them know the action is on them. Use players' names whenever you can. You'll get more tips, and everything is smoother. But if a player is thinking, or might be thinking, don't tell them it's their turn. That's needling them, and it's obnoxious.

The rule behind all of it: you need to know with 100% certainty what action was taken before you move on. You're not allowed to guess or put the pieces together. If you're not sure, get clarification, and phrase it so it doesn't suggest an action, because suggesting one breaks the one-player-per-hand rule. If a player's cards are sitting out and they haven't done anything with them, "Have you looked?" is a good way to ask.

Preflop: fold, call or raise

The first player to act has three options: fold, call the $2, or raise. If a player asks you what they can do, give them all of their options. Leaving one out is steering them.

Folds and the muck

A fold is throwing your cards forward, face down, when you're facing a bet. When a player does that, scoop the cards, verify the number of cards (two in hold'em, four in Omaha), and bring them to the muck. If a player pushes their cards forward a little, you can assume that's a fold. But you don't want to kill a hand they didn't mean to fold, so if a player looks at their cards, sets them down and seems to be doing nothing, ask something like "Are you done with those?"

A verbal action is binding over a physical one. If a player facing a bet says "fold," your job is to say, "You said fold, you need to fold your cards." If they give you resistance, that's a floor call. Whenever a player resists a rule, you get a supervisor. That's universal.

How to keep the muck:

  • On your left, by your deck hand, where you can protect it. No player sits on that side, and your resting hand blocks the other side, so nobody can reach into it.
  • Slide folded cards into the middle of the muck, not on top and not on the bottom, so they become indistinguishable.
  • Keep it tight, roughly circular and random. Never squared up. I never want to see a squared muck. In an audition, it makes me instantly uncomfortable, because a squared muck makes it hard to make cards indistinguishable and keeps players from mucking their own cards. Not spread out all over the table either: pull it in with your fingers.

The big blind exception: if no one has raised and the big blind folds because they don't realize they're the big blind, push their cards back to them. It costs them nothing to stay in, so folding makes no sense. Not every casino agrees with that, but in general that's how it's done, and it's a little bit of guest service.

What I correct in class

The pot on top of the muck

Keep the pot and the muck clearly separate. Back in 2011, I failed someone in his audition because his muck was underneath his pot. I didn't know how that happens, but it scared me, so I couldn't give him a dealer job. That was Victor, who over time became COO of Texas Card House. So it didn't end his career, but don't do it.

Calls

A player calls by putting the amount out in front of them, in their betting area, or by saying "call." They shouldn't be throwing chips across the table. Some players use the word "call" in a sentence, like "how much is it to call?" Find out what your casino says about that. A few rooms (it's rare nowadays) say any time a player says "call" or "raise" on their turn, they're bound to it. Discourage players from doing it either way, because it can become an angle.

If the verbal and physical happen at the same time, the verbal wins. A player says "call" while putting out $7 against a $2 bet: that's a call. But if they put the $7 out first and say "call" after, the physical action came first, and it's a raise.

Verify every call. If it's six chips or more, break it down so everyone can see the amount. If it's short, tell them: "I need five more."

Raises

There are four ways to raise: put out a raise amount, say "raise" and then put out the amount, say "raise 15," or just say an amount ("20," and you immediately say "Raise to 20"). If a player says "raise" and then puts out only the call amount first, they can still go back and raise whatever they want, because they said "raise" first.

  • The minimum raise is the amount to call plus the size of the previous raise, not double the bet. If it's $6 and someone raises to $12, the raise was $6, so the next minimum raise is to $18.
  • Half a raise or more gets enforced as a full raise in most houses. If the minimum raise is to $12 and a player puts out $9 or more, you say "Raise to 12" and have them complete it. Check your casino's rule.
  • A single oversized chip with no announcement is a call. And if a player has only red chips, facing $6 they have to throw two. Neither chip alone is enough for the call, so the second one is treated as an oversized chip too. It's a call. Announce it clearly, so nobody at the table thinks it's a raise to $10. (The full oversized chip rule, string bets and minimum raises are in the technical rules of betting.)
  • An all-in is still a type of raise, just with all of the player's chips. If your room uses an all-in button, toss it out and say "all in." I like it, because it gives everyone, including surveillance, a visual.

You announce every bet, every raise and every all-in, every time. The amount to call changed, and you have to let everybody know.

Bringing the bets in before the flop

When everyone has acted on the current amount, bring the bets in. Preflop, you always bring every bet in before the flop, no shortcuts.

  1. Bring in the exact bets first. Go around in whatever order feels natural to you, and keep it consistent.
  2. Leave any oversized chip in front of the player until you have their change in your hand. Deliver the change, then take the chip. Never collect everything and plan on remembering the change later. You will forget.
  3. Count players and the pot as you go. As I bring bets in, I'm thinking "1, 2, 3, 4: four players" and "2, 4, 6, 8, plus $1 is $9." When it's time for the flop, I already know what to announce and what's in the pot.
  4. Make the pot look like a pot. Don't stack it up.

(The full order for making change, and how to break down and verify bets, is in bets, calls and making change.)

Then tap, announce "four players" as you burn, and bring the flop. That whole sequence is in how to bring the flop, turn and river.

What I correct in class

Change before the chip comes in

When a player calls with a $5 chip, students sweep it into the pot and promise themselves they'll come back with change. Sometimes they don't, and now the pot is wrong and a player is short. Same thing with a whole table: one student gave change to one player and brought the flop while the rest of the table was still owed. Change first, then the chip.

The flop: action first, rake second

As soon as the flop is out, two things happen. First and most important, no exceptions, your eyes go to the action window of the first player to act. At the same time, if there's rake, you reach over and take it, subtly. Nobody needs to notice.

Rake structures vary from casino to casino (the different structures, time rakes and the jackpot drop are in rake, promo drop and tips). For practice, I use a $5 maximum taken "on the tens," $1 for every $10 in the pot. So a $9 pot has no rake yet, and a $10 pot is $1. Why take it now, when it's almost definitely going to change? Because sometimes there's no more action, and you don't want to stop everything at the end to take it. Take it as it comes, every street. Following the action still comes first: if players are acting quickly, wait a moment.

Checks

After the flop it's zero to call, so the first player can check or bet. The official way to check is to tap the table twice with an empty hand, or say "check." Anything else (a single tap, a wave, "all set," checking with chips in their hand) needs clarification. Players who bring chips forward when they check are often ruled to have bet in a lot of rooms. You also have to train players, but how you say it matters. "I don't know if you took an action" lands better than "This is the way to check."

When you're starting out, repeat every verbal check out loud so the whole table hears it. If a player checks quietly and you're only 99% sure, ask: "Did you say check?"

The first player to act can check in the dark (before the flop comes out), and after that, the next player can too. Anyone doing it when it isn't their turn is acting out of turn.

If a player folds when it's checked to them, take the cards and muck them, but let them know it isn't good etiquette. Plenty of casinos really frown on it, and in a tournament it can get a penalty.

Bets

In no-limit, a bet can be anything from the big blind (so $2 minimum here) up to everything in front of the player. Players bet the same way they call: chips in their betting area, or an amount said out loud. If a player says "six" and puts out $5 first, announce "Six is the bet" clearly and have them put out the rest.

The turn and the river

Every round works the same way: everyone acts on the current amount, and any raise means everyone still in has to act on the new amount. Bring the bets in, count the players and the pot, announce the players, bring the turn, and update the rake.

Counting players does more than help your announcements. Every once in a while a player accidentally covers their cards. If you know there should be three players and only two have acted, you'll catch it without even thinking.

Same again for the river. Once the river is out, drop the deck so both hands are free, protect the stub, and update the rake while your eyes stay on the action.

What I correct in class

The two most important things

I ask my classes this constantly: what are the two most important parts of this job? Following the action is one. The other is that everything has to be correct: every bet and call amount verified, the right rake taken, the right hand winning. When students forget to verify a bet amount, the wrong amount ends up in front of a player. Forgetting the rake is the one I see all the time in auditions, and a supervisor who's auditioning you will 100% notice.

The showdown and pushing the pot

When the betting on the river is done, give change first if anyone put out an incorrect amount, then bring the bets in. To win, a player has to table their hand, face up on the table. If a player holds their cards up but never sets them down, and mucks them when another player says "I guess you win," that's a floor call. They never tabled their hand. (Who shows first, mucking and side pot showdowns are all in the showdown.)

Then:

  1. Frame the board and announce the winning hand.
  2. Kill the losing hand. Reach for it and tap it twice on the table on the way to the muck. That tells the player you're about to take their cards, and gives them a last chance to speak up.
  3. Pull out the burn cards so you can push the pot cleanly.
  4. Check the rake as you touch the pot. Make it a habit to glance at the rake every time you're about to push a pot. Your worst case is that you notice it's wrong and fix it before you push. Once the pot's pushed, it's supposed to be too late, and asking a player for rake from a pot you've already pushed is one of the most uncomfortable things you can do as a dealer. This habit can save your job.
  5. Push the pot all the way to the player, with your hands open around it, never covering it. A big pot can go in two waves. Players shouldn't reach for the pot. If one does, let them know you'll push it when it's time.

After the pot: push, button, rake, tip

Do these in the same order, every single hand:

  1. Push the pot.
  2. Move the button. The very next thing, before you touch anything else.
  3. Drop the rake. Rake before tips.
  4. Drop your tip. Say thank you every time, then drop the tip by tapping it twice on the upper corner of your bank. That tells surveillance it's a tip. (It's also a sneaky little reminder to players: anyone new to the table wonders what that is, and asks.)
  5. Clean up the board. Scoop the board cards in, do a little mini wash, and you're on to the next hand right away.

What I correct in class

Cleaning up before moving the button

The instinct after pushing a pot is to clean up the cards. I correct this constantly, because if moving the button isn't the very next thing, you will eventually lose track of it. I know because it happened to me. There was a stretch of about two years at Texas Card House where I had trouble remembering where the button was. So embarrassing. I just hadn't built the habit all the way into my instincts. One of the worst things you can do is start the next hand with the button in the wrong place, so someone has the button twice or pays the big blind twice. Push the pot, move the button, drop the rake, drop the tip.

How to practice a full hand

  • Deal a whole hand to an empty table. That's how I demonstrate it: I make up the action as I go ("this player calls, this player raises to five") and deal every step, including change, rake and the push.
  • Count in your head as you go, every time: the players, the pot total, the rake.
  • Record yourself with your phone and watch it back to find your mistakes, then drill the fixes until they're habit.
  • If you're auditioning somewhere specific, learn their rules, and practice with the card size they use, poker or bridge. Auditioners only think positively of a candidate who went out of their way to learn how their room does things.

Common questions about dealing Texas hold'em

Who acts first in Texas hold'em?

Preflop, the first player to the left of the big blind. On the flop, turn and river, the first player still in the hand to the left of the button.

Should the dealer wait for the blinds before dealing?

Not in a cash game. Say "blinds" and keep pitching. If the big blind still isn't out when they get their second card, let them know they're the big blind.

What is the minimum raise in no-limit hold'em?

The amount to call plus the size of the previous raise. If a $6 bet is raised to $12, the next minimum raise is to $18. You don't have to double the bet.

How do you check in poker?

Tap the table twice with an empty hand, or say "check." Anything less clear, the dealer should get clarification on.

What does the dealer do after pushing the pot?

Move the button, drop the rake, drop the tip, then clean up the board and start the next hand.

Where does the muck go?

On the dealer's left, by the deck hand, kept tight and circular, where the dealer can protect it.
