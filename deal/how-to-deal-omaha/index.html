How to Deal Omaha and Read Omaha Hands

The hardest thing about Omaha for a dealer is reading the hands at the speed the players already do. Here's the technique I teach for reading the board first, how I read hi-lo, and the pot-limit formula I use at the table.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 16, 2026 · 17 min read

A lot of hold'em dealers get their first taste of Omaha by being thrown onto a pot-limit Omaha table, and that's why so many of them are scared of it. The procedures are mostly the same as hold'em. What's different is that every player has four cards, the hands get bigger, and the hardest thing for you as the dealer is reading those hands at the speed the players already do. Add a high-low game or pot-limit math on top of that and it's a lot to keep track of.

It's worth learning. The WSOP's audition includes a hand of a "flop game," and most of the options are Omaha games. And Omaha players can have very high expectations of their dealer. If you can keep up with them and nail their challenges, you're going to get tipped better. Here's how I teach it.

The one rule: exactly two from the hand, three from the board

In Omaha, every player gets four cards, and they can only use two from their hand and three from the board. There are no exceptions to that. The hand rankings are the same as hold'em. Because everyone has four cards, players make bigger, stronger hands than in hold'em.

Almost every Omaha reading mistake comes from a hold'em habit breaking this rule:

  • Four of a suit on the board. One card of that suit in the hand does not make a flush. The player needs two of the suit in their hand.
  • Three of a suit in the hand. If there are five hearts but three of them are in the player's hand, that's no good. They can only use two of them.
  • Trips on the board. In hold'em, we'd be looking for a single card that pairs with the board to make a full house. We can't do that here. On a board with three deuces, an ace and a six, a player holding an ace-king has three of a kind, deuces, not deuces full. For a full house on a trips board, you're looking for pocket pairs.
  • Two pair on the board. In hold'em we're used to a single card that matches one of the board pairs being a full house. In Omaha it doesn't work that way, because you can only use three from the board.

Dealing the hand

  1. Pitch four cards to each player, and don't rush it. Dealers have a tendency to want to rush the pitch in Omaha. Make sure every card goes to the right place. If cards get mixed up, you don't want the players to be the ones figuring out whose card is whose. That's an integrity issue.
  2. Know your card count. Ten players times four cards is 40, plus three burns and five board cards is 48. That leaves four cards, which is why you can't deal an 11th player in Omaha. When you count the stub at a full table, four is your number; add four more for each seat that wasn't dealt in.
  3. Verify four cards every time someone folds. You're technically supposed to check that there are four cards in every folded hand. If you miss it once or twice, nobody's really going to notice, but do it as often as you can. It's something they will literally look for in a lot of auditions.
  4. Never talk about the board. You would never say something like, "Oh, it's almost a low."

The flop, turn and river are brought the same way as in hold'em. If you need a refresher, see how to bring the flop, turn and river.

How I read Omaha high hands: read the board first

Here's the technique that makes Omaha easier. Don't look at each hand and try to figure out what everybody has. Read the board first, figure out the best possible hand, and then look only for the specific cards that make it. It's not about figuring out what everybody has. It's about knowing what to look for. If you can eliminate possibilities and zone in on the things you specifically want, you make your life a lot easier.

I go through the same questions every time. Is the board paired? Are there any possible flushes? Are there any possible straights? Then I know what kind of hand I'm looking for, and the board tells me the shortcut:

  • No pair, no flush, no straight possible. The best hand is a set, so look for a pocket pair that matches a board card. After that, two pair, starting from the top card.
  • A straight is possible. Name the exact two-card combinations that make it and scan for those. Often there's a key card: if someone has to have an eight to make the straight, every hand without an eight is out, and you can eliminate it in your mind.
  • A flush is possible. These are among the easiest boards. Any time there's a possible flush, your life is easy. You're just looking for two cards of that suit.
  • A paired board. Check for quads, then pocket pairs that make a full house, then a card that matches the board pair together with a card that matches another board card. One card that matches the board pair by itself is only three of a kind.
  • Two pair on the board. Look for quads, then a pocket pair that matches the fifth card, then one card from a board pair together with a card that matches another board card. A single card that matches one board pair is only three of a kind, because the player can't use both board pairs. This is the type of board to practice, because it's where hold'em instincts trip people up.
  • Trips on the board. Pocket pairs, pocket pairs, pocket pairs. That's what you want.

What I correct in class

Making a hand the rules don't allow

A student nearly reads "queens and sixes" when there's no such thing in that hand. On a board like queen-queen-jack-jack, a single queen or jack is tempting to call a full house, and it isn't. Double-paired boards are a common one that gets new dealers. In class I say that's how you can tell if someone's an Omaha person or just a hold'em person.

Skipping the four-card check

Players fold and the cards go straight into the muck. Get in the habit of seeing four cards every time, because an auditioner will be watching for it.

At the showdown

  • Read it out loud. Say what each hand is as you see it. That gives the players a chance to follow along and speak up.
  • You don't have to frame Omaha hands the way you would in hold'em. When a player has all four cards face up, you don't need to push their winning cards up, and players will often do that themselves. If you need to, you can. You'll see me frame the winning hands in the video above. Either way, read the hand and announce it.
  • Kill the losers. Tap the hand face up on the table to let everybody know it's leaving, then muck it.
  • Odd chip in a high-only chop. It goes to the player in the worst position: the next player who's going to receive the button.

The full showdown procedure, including who shows first, is in the showdown.

Reading high-low (Omaha 8 or better)

In Omaha high-low, the pot is split between the best high hand and the best qualifying low hand. A player can use different cards for each: exactly two from the hand for the high, and exactly two for the low, which can be any two of the four.

What makes a qualifying low

  • Five different ranks, all eight or lower, using two cards from the hand and three from the board.
  • A pair doesn't count as two. It only counts as one.
  • The board needs three different cards eight or lower, not a pair, or no low is possible.
  • The best low is 5-4-3-2-A, the wheel. The next best is 6-4-3-2-A, then 6-5-4-3-2.

Comparing two lows

Read a low starting with the highest card. Compare the two hands card by card, and the first moment one hand has a lower card, that hand is winning. So 7-6-4-2-A beats 8-6-3-2-A, because seven is lower than eight.

Watch for cards in the hand that match the board. A hand card that's already on the board doesn't help the low. A hand with a deuce in it can feel really low and still only make an eight low, because the cards that match the board don't give it five different low ranks.

Always read the high first

This game requires you to focus on the high hand first, then speak about the low. "Jacks and treys with a ten, no low." When there is a low, it's best to say it like "seven low with a four-deuce." There is nothing that's more of a giveaway that you don't deal high-low games than reading the low first. I correct it in class all the time. The sign of an experienced dealer is that they always start with the high.

Scoops, chops and quarters

  • Scoop. When one hand wins both the high and the low, I still push it out in two parts, as if I were chopping it. Some places say you can just push the whole pot.
  • Split the pot. Split the biggest denomination first, with the largest denomination closest to you, then cut the rest. In a cash game, use your fingertips, never your palm. Push the high first, then the low. Leave the board where everyone can see it while you chop.
  • Odd chips. The high gets the odd chip between the high and the low, every time. If the high or the low is then chopped, the extra chip goes to the worst position, the next player to get the button.
  • Quartering. One player wins the high all by themselves, and two players chop the low. Each of the low winners gets a quarter of the pot. I didn't see quartering in over a decade as a supervisor. Double-board bomb pots and pot-limit high-low made it common, so it's worth practicing. Go slow and double-check. It's okay to take the time.

Stacking the pot as you go is one of those things that differs room to room. The WSOP procedure is to stack after the river; I was taught in cash games to stack throughout. There are two schools of thought, and they're both right. Stack even when no low is possible, because you don't want to give that information away. The step-by-step chop, with the math, is in split pots and quartering.

Pot-limit Omaha: how to calculate the pot

Pot-limit Omaha, PLO, is where the math comes in. The minimum raise is the same as no limit. The maximum a player can raise is the amount of the pot. Conceptually, the player puts the call in, and then they can raise by everything that's there. There's no limit on the number of re-raises.

The formula: three times the last bet, plus the trail, plus the pot

The trail is all the active bets in the same betting round that are in front of players who aren't in action. Here's how it works with $5/$5 blinds and two calls:

  • Three times the last bet: 3 × $5 = $15.
  • Plus the trail: the other $5, $5 and $5 out there = $30.
  • Plus the pot from earlier streets: nothing yet. So the pot is $30.

A few more, all from my lessons:

  • Someone raises to $15 after limpers. Three times 15 is 45, plus a $30 trail, and the pot is $75.
  • Facing a $75 raise with two calls of $75 behind it and $20 dead in the middle: 3 × 75 is 225, plus 75 is 300, plus 75 is 375, plus 20 is $395.
  • On the flop, the pot is $320. Someone bets $150, someone calls $150. Three times 150 plus 150 plus 320 is $920.

Two mistakes everyone makes

Counting the chips in front of the player in action. This is the number one PLO pot mistake. If an amount of money is already in front of the player who's asking, it does not count as part of the trail. Say six $5 bets are out, the blinds and limpers. Someone raises to $25 and gets a call, and the player in action is one of those $5 bets. Three times 25 is 75, plus the $25 call is 100, plus the other five $5 bets is $125. That player's own $5 isn't counted.

Using an all-in for less as the last bet. An all-in for less counts as part of the trail. It does not count as the previous bet. The pot is $920, someone bets $500, and a short stack goes all in for $245. The wrong way is three times 245, plus 500, plus 920, which is $2,155. The right way is three times 500, plus 245, plus 920: $2,665.

Shortcuts that come up every hand

  • The first raise preflop is four times the big blind. Each limper adds one more big blind: with one call it's five times, and so on.
  • In a cash game, the small blind counts as a big blind. In 5/10, the first pot raise is $40. In a tournament you count the real amount: at 100/200, the first pot raise is 700. In a 1/2 game where the small blind counts as $2, the first raise is $8. In 2/5, it's $20.
  • Keep dead money separate until you add it, physically, so it doesn't get lost in the count.
  • Loose change rounds up. When the pot comes out to an odd amount, it goes up to a full increment. Exactly how a room rounds can depend on the game and the structure, so learn your room's way.
  • When everyone is equal, it gets simple. Bet and call on the flop means four times the bet plus the pot.

Track the pot. Never count it down.

You need to know the total before you bring the bets in, so keep track of it street to street. Counting the pot down in the middle of a hand slows the game and goes against the one player per hand rule. If you need to know the pot and you don't, you don't touch that pot as the dealer. You call the floor. The last thing you want is to build the habit of counting the pot down. You also only need the total for pot bets, not the main and side pots separately. And if nobody has enough left to bet the pot, you don't need to track it at all.

Talk your game

You don't need the number instantly. It's okay to calculate it out loud as you go. If you need to say "20, 30, 40," that's fine. Better still, work it out while the player is thinking. Pointing and saying it in steps, "three times 650 is…", lets the table follow along instead of waiting while you compute silently, and walking the players through each piece shows them you know what you're doing.

Announcing a pot bet

  • Announce bets, raises, re-raises and all-ins as you would in any game. "Pot" replaces "raise": "Pot, one twenty-five."
  • If a player silently puts out the exact pot, say "raise," check it, then "pot."
  • If they put in more than the pot, announce the pot and make change.
  • If a player declares more than the pot, say $175 when the pot is $125, correct it to the pot.

When a player asks "how much is the pot?"

In pot limit, players are allowed to ask, and we do tell them how much is in the pot, in pot limit only. Not in no limit, not in limit. Not every casino agrees on exactly how this works, so it can be room to room. If someone you're trying to get a job with says otherwise, go with their answer. And if a player asks and you don't know, you call the floor. You never count it down.

Asking how much the pot is isn't the same as saying "pot." The question doesn't commit the player to anything.

What I correct in class

The bettor's own chips

People get confused about this over and over again. Before you add anything to the trail, look at whose action it is and leave their chips out.

The all-in for less

It's a common mistake dealers make: the short all-in becomes the "last bet" and the whole pot comes out wrong. The full bet before it is still the last bet.

Expecting to be perfect

PLO math is hard for everyone. You will not get every single pot right, even as an experienced dealer, and players help dealers with the math constantly. What matters is that you talk your game and fix it when you're off.

Big O (five-card Omaha)

Big O is five-card Omaha. The WSOP plays it in tournaments as pot-limit Omaha high-low eight or better, and some cash games play it that way too. Players still must use exactly two from the hand and exactly three from the board. It's a seven-handed game at most, because of how many cards it takes.

  • Verify five cards on every fold.
  • The pot math works the same way. In 5/10 Big O, the first pot raise is 30 plus 10, because the small blind counts as a big blind: $40. With one limper, it's $50.
  • In a hi-lo Big O game, I start stacking once the river is out, after getting the action going.

Five-card Omaha double-board bomb pots are their own animal. See how to deal a bomb pot.

How to practice Omaha

  • The one-deck drill (in the video above). Shuffle, burn, and deal a board. Then deal Omaha hands one at a time. Keep the best hand to one side, and see if each new hand beats it. Kill the losers, and keep going through the whole deck. Don't be afraid to take a little extra time while you're learning.
  • Do it high-low. Keep the best high hand on one side and the best low on the other.
  • Force the tricky boards. Change a board card to make trips, two pair or four to a straight, and run the drill again. Put out every type of board in this guide.
  • The eight-hand version. Deal eight Omaha hands around, then deal several boards against the same hands. The more you do it, the better you get at it.
  • Pot math. Create different scenarios for cash games and for tournaments, especially the structure you're going to deal. Most of what you do ends up memorized: by about week seven of class, numbers like 15 × 7 or 35 × 7 stop being math, because they come up constantly.

Common questions about dealing Omaha

How many cards do you use in Omaha?

Each player gets four cards and must use exactly two of them with exactly three from the board. In Big O, players get five cards, and the same two-and-three rule applies.

Can you have a flush with one suited card in Omaha?

No. Even with four of a suit on the board, a player needs two cards of that suit in their hand.

How many players can you deal Omaha to?

Ten. Ten players at four cards each, plus three burns and a five-card board, uses 48 cards, so there aren't enough for an 11th player. Big O is seven-handed at most.

How do you calculate the pot in pot-limit Omaha?

Three times the last bet, plus the trail, plus the pot. Don't count the chips already in front of the player in action, and an all-in for less counts as trail, not as the last bet.

Can a dealer tell a player how much is in the pot?

In pot limit, yes, when the player asks, though it can differ room to room. In no limit and limit, the dealer doesn't. If you don't know the amount, call the floor instead of counting the pot down.

Do you read the high or the low first in Omaha hi-lo?

The high, always. Announce the high hand, then the low, or "no low" if there isn't one.

Who gets the odd chip in Omaha hi-lo?

The high hand gets the odd chip between the high and the low. If a half is then split again, the extra chip goes to the worst position.
