Game Protection for Poker Dealers

Most of what looks like fussy dealer procedure is game protection. Every habit is there so a card is never exposed, a chip never goes missing, and if anything is ever questioned, the cameras show exactly what happened.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 21, 2026 · 23 min read

As a dealer, you're responsible for everything that happens on the table. In poker it isn't the house's money in the pot, but you're still the one protecting the integrity of the game. Players sit down trying to leave with thousands more than they brought, and you're the one who notices when somebody crosses a line.

That's what game protection is. A lot of what looks like fussy dealer procedure (the 45-degree rule, clearing your hands, where the muck goes) exists so that across every hand, dealt by every dealer, a card never gets exposed, nobody can control the deck, and if anything is ever questioned, surveillance can see exactly what happened. In a home game, friends just sort it out. In a card room, a player who doesn't know anyone at the table is counting on that procedure.

Protecting the deck

Surveillance needs to be able to see the top of the deck at any given moment. Almost every deck rule comes back to that.

Never roll the deck

Keep the stub close to parallel to the felt, facing the ceiling. You can tilt it a little, up to 45 degrees, for example when you use your pinky to bring in chips on the deck side, if your room allows it. Never more than that. Tilting it further is called rolling the deck. Go past that angle and I can push the deck so the cards pop up, and I can see what's in there. A magician would be able to tell what card is there if they wanted to control the deck. Rolling it also means cards can fly out if someone bumps your hand. And some very fast pitchers tilt the deck while they deal, so the players on that side can see the cards come off.

"I have a death grip" isn't a reason. No hand covers the corners of the cards, and you can deal smoothly without rolling it. Casino procedure eliminates every risk it can. If you just want to look at your watch, don't roll the deck in your hand to do it.

Don't cover the top of the deck

After you cut the deck and put it in your hand, a lot of new dealers cover the top of it with their other hand, even for a split second. A magician does that if they want to control where a card goes, so poker organizations, the World Series of Poker especially, are very strict about it, especially in auditions. Surveillance specifically doesn't like it. If the deck needs squaring, do the big work as you're picking it up, then put it in your deck hand and finish there with that hand's own fingers and thumb.

Never cross your hands

If you need to reach across the table, don't reach over the deck. Slide the deck back, keep it on the felt, and go around with your other hand. Casino procedure, pretty universally, even in small poker rooms, doesn't want you crossing your hands. When you cover the deck from surveillance, that's when a card mechanic can switch cards or control the deck. And if you have money in your hands, once one hand covers the other, that's when someone could steal a chip.

The deck also never goes off the table. You never take it over the edge of the rail.

Keep the stub in one hand

Never switch the stub to your other hand. It gains you nothing and risks dropping it or exposing cards. Procedure is built for every dealer and every hand, not for one dealer's record. Some rooms even treat the deck leaving the dealer's hand as fouled. That's strict, but I agree with the concept. It's the same reason one-handed dealing worries me: the moment the deck switches hands is an opportunity for a skilled card mechanic. If it's easier for you to pitch with one hand and handle chips with the other, train your weaker hand. If you're working and truly can't, ask management for an exception.

Square it facing away, cut it properly

  • Square the deck with the faces away from you. In table games, the dealer faces the cards toward themselves, because the house is protecting itself from players following cards. Poker is player against player, so you face the cards away to show you can't manipulate the deck.
  • Use the dull side of the cut card. The shiny side can reflect a card. It almost never matters, but avoid it.
  • Cut at least a third. Take a third off the top or leave a third on the bottom, so nobody can follow a card through the shuffle. The full shuffle is in how to shuffle cards.
  • Drop the deck once the river is out, so both hands are free, and be careful with the bottom of the stub as you do it. A player could see what's in there. The bottom card should always stay concealed. Dropping the deck is covered with the board.

What I correct in class

Covering the deck after the cut

A lot of students cover the deck with their other hand as they put it in their hand after cutting. It's really, really hard to pass an audition if that's one of your habits, so it's one of the ones I focus on the most. Every time I see it, I call it out. Once it's been called out enough, it starts to feel bad, and your instincts learn not to do it.

Crossing your hands

About 90% of people in my classes mess this up at first: they reach across the deck to take a player's chips. One of the easiest ways to fail an audition is to cross your hands at all. You want it to feel like you're doing something seriously wrong. After a few weeks of being corrected, it's just a habit.

Rolling the deck to square up

Students turn the deck perpendicular to the table to square it, or flip it over while gathering cards. Keep it parallel. Use your pinky to scoop instead.

Protecting the muck and folded cards

  • Keep the muck by your deck hand. Both sides are professional, and some rooms put it on the right, sometimes because of where equipment is built into the table. The WSOP introduced keeping the muck just to the left, by the deck hand, where that hand protects it, and that's what I teach. If you're a lefty, flip it.
  • Don't let it hang over the well. That gives the cards a chance to flip up if your hand goes in the well for something.
  • Don't show folded hands on the way in. Some dealers bring in a player's cards and tilt them toward the players, so you can see what that player had. Your job as a dealer is to prevent that. Sometimes you can tell when players are looking.
  • Don't just tuck folded cards under the deck, where players can glance at them. Put them into the muck, or into the spread stub, and do a quick mini-wash before you scoop.
  • Keep the burn cards separate from the muck, under at least one chip, with the next burn stair-stepped out from it. The chip keeps you from accidentally scooping them into the muck. How to burn is with the board.
  • Get mucked cards into the middle of the muck, and never go into the muck for any reason. The showdown guide explains why.

Shortcuts here can bite you. There was a dealer who mixed the stub, the burn cards and the muck together after the river, but only when no bad beat was possible, since that room needed the cards verifiable to pay one. Another dealer copied it without understanding why, and did it on boards where a bad beat was possible. Understand a shortcut before you use it.

Counting the stub, and the deck itself

Count the stub, the cards left after a hand is dealt. Where there's no shuffle machine, a deck with an extra or missing card threatens the integrity of the game. Most rooms want it counted once per down. Some, the WSOP among them, say twice: once in the first two or three hands and again near the end of your down, though even there nobody will usually say anything if you do it once. The numbers to look for are in counting the stub.

A card can go missing in ways you'd never expect, like getting mixed in with cut cards left standing in the well. Counting the stub every down is how you catch it.

If your count comes up wrong, don't bring attention to it. Quietly verify it. If it's really off, switch the deck out and tell the table you've got a sticky deck.

Duplicate and missing cards

Could a player exploit a duplicate card only they know about? Technically, yes. But in my whole career I've essentially never seen a true duplicate. The protections stack up: shuffle machines count the deck, hand-shuffle rooms count the stub every down, tournament decks get suited every break, and fanning a new deck lets surveillance see what was there. Shuffle machines' biggest value isn't speed, it's integrity: the dealer isn't shuffling, so manipulating the deck is very hard.

Deck changes and marked cards

Poker uses plastic cards. Table games use paper cards and change them often, because a marked card is so costly to the house. A poker setup starts to feel worse after roughly 20 to 60 hours of play, and rooms keep a lot of setups in circulation so the same deck isn't always on the same table. How setups get checked, suited and swapped is in deck changes, suited decks and dead spreads.

If you notice a mark on a card, the question is whether it makes that card distinguishable. A deck where lots of cards have similar fingernail marks isn't the problem. One card with a mark you could pick out from the back is: take that deck out of play and call the floor. Rooms keep two decks of different colors in rotation, and when you switch, check the backs.

Clear your hands

Clearing your hands means quickly showing the camera your palm and the back of your hand. It can be one quick motion. Surveillance cameras watch all casino activity to protect the casino, the players and the employees. Clear your hands:

  • After the shuffle and the cut, before you put the deck in your hand.
  • After you push every pot. Build the habit so it's automatic, every single time.
  • After you touch cash or come out of the bank.
  • After you deliver chips, especially if you didn't splash them. Otherwise, how does anyone know you didn't palm a chip on the way back?
  • Between pots, so touching a side pot and then the main pot doesn't look like moving chips from one to the other.
  • Before and after you touch your face, hair or clothes. Clear, scratch, clear again. If you need to wipe a bug off your shirt, same thing.
  • Before and after your hand goes below the rail. You're supposed to avoid it, but sometimes you have to. Some rooms keep menus under the table, and handing them out is part of the dealer's job.

Clearing your hands isn't for management. It's for you. Victor, now COO of Texas Card House, told the staff there exactly that: if anything is ever investigated, clean hands on camera show you did nothing wrong.

In the bank

  • Never let your hands cover each other in the tray. That's an opportunity for theft. Some people say one hand in the tray at a time, and some casinos require it. I've always taught that two hands are fine as long as they never cross in any way, but the safest habit is not to have two hands in the same spot.
  • No passing chips hand to hand in the well. Once you've been working in the bank, stop passing spacers hand to hand too, because surveillance can't tell whether it's a spacer or chips in your hand.
  • Put a spacer on top of any short stack in your bank, so surveillance and the next dealer can tell right away it's not a full 20.
  • Keep the lowest denominations on the outside and the larger bills on the inside, so anyone trying to sneak a chip gets the smallest one possible. Working the bank has the full layout.

The good news is that in poker, the well is usually a fixed amount, so if anything is missing, you can identify it pretty quickly. How strict a room is varies. Big casinos with strict table games often apply the same rules to poker, because it's the same surveillance team.

Taps, the button and fidgeting

  • Tapping is a signal. When a player tips you, tap the corner of the bank, drop it in the tip box and clear your hands. The tap is a visual and audio cue to surveillance that money is going into your box. So don't tap the table when you drop the rake. That would look very suspicious. Tips are covered with the rake.
  • Don't fidget with the pot, the button or spacers. The no-fidgeting rule comes from table games, where the house's money is in the well. In poker it matters less, but shuffling chips in the pot creates an integrity risk, and everyone at the table needs to know where the button is.

What I correct in class

Hands below the rail

This one comes up in every cohort: a hand drifts below the rail while a student talks, or reaches under the table mid-hand. Your instinct should be to never let your hands drop below the rail, and in an audition it can fail you. If you truly have to, clear before and after.

Forgetting to clear after the push

Students push a pot and go straight to the next thing. Clear your hands after every pot, until you don't have to think about it.

Protecting the chips and the pot

You're a money communicator. Every bet, every stack and every pot should be readable from above, by the players and by surveillance, which is watching from above.

  • Break bets down and splash them so the amount is clear before it goes into the pot. A clump of chips can't be verified. The method is in counting down large bets.
  • Don't restack a splashed short all-in. When you splash a short all-in, don't restack it neatly afterward, because that makes it look like a full stack.
  • Glance at every stack when you sit down. I eyeball the stacks like a player would. It helps me engage, and it makes it easy to notice chips coming off the table, or big chips hidden behind smaller ones. Then you can smoothly ask for them on top or out front.
  • Bring the bets in. Leave them out and a player can quietly take some back (that's one of the angle shots below). Building the pots, even during all-in action, is the industry standard, and a lot of rooms require it for exactly that reason.
  • Never pick up a pot in your hand, and never carry chips over other pots. Push them, with your hands open around the chips.
  • Stack side-pot contributions next to the original bet, so if a player asks "did you get his 56?", you can prove it. The full method is in how to build side pots.

Players who take shots

Some players short their bets on purpose to see if you'll catch it. I once tapped into a game, noticed a look, counted the pot, and found one player had seen the flop for $5 of a $40 raise. In my own down, that same player stalled on the small blind and twice tossed in $15 for a $20 raise, with the reds hidden under splashed white chips. Every bet must be correct, which means every bet gets verified, cleanly and quickly, before you assume it's right.

When a player keeps doing it, don't call them out at the table. If they defend themselves well, it can backfire on you. Tell the floor on your way out, so they can check the footage first. If it keeps happening, something light works better than an accusation: count it out loud, "one, two, three, four red chips. You got this."

Angle shooting

Angle shooting is gaining an edge outside the rules: a player using a gap in the procedure, or a dealer who isn't paying attention, to get something they shouldn't. A lot of poker rules exist to close those gaps. Following the action is a big part of it, too. If you saw everything, a player who claims something different can't pull one over on you.

The angles dealers see most

  • Taking back part of a bet. The dealer doesn't bring the bets in before the turn, and a player quietly takes back their 50. Bring the bets in first, every street.
  • Shorting a bet on purpose. A player tosses in less than the bet and waits to see if you catch it. That's the player in players who take shots above. Every bet gets verified.
  • An action with two possible meanings. A player puts out something that could be read two ways, like a single oversized chip, reads the table, and then decides which one he meant. That's why rooms have rules like the oversized chip rule and why I lean away from letting players clarify after the fact. The rule takes away the second option. If your room has no oversized chip rule, using lower-denomination chips and changing big chips out of the pot helps. The rule itself is in the technical rules of betting.
  • Making you do the work. Some players throw out the wrong amount again and again and make you figure out what they meant. Once in a while it's just a player challenging the dealer. If it keeps happening, it can be angle shooting. Tell a supervisor, and handle it with care.
  • Asking for counts to buy time. I never give a guess when a player asks how much an all-in is. I count it. If a room's clock only starts after the count, a player can keep asking for counts just to stall.

What the dealer does about it

Your job is the same as with any suspected cheating: you don't accuse anyone. Enforce your room's rules exactly, especially when you're new. If something happens in a hand that needs a decision, call the floor. If it's a pattern, tell the supervisor on your way out so they can check the footage. Deciding whether a player was angle shooting, and what happens to them, is the supervisor's job, not yours.

Protecting the game at the table

Game protection isn't only about your hands. It's also enforcing one player per hand: nobody gets help during a hand.

Phones

I've never seen players meaningfully cheat with their phones, but maybe 15 times I suspected two players were texting during hands. Talking strategy between hands is fine. One player per hand only applies during the hand. When it does look like two players are on their phones at the same time, you don't accuse anybody. Say what you see and enforce the rule: "I don't know if you know each other, but you both seem to be on your phones at the same time. The way to resolve it is to enforce our rule: no interacting with your phone during a hand." Articulation is the dealer's weapon.

Tournaments take it more seriously, because collusion affects the whole field. In cash games, rooms are usually more relaxed unless there's a reason to suspect something. If anyone at the table looks bothered, or it's slowing the game down, enforce it right away. And if you're a new dealer or in an audition, enforce it every time.

Why the phone rule isn't enforced the same everywhere

When smartphones showed up, rooms banned them out of fear of cheating. Banning phones completely to stop cheating was like killing an ant with a sledgehammer. Over about a decade, rooms learned to handle cash games case by case, while tournaments still require it. What I prefer is simple: no phone use while you have cards. How strictly a room enforces it depends a lot on the local culture, and if surveillance ever needs to, it can zoom in on a phone.

There's another side to it, too. The rule exists so the room can say it's preventing cheating. But a recreational player who's on his phone is exactly who the pros want at their table, so nobody's in a hurry to chase him off. That's why "does anyone else at the table care?" matters so much in a cash game.

When you do enforce it, you don't have to be stern. "I need you to get off your phone" lands worse than friendly energy: "I'm here now, let's get those chips stacked." If you're the rule-follower at your room, make it a fun persona. Players can love a dealer who runs a tight game.

Smartwatches

The electronic-device rule covers watches too. Treat a smartwatch the same way as a phone: no interacting with it during a hand.

Showing cards to a neighbor

  • The neighbor is in the hand: expose the cards immediately, and often notify the floor.
  • The neighbor isn't in the hand: in a cash game it's usually fine. In a tournament, it's warning or penalty territory. In a small tournament full of regulars, a friendly "it's not like a cash game" first. In bigger events, call the floor.

Table talk in a multiway all-in

With three players in, talk between two of them affects the third. If a player flips up a card and another player tells someone to fold, tell them it isn't allowed, then let the action continue. In a tournament it would be a penalty. Anyone who keeps doing it gets reported to a supervisor. You can prevent a lot of it, too. When one player is all in and two are still playing, announce, "Main pot, all in. We still have action on the side," so the all-in player doesn't flip their cards. I always teach preventative dealing. The exposed cards page covers what happens when a card gets shown anyway.

Even the betting limits come from game protection. In limit games, the cap of a bet and three raises stops two players from re-raising a third player stuck between them until his stack is gone. Heads-up there's no cap, because there's nobody to collude with.

Why it takes a live dealer

Years ago I played 1-2 on a cruise ship, on an electronic touchscreen table with no dealer. The technology worked fine. But with no one policing the game, players talked about their hands, pressured each other, and one went wildly out of line, and no supervisor was close enough to help. A dealer doesn't just need to exist. They need to be right there, all the time.

If you suspect cheating

(How cheating gets caught, and what surveillance watches dealers for, is in how casinos catch cheating at poker.) Cheating is much harder in a card room than in a home game. There are cameras, marked cards get removed, and experienced players notice things. Home games are the scary place. As a supervisor and shift manager, I investigated reports on surveillance many times. A handful of times I confirmed cheating: players adding chips to their stack in the middle of a hand, and one who mixed his cards with a folded hand. In more than ten years, I kicked out collusion once: two players trying to build pots against a third. The pot went to the player they were working against. Penalties for cheating go up to lifetime bans, and a dealer who cheats can lose their job and face fines or even criminal charges.

Your job as the dealer is not to investigate or accuse. It's to report:

  • Something you can see in the hand, like a player adding chips mid-hand, a marked card, or a player getting help: call the floor and tell them what you saw. What to say to the floor walks through it.
  • A pattern you've noticed, like a player who keeps shorting bets: tell the supervisor quietly, on your way out, so they can check the footage.

When a supervisor asks for a review, they call surveillance with the table and the time window. Surveillance isn't supposed to be friends with the people it watches, so the supervisor watches the replay in a separate room, not the real surveillance room. A poker person is needed to understand what happened on the tape. That's also why every habit on this page matters: the cleaner your procedure, the easier it is for anyone to see on camera exactly what happened.

How to practice game protection

  • Record yourself. Set your phone up to film your practice table, ideally from above. A bird's-eye view is basically what surveillance sees. Watch it back for any moment the top of the deck disappears, your hands cross, or a stack doesn't read at a glance.
  • Deal a full hand without crossing your hands once. Reach for the far side of the table by sliding the deck back and going around.
  • Make clearing automatic. Clear after every push, every trip into the bank, and every touch of your face, until you do it without thinking.
  • Count the stub while you practice, so counting it at work is automatic.

Common questions about game protection

What is game protection in poker?

It's everything a dealer does so a card is never exposed, the deck can't be controlled, chips can't go missing, and surveillance can see exactly what happened. It also means enforcing one player per hand.

Why can't a poker dealer roll the deck?

Tilting the stub past about 45 degrees lets someone peek at cards or control the deck, and it can expose cards to players. Keep it close to parallel to the felt.

Why do poker dealers clear their hands?

To show the camera there's nothing in them, after the push, the bank, cash, or touching their face. It protects the dealer: if something is investigated, clean hands on camera show they did nothing.

Why do dealers count the stub?

To catch a missing or extra card before it affects a hand. Most rooms want it once per down; some say twice, early and near the end.

Can players use their phones at a poker table?

Many rooms have a rule against interacting with your phone during a hand. Between hands is usually fine. Tournaments enforce it more strictly than cash games.

Can you wear a smartwatch at a poker table?

The electronic-device rule covers watches too, so treat a smartwatch like a phone: don't interact with it while you have cards.

What is angle shooting in poker?

Gaining an edge outside the rules: taking back part of a bet the dealer left out, shorting a bet on purpose, or making an ambiguous action and picking its meaning after seeing how the table reacts. The dealer doesn't accuse anyone. They enforce the rules and tell the floor.

What happens if the dealer sees the bottom card?

Call the floor. The bottom card should always be concealed, and rooms handle it differently, from treating it like an exposed card (after substantial action) to calling it a foul deck where all the bets are returned. That decision belongs to the supervisor. Exposed cards covers the related situations.

What should a dealer do if they suspect cheating?

Don't accuse anyone at the table. If you see something in the hand, call the floor and say what you saw. If it's a pattern, tell the supervisor quietly so they can check the footage.
