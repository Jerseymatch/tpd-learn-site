Rake, Promo Drop & Tips

This is how the casino makes money, and how you make money. Taking the right rake every single hand is tied for the most important thing you do as a dealer, and a lot of dealer schools don't teach it with the seriousness it deserves.

By Marc Shumsker · Updated November 15, 2026 · 22 min read

Poker is different from every other table game. In blackjack, the player plays against the house, and the house advantage is how the casino makes money. In poker, the players play against each other, so when somebody wins a pot, the house doesn't win anything. The way the casino makes money is by taking a little bit out of each hand, almost like a commission. That's the rake. In a tournament, the same idea comes out of every entry as an entry fee (a $200 tournament might have a $20 entry fee), and that's handled when the player buys in, so as a tournament dealer you don't have to worry about it.

In a cash game, though, the rake is on you, every hand. I tell my classes the two most important things you do as a dealer are following the action, and making sure everything is correct: every bet, every call, and the rake. A lot of dealer schools teach you how to take the rake and have you practice it, but they don't emphasize how serious it is. It's not just another thing to learn. It's what the room is open for. I've seen dealers get fired for not taking it properly, and there's nothing the dealer can say as an excuse.

The rake and the promotion (jackpot) drop

In most cash games you're taking two separate things out of the pot.

  • The rake is the casino's money. It pays the employees, covers the expenses, and is the profit that makes it worthwhile for the casino to host the game. It goes through a rake slide into a locked box under the table, and security swaps the boxes out and counts them, usually once or twice a day.
  • The promotion drop, or jackpot drop, is separate. In the United States, states generally allow casinos to take it so they can give it away: a bad beat jackpot that builds up and pays out under a rare circumstance, or frequent promotions like high hands or splash the pot. The premise is that 100% of that money goes back to the players, and every player at the table has to have an equal shot at it. (Some states allow a small handling fee, and having worked in casinos and seen how much work it takes to count those boxes every day at every table, I think that makes sense.)

It's pretty universal that the rake goes on the right and the promotion goes on the left, but it is not fully universal. Follow whatever your room does. And there's no such thing as a left-handed or right-handed rake. The rake slide is where it is.

The jackpot drop usually goes into a very thin slit in the table, just big enough to fit one chip standing vertically. A lot of the time Hold'em games have a jackpot drop and Omaha and stud games don't, because with four cards in your hand in Omaha, big hands like quads and straight flushes are more likely. Including those games would be unfair to the Hold'em players.

Know the pot without counting it

Every rake, at every casino, starts with the same thing: you have to know how much is in the pot. And you get that by doing the math as the money comes in, not by counting the chips in the middle.

  1. Count dead money separately, as you take it in. Blinds that didn't call, and players who called and then folded to a raise.
  2. When the betting round is complete, multiply. The number of players still in, times the bet. Count the players as you bring the bets in ("one, two, three, four"), because you need that number anyway to announce it after the flop.
  3. Add it up and keep it in your head. In 1/2, if four players call a raise to $6 and both blinds fold, that's 6 times 4 is 24, plus the dead small blind and the dead big blind: $27.

You never want to go into the pot to count it. You don't want to touch the chips in the pot more than you have to. You don't want to call attention to the rake or slow the game down. And counting it out loud violates one player per hand: the money went in right in front of everybody, and a player who kept track of it on their own earned that information. If a player asks you how much is in the pot, you're not supposed to answer. Some casinos let you spread the chips a little so they get a better idea, but you don't count it for them.

Only called money counts

This is true all the time: only the portion of a bet that another player called counts toward the pot.

  • 1/2 no-limit, a player goes all in for $21 and everyone folds. The pot isn't $24. Only $2 of the all-in was called, so the pot is $5.
  • Someone raises to $10, someone re-raises to $40, everyone folds. With the blinds, the pot is $23. Only $10 of the re-raise counts.
  • On the turn, with $20 already in, someone bets $10, someone raises to $30, and the bettor folds. The pot is $40: the $20 from before, the $10 bet, and the $10 of the raise that matched it.

When to take the rake: every street

Take the rake as you go, on the flop, the turn and the river. Why take it on the flop when it's almost definitely going to change? Because if someone bets and everyone folds and you haven't taken it yet, now you have to stop everything before you push the pot, maybe make change, and take it while everyone watches. It's a weird spectacle. Take it as it comes, so that at any moment you're ready to push the pot.

The order matters: bring the card first, then take the rake, while the action is on the players. Your eyes still go to the action first. One of my biggest pet peeves, and one of the most common mistakes I see, is a dealer who brings the flop and starts doing other stuff, making change, taking rake, while players act. Then a player puts chips in and the dealer just assumes the players before them checked. Most of the time they're right. Occasionally they're wrong. You have to follow the action. If you're doing something with your hands, keep glancing back. If players are acting quickly, take it when you can and catch up on the next chance.

You keep taking it until you hit the maximum, and once you've hit it, you're done with the rake for that hand. (Some games have no maximum, which I'll get to.)

What I correct in class

Making the players wait for the rake

A student who takes the rake before bringing the turn has it backwards. Now everybody is waiting while you take $3. Bring the turn, let the action start, and take the rake while the players are thinking. You never want the players waiting on you for this.

The street where everything blows up

The number one scenario where dealers forget to rake: there's a small rake in on the flop, and then all the action blows up on one street, someone's all in, and the next thing you know you're pushing the pot. That's exactly when you have to update the rake, and it's exactly when your brain is busy with everything else.

How the rake is calculated: on the tens or on the sixes

A lot of rooms say "10% up to a $5 max." But what does 10% actually mean at the table?

On the tens means $1 at $10, $1 at $20, $1 at $30, and so on up to the max. So a $10 pot is $1, which is 10%. But a $19 pot is still $1, and that's 5.26%. A $34 pot is $3, about 8.8%. Taken on the tens, the rake is really between 5% and 10%, not 10%.

On the sixes means $1 at $6, $1 at $16, $1 at $26, $1 at $36 and $1 at $46. Taking $1 from a $6 pot is more than 10%, but taking $1 from a $15 pot is less, and over the long run it balances itself out to about 10%. That's why a lot of casinos have started taking it on the sixes.

Why do I go into this? Because players complain about the rake, and a brand new dealer who doesn't know how to respond just goes along with it. That makes the situation worse, and now you've got nine sour people at the table. If you work somewhere that rakes on the sixes and somebody's upset about it, you can explain it: yes, the casino wants to make money, and here's how it balances out to 10%. Having that perspective is the thing that defuses those situations, and it helps your guest service and your tips.

Rake structures you'll run into

The rake is never something you decide. It's set ahead of time, and you learn it wherever you work. Some I've seen and heard of:

  • 10% up to $5, on the tens or on the sixes.
  • 10% up to $3. Super old school, but common in shorter games, like six-handed.
  • Nowadays I've heard of regular land-based casinos taking as much as $7 max. Someone from Canada told me in a community call that their room takes $10.
  • 10% up to $15 on a cruise ship 2/5 game. Imagine keeping track of a pot up to $150 every time.
  • 5% up to $15 in some private games.
  • 5% uncapped: $1 at $20, $1 at $40, $1 at $60, and it never ends.

The jackpot drop has its own structure. A common one is $1 at $10 and $1 at $20, for a $2 max. Some rooms take $2 automatically at $10. There are a lot of variations, and whatever your room says, you do.

The one trick: learn to count the pot

People ask me regularly for tricks to keep track of the rake. There's one trick. Learn to count the pot, every single hand, and you will never have an issue with the rake.

Most new dealers start with Texas Hold'em and never build the habit of counting the pot. (In 2/5 you're almost always going to hit the max, so it's easy not to.) Then the poker room asks them to deal pot-limit Omaha, where you have to know the pot to know the maximum bet, and it's overwhelming. So practice as if you're in a 5% uncapped game. If there's $75 in the pot from preflop, and on the flop it goes bet $35, call $35, someone goes all in, and the rest fold, you're adding $105 (the bet, the call, and $35 of the all-in), for $180. 5% of that is $9, and you need to know that before you push. If you can take a 5% uncapped rake consistently, you can deal any game without the rake being a problem.

The math gets easier the more you do it. The first time you do 37 times 3, it's frustrating. The third time you're doing 35 times 3 plus 2 times 3. By the tenth time you might have it memorized, because the same numbers come up over and over.

Limit games are their own challenge. The numbers are so often the same (24, 32, 16) that it's easy to forget which pot you're on. Announcing the number of players out loud helps jog your memory.

What I correct in class

Guessing the rake

There's a thing that happens to new dealers with the rake. They get overwhelmed and just take an amount: "I'll start here. At least I took something." When that happens, you have to be your own teacher. If you develop the habit of not knowing how much it is and guessing, that habit is very hard to break. Notice you couldn't keep up, and solve that problem, so you always know the number. I don't care which method you use to get there. Counting the chips in the pot is an okay backup, but it's not the method to rely on.

No flop no drop, chops and side pots

  • No flop, no drop. Almost every poker room I've seen doesn't take any rake when the hand ends before the flop. It's common practice, but it's not universal. Some rooms rake every hand no matter what, and many take at least $1 for the jackpot. If you're in one of those rooms, you have to be able to calculate the pot preflop. In class we practice no flop, no drop, because I think it has the highest integrity.
  • A chop. When everyone folds to the blinds and they agree to chop, they get their money back and you go to the next hand. You don't take a rake on a chop.
  • Side pots. You take the rake from the pot where the total pot reached the next rake amount. You're not taking the whole rake from the main pot every time. If the main pot stopped at $34 and the total reached $54 with the side pot, the rake for that last $20 comes from the side pot. This is hard. (How to build the pots is in how to build side pots.)
  • $2 and $3 chips. Some casinos allow them in the game and some don't. Only color your rake up into one while the hand is still going if those chips are allowed in play, because the chips in the pot might still end up in front of a player.

What I correct in class

Missing the rake on a split pot in an audition

When a student realizes after handing out a split pot that they missed the rake, my advice is: in an audition, call the floor. "Table 16, I just handed this out and I forgot to take the rake." You could figure it out given a minute, but everyone is watching and waiting, and that makes it infinitely harder. The habit to carry over is that split pots and stacked pots get raked too.

Dropping the rake

Push the pot, move the button, drop the rake

At the end of every hand, in this order, no exceptions:

  1. Push the pot.
  2. Move the button.
  3. Drop the rake. Pull the spring-loaded rake slide back and it falls into the box. Drop the jackpot at the same time, then clear your hands.
  4. Drop your tip.
  5. Clean up the board and get to the next hand.

The instinct is to pick up the cards first. I correct that constantly. If you clean the board first, you're going to forget the rake, or lose track of the button.

Check the rake every time you touch the pot

The second your hand touches the pot to push it, glance at the rake. It doesn't take any more time. You're just looking over with your head. Do it 30 times, 40 times, however many it takes, until you never have to think about it again. That instinct will protect you whenever you forget, in an audition and after you're hired. Forgetting to take the proper rake is possibly the most common way dealers get fired, and once you've pushed the pot, it's supposed to be too late. That's a problem you don't want to have. I don't know why more poker schools don't teach this.

Drop the biggest chips you can

Never drop five white chips when you could have dropped one red chip. Always color the rake up from the pot, so a $5 rake is one red chip, and a $7 rake is a red chip and two whites, not seven whites. Those boxes have a finite amount of space. If they fill up with white chips, security has to do an emergency drop, there's paperwork, and it's more work for everybody. When I was a supervisor at Coconut Creek in South Florida, they kept chopsticks on the tables because they couldn't get the dealers to drop large chips, and they had to push the chips around in the box so more would fit. The first time I saw that, I almost fell over. When you have a full rake or a full jackpot, do one quick proof and stack it with the large chip on the bottom.

There's a bonus, too. Coloring up the rake puts white chips back in the pot, and a pot with white chips in it is a lot more likely to get you tipped. Doing the job right helps you. (Making change to keep the rake up to date does the same thing, and it's completely different from hustling. That's covered in bets, calls and making change.)

Once the rake is on the slide, leave it alone. Be professional. Don't play with it or mess with it.

What I correct in class

Tapping the table before you drop the rake

Tapping is how you tell surveillance you're putting a tip in your tip box. So if you tap the table and then drop the rake, it looks very suspicious. Tap for tips. Never for the rake. And the opposite mistake happens too: a student gets so used to anything on that side being a tip that they make a tip motion with the rake. You drop the rake through the slide.

Time rake and time pots

In larger games, many rooms charge for time instead of raking every pot. Players who play more hands pay more rake in a per-hand game, and in a 5/10 no-limit or 5/5 pot-limit Omaha game, almost every pot hits the max. If it's a $5 max and you deal 12 hands in 30 minutes, that's $60. Eighteen hands is $90, so $180 an hour. With time, it's even for everybody and every playing style is encouraged. Your casino will teach you their way, but here's how it generally goes:

  1. When you tap in, say "I need time, everybody." Each seat puts out the set amount. It might be $6, $7, $8 or $9. You never decide that.
  2. Go around in one direction and verify every single seat has paid before you take anything. If someone hasn't, tell them, "I need the $7 here." In a time game, a locked-up seat usually pays even if the player is away from the table, so you'd ask someone to take it off that player's stack.
  3. Then take it, making change from the pot, not from the players' chips, and color it up.
  4. Put it where your room says. Some rooms want it on the rake slide, some want it right next to the slide until a supervisor comes and verifies it. One room I worked in had us keep it off the spot where the rake goes, because after two hours of dealing games where you drop the rake every hand, your instinct at the end of a hand is to pull the slide.

The worst scenario is a dealer who starts collecting before everybody has put it out. Then somebody didn't pay, nobody knows who, and nobody wants to go to surveillance to find $7. It's one of the most annoying floor calls you can get. Rooms also have rules for new players, like a 15-minute window where a player who sits down still owes time for that half hour.

Not every room collects it at the table. In Texas, for example, players pay at the host stand and the money shows up in their account, so you don't collect it at the table like this.

A time pot avoids collecting from everybody. The table and the house agree on a pot size that's going to be reached every 30 minutes (at 5/10, $500 is a common number), and the first pot that reaches it pays the whole table's time. With nine players at $7, you take $63 out of that pot, just like a normal rake. Some of these games also tell the dealer to take a $25 tip from that same pot.

The downside of any time rake is that it calls attention to how much the players are spending, and some players feel like they have to get hands out to make it worth it. Follow the rules and the procedure, even when a player thinks you're needling them. You're the one enforcing the integrity.

There's one more version I've rarely seen but heard of: the button puts the rake out before every hand, like a blind, and the dealer collects it.

Rake reductions

When a table gets short, it can seem unfair to keep taking a full rake, so a lot of casinos allow rake reductions. As a dealer, you never offer one. You don't hint at it. You represent the house, and you keep taking a full rake until somebody says something. A dealer who gets caught suggesting it is costing the casino money and can get in trouble.

If a player asks, you never say no. You say, "I'll ask," and you call the floor. Your job is to be the messenger. Common practice is a $3 max at six players, $2 at five, and $1 at four or fewer, but some casinos won't go below $3 no matter how few players there are. (How I had dealers signal this when I was a supervisor is in cash game procedures.)

Tips

Tips are how you make your money as a dealer, and how you handle them matters.

  • Say thank you, every time. Poker rooms are very big on this, and they'll enforce it. The more sincerely you can do it, the better. I can't tell you how many times players have decided not to tip someone because they weren't thanked. It's an opportunity to show appreciation, which is one of the most valuable ways you can affect somebody.
  • Always accept a tip. Even if a player upset you, take it and say thank you. On behalf of all dealers, never say "keep your dollar." If someone is upsetting you at the table, that's a real problem and it should be handled, but refusing a tip is not the way.
  • Tap, drop, clear. Tap the corner of the bank or table (twice, on the upper right, or however your casino says), put the tip in your tip box, and clear your hands. The tap is a visual and audio cue for surveillance that money is going into your box.
  • Leave it where it lands and get to it right after you drop the rake. If it needs to be secured, move it over to the side where your tip box is. Don't pass tip chips from hand to hand.
  • Never color up a tip. Take what you're given. Tips stay separate from everything else.
  • Mid-hand tips are fine in a cash game. Sometimes a player tips before the flop because they have a feeling about the hand. Thank them and drop it right away.

Real, non-manipulative connection gets you tipped better. When a player shares something personal and you're genuinely excited for them, that's the kind of thing that turns into a big tip. What you can't do is trick people into tipping you. Breaking a pot down into white chips just so it's easier to tip you is hustling, and it can get you fired. (More on that in making change, and on how tips work in tournaments in cash game procedures.)

Players cards and comps

A lot of casinos give players comps for playing poker, something like $1 per player per hour at the table to spend on food. Someone who played 10 hours yesterday and five today has $15 for a free meal, and that's a reason to come back to your room.

So if a player sits down without a players card, offer to get them one: "If you give me your ID, I can have someone make you one." Usually a supervisor, chip runner or brush comes to the table to do it. Every time that player sees the card in their wallet or their car, it's an advertisement for the poker room. You make your money by people coming to play, and if the whole staff does this, it can be worth a table or two at any given time.

How to practice the rake

  • Never put the rake in your bank, even at home. You'll create the habit, and taking the rake and putting it in the bank can get you in serious trouble. Pick one designated place and always use it: a cup holder on the right, a bucket on the floor. Same for your tips.
  • Deal four hands at a time to an empty table, making up the action, and practice as if it's a 5% uncapped game so you count the pot all the way, every hand.
  • If you lose track, start over. Don't count the chips in the pot to catch up, or you'll train yourself to do exactly that. Force yourself to get it right.
  • Add distractions. Have someone ask you what time it is and talk to you while you deal. If you can count the pot and take the right rake through that, you're golden.
  • Drill the end of the hand: push the pot, glance at the rake, move the button, drop the rake, drop the tip, clean up.
  • Don't rush. When you're learning, figure out everything you have to do properly, and make people wait if they need to wait. The people running an audition see everything. If you try to slide a mistake by, you're automatically not hired, by me and by anyone who agrees with how I hire dealers. I'd much rather hire someone who made a mistake and handled it with integrity.

Common questions about rake and tips

What is the rake in poker?

It's the money the casino takes out of each cash game pot, like a commission. Because players play against each other, not the house, the rake is how the poker room makes money. In tournaments, the same thing comes out of each entry as an entry fee.

How much rake does a casino take?

It depends on the room. One structure is 10% up to a $5 max, taken on the tens or on the sixes. There are also $3 max games, rooms that go as high as $7, 5% up to $15, and 5% with no cap. The dealer never decides it.

What does "on the sixes" mean?

You take $1 at $6, $16, $26, $36 and $46, instead of at $10, $20, $30 and so on. Over the long run it works out to about 10%, where taking it on the tens is really between 5% and 10%.

What does "no flop, no drop" mean?

If the hand ends before the flop, no rake is taken. Almost every poker room does this, but some still take $1 for the jackpot drop, and a few rake every hand.

When does a dealer take the rake?

Every street: bring the flop, turn or river first, then update the rake while the players act, until you hit the max. That way you can push the pot the moment the hand ends.

What is a jackpot or promo drop?

A separate amount taken from the pot, usually on the opposite side from the rake, that's paid back to the players through bad beat jackpots and promotions like high hands.

What is a time rake?

Instead of raking each pot, every seat pays a set amount every 30 minutes. It's more common in bigger games, where nearly every pot would hit the max rake anyway.

Should a poker dealer ever refuse a tip?

No. Always accept it and say thank you, even if the player upset you. If a player is causing a problem, get it handled another way.
