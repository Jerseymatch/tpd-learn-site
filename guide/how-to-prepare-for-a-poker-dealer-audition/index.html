How to Prepare for a Poker Dealer Audition

True Poker Dealer · Module 6, Lesson 1

Auditions are stressful even for dealers who know the mechanics cold — an entire job is riding on a few minutes at a felt, usually in front of someone whose job is to find reasons to say no. The good news is that what gets you hired isn't flawless play. It's whether the table feels like real poker when you're in the box, whether your procedure holds up under pressure, and how you handle it when something inevitably goes wrong.

Set up a practice table that actually resembles one

You don't need a casino-grade setup to practice this correctly. A tray or even a simple chip rack, a reasonable pile of cheap chips in two or three denominations, a couple of spacers to separate full stacks from short ones, and a regular deck of cards covers almost every scenario an audition will throw at you. A missed-blind button and a real cut card help you look more polished, but the equipment doesn't have to be perfect — a joker can stand in for a cut card in a pinch, and nobody auditioning you is going to fail you over it. What they will notice is whether your procedure is perfect regardless of what equipment you're using.

Set the bank up the way you'd set up a real one: chips organized by denomination, spacers in place, everything within reach the way it would be at a table. The point of practicing this way isn't the equipment — it's training your hands to do the setup without you having to think about it, so that on audition day none of your attention is spent on logistics.

Know what a typical audition actually covers

Most in-person or video auditions start with a hand or two of limit Texas hold’em, then move into no-limit hold’em, and from there into whatever mixed games the room wants to see — commonly pot-limit Omaha, and sometimes a specific scenario like a big multi-way all-in or a double-board bomb pot. If a room tells you in advance which games they care about most, believe them and weight your practice accordingly: there's nothing wrong with spending extra reps on pot-limit Omaha if that's what the job actually needs.

Within each hand, you'll be asked to run through the full sequence — shuffle, cut, pitch, manage the betting rounds, bring in bets correctly, make change, and run the showdown — exactly as you would at a live table. A video audition adds one extra wrinkle: since there usually aren't real players physically present, you're often the one placing bets into position and even playing both roles out loud, narrating decisions the way an auditioner would just to keep the hand moving.

Understand what's actually being evaluated

More than anything else, an auditioner wants to know if it feels like poker when you're dealing. That's a harder thing to fake than raw speed, and it's why going as fast as you possibly can isn't actually the goal — a comfortable, controlled pace that never breaks procedure beats a fast pace that falls apart under pressure almost every time. Auditioners are watching your fundamentals: correct bring-ins, clean pitches, accurate pot math, proper chip handling, and the dozens of small habits that together make a table feel like it's being run by someone who's actually been trained, not someone who learned the rules from a book.

They're also watching how you carry yourself. Crossed arms, a hand resting below the rail, or anything else that reads as disengaged or unprofessional can sink an otherwise clean audition on its own — keep your hands visible and your posture attentive the entire time, even when nothing's actively happening in the hand.

Mistakes don't end an audition — how you handle them does

You will almost certainly make at least one mistake somewhere in an audition, and that's genuinely fine. Auditioners expect it and are far more interested in how you respond than in whether you're perfect. The single most important habit to build before you ever sit down for a real audition is this: if something goes wrong, don't try to quietly fix it yourself. Call the floor, explain exactly what happened, and let them make the call. Trying to self-correct a mistake, or hoping nobody noticed, reads as far worse than the mistake itself.

Just as important: never give up on yourself partway through. A rough hand doesn't disqualify you — the very next hand is a fresh chance to show what you can actually do, and plenty of dealers who stumbled early in an audition still got the job because of how they recovered. Treat the whole session, start to finish, exactly like a real game in every way you can — that mindset alone does more for your odds than any single piece of technique.

Commonly corrected in training
  • Dealing noticeably slower than audition pace requires. A comfortable, controlled speed is fine, but if your overall pace is visibly slow, it needs to come up before audition day — practice with the specific goal of dealing faster, not just more accurately.
  • Speeding up before you're actually ready. A lot of students push their pace up because they believe, incorrectly, that speed alone is what gets you hired. Going fast before your fundamentals are automatic just produces more mistakes, not a better audition.
  • Crossing your arms at the table. This reads as an automatic red flag to an auditioner — keep your arms and hands visibly engaged with the game at all times.
  • Letting a hand rest below the table or rail. Like crossed arms, this is treated as an automatic failure indicator in an audition — both hands stay visible, always.
  • Forgetting to collect the rake. This is one of the most common mistakes dealers make during auditions, and supervisors notice every time — it has to become fully automatic well before audition day.
  • Not following a specific instruction the auditioner gave you. Auditioners sometimes deliberately give you a small instruction mid-hand just to see whether you follow it — if you're told to use certain chips or handle something a specific way, do exactly that.
Gear for practicing this at home
  • A chip tray or rack — you don't need a full casino bank, just something that lets you organize chips by denomination the way a real one would be set up.
  • Clay poker chips — enough volume to simulate real buy-ins, side pots, and the occasional big multi-way all-in.
  • A real cut card — inexpensive, and it presents far better in a video audition than a spare card from the deck.
