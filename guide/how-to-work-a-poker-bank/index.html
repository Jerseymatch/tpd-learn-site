How to Work a Poker Dealer's Bank

True Poker Dealer · Module 2, Lesson 1

The bank — also called the tray, the well, or the rack — is where every chip and dollar on your table lives when it isn’t in play. Everything else in this module, buy-ins, color-ups, making change, is really just you reaching into this tray correctly and putting things back the same way. Learn to interact with it cleanly and keep it organized, and every one of those moments gets faster and harder to get wrong. Learn it sloppy, and you’ll be fighting your own tray all night, every night.

Plucking and pinching chips out of the tray

For one or two chips, “plucking” is fastest: middle finger pushes up into your thumb from behind the chip, index finger comes down on top to regrip it, and the chip (or small group) lifts cleanly out in one motion. Done right, you can do this without looking — once you know where a denomination lives in your tray, your hand should be able to find it on its own.

For a specific count — say, five out of the white tube — “pinching” is the move: separate the target number with your thumb, keep your grip with your index finger, and roll your middle finger to the side to settle them into a splash. Most dealers need to look while pinching at first; eyeballing five or four chips accurately is a skill that comes with reps, not something to fake early on.

Verifying a stack without pulling it out

If you need to know how many chips are in a stack, resist the urge to pick the whole thing up, set it on the felt, and count it loose — that’s exactly how chips end up on the floor, in someone else’s stack, or scattered across the table. Instead, push the stack forward and pinch back the correct count in groups (fives for $1/$2/$5/$100 chips, fours for 50¢/$25/$500 chips — the same cutting convention covered in how to cut chips). You’ll see exactly how many are left behind without the stack ever leaving the tray.

It’s fine to work with two hands in the tray at once — one handling chips, one holding spacers — as long as they never cross. Crossing your hands inside the bank is an opening for theft, real or perceived, and it’s avoided without exception.

Working outside-in, right before left

When you need to take chips out of the bank, always work the outside tube before touching the inside tube. And when two stacks of the same denomination sit at equal distance from the center, take the right-hand one before the left. Neither rule is arbitrary: it keeps every dealer, and surveillance, able to predict exactly where you’ll pull from next, which is the entire point of having a standard in the first place.

The casino-standard layout

Largest denomination goes toward the inside and closest to the dealer; lowest denomination sits on the outside, farthest from you. That’s game protection — if anyone ever reaches in uninvited, they get the least valuable chips possible, and it’s the first thing they’d have to get past either way. Every complete stack is 20 chips with a spacer on top to mark it as full; an incomplete group (a “short stack”) gets no top spacer, so anyone glancing at the tray can tell a full stack from a broken one instantly. Keep exactly one short stack per denomination — if you end up with two, consolidate them into one rather than letting them sit side by side.

Cash works the same logic. Bills face down, largest denomination innermost and protected, each smaller denomination folded in front of it working outward, angled so surveillance can see multiple corners of each bill. Clear your hands every time after you touch cash.

Fixing a messy bank

If you ever inherit a genuinely messy bank — and it will happen — don’t try to quietly sort it out while pretending everything’s fine. Call the floor as soon as you sit down and let them know what you walked into; that protects you, not just the bank. When you do rebuild one, start with the largest denomination first and work outward, hold spacers in your off hand instead of setting them on the table, and build complete 20-stacks before you worry about anything else. The one rule that never bends: chips in a messy bank get sorted inside the tray, never pulled out and arranged loose on the felt first.

Commonly corrected in training
  • Pulling chips from the bottom of a stack instead of top-down. A professional dealer always takes chips top-down, outside in — reaching underneath a stack is one of the fastest tells of an untrained hand.
  • Crossing hands inside the tray. Working two hands in the bank is fine; letting them cross each other is not — it’s treated as a theft risk every time, intentional or not.
  • Passing spacers hand-to-hand while working in the bank. Even spacers can’t move directly from one hand to the other while you’re in the tray — it makes it impossible for surveillance to confirm nothing else moved with them.
  • Taking chips out of the bank and setting them on the felt to sort a mess. It feels like it would be easier, but the rule holds even for a genuinely messy tray: sort it inside the bank, not on the table.
  • Not calling the floor for an unworkable bank. New dealers often try to quietly fix a bank that’s far too messy to be their fault, when the right move is simply to flag it to the floor the moment they sit down.
  • Leaving two short stacks of the same denomination. It happens easily when you’re pulling from alternating sides — the fix is to consolidate into a single short stack per denomination, every time.
  • Rebuilding a messy bank out of denomination order. When you’re reorganizing from scratch, start with the largest denomination chips first and work outward — starting in the middle of the tray makes the whole rebuild slower and harder to verify.
Gear for practicing this at home
  • A chip tray or rack — you can’t really drill outside-in order or tube organization without an actual tray to work in.
  • Clay poker chips — real weight and texture matter for plucking and pinching cleanly; lightweight plastic chips teach the wrong feel.
  • Chip tray dividers / spacers — practice marking full stacks correctly; it’s a habit that only builds with the real thing in hand.
