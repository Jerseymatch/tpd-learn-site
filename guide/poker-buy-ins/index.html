How to Handle Poker Buy-Ins Like a Casino Dealer

True Poker Dealer · Module 2, Lesson 2

A buy-in is only two things: something coming in to the dealer, and something going out to the player. That sounds simple, and the mechanics are — but a buy-in happens dozens of times a shift, in front of surveillance, with real money, so the procedure around it is exact. Get the habits right here and every buy-in becomes fast and unremarkable, which is exactly what you want.

Incoming left, outgoing right

Your tray splits into a working space: the left half is where everything coming in from the player lands — almost always cash. The right half is where everything going out to the player gets built. Keep that split consistent every single time, so there’s never a moment of confusion about what’s already been accounted for and what hasn’t.

Taking the cash

Never take money out of a player’s hand — have them set it down on the table first. That one habit alone removes almost every possible dispute about what was handed over. Lay the bills angled so surveillance can see multiple corners of each one, face down, largest denomination closest to you. If a bill comes in backward or upside down, take the extra second to fix it before you start working — it makes everything after easier to read on camera.

Keep the cash in front of you the entire time you’re building the buy-in. It sounds obvious, but it’s the single most common way a buy-in goes wrong: a dealer builds the chip stack, hands it off, and only then realizes the cash never made it into the bank. If it’s sitting in front of you the whole time, you physically can’t forget it’s there.

Building a readable stack

Chips go out on the right side of your working space, largest denomination closest to you, exactly the same logic as the bank itself. Cut every stack to the standard convention — fives for $1/$2/$5/$100 chips, fours for 50¢/$25/$500 — and separate a short stack from a full one rather than leaving an odd count buried inside a stack. When you’re breaking a larger denomination down into a smaller one, it often helps to mirror the layout: if the cash is displayed as one full group plus a short amount on top, build the chip stack the same way, so a glance at either side tells the same story.

Never stack two different chip denominations directly on top of each other. It looks tidy to you and to the players in the moment, but to a camera reviewing footage later, two different colors stacked together can be genuinely hard to tell apart from two stacks of the same color. Keep every denomination in its own clearly separated group.

Matching the denomination to the game

Read the table before you decide what to give someone. The general rule is to give the largest denomination reasonably possible rather than a pile of small chips — it’s easier for everyone to play with, easier to count, and easier on your bank. But that rule bends to the game and the table: in a $2/$4 limit game, there’s no reason to hand out red $5 chips just because your bank has plenty; in a game already heavy on one color, match what’s already in play rather than introducing a denomination nobody else is using. If a player has a specific, reasonable request and your bank supports it, accommodate it.

Finishing the buy-in

Pick the finished stack up with the lower denomination on top (or to the side, if you're sliding more than one stack at once), and push it to the player in one clean motion. Then immediately take care of the cash sitting in front of you — fold it into the correct position in your well and clear your hands. Tidy the bank itself (lamming off short stacks, re-squaring anything you disturbed) whenever there's a natural gap in the action, not mid-hand. And where you can avoid it, don't run a buy-in in the middle of an active hand at all — chips and cash moving around while live chips are already in play is exactly the kind of moment that creates confusion if anything falls into the pot. Your supervisor's house rules always take priority over any of this.

Commonly corrected in training
  • Taking cash straight from the player's hand. Even an experienced-looking player will sometimes just hand you the bill — the dealer's job is to have them set it on the table every time, no exceptions.
  • Stacking two denominations directly on top of each other. It's one of the most common early habits, and it's exactly the kind of shortcut that creates a real problem for anyone reviewing the footage later.
  • Defaulting to small denominations instead of the largest reasonable one. New dealers often hand out a pile of white or red chips out of habit when the game and the bank both support giving the player fewer, larger-denomination chips instead.
  • Miscounting the stack before handing it off. Grabbing one chip too many or too few happens to everyone — the habit to build is re-proving the stack before it goes out, every time, not just when you think something looks off.
  • Pulling buy-in chips from a player's existing stack instead of entirely from the bank. The chips for a buy-in always come from the well, never partly from what's already in front of the player — mixing the two never happens in a real game and reads as wrong immediately.
  • Losing track of the cash because it wasn't kept visible. This is the single easiest buy-in mistake to make and the easiest to prevent — keep the bill in front of you the entire time you're building the stack.
Gear for practicing this at home
  • Prop / play cash — you can't drill buy-in realism without something to actually set down, angle, and display.
  • Clay poker chips — for building the readable, correctly cut stacks this lesson covers.
  • A chip tray or rack — practicing the incoming-left, outgoing-right workspace only works with a real tray in front of you.
