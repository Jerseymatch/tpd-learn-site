Working the Floor & Handling Mistakes

True Poker Dealer · Module 6, Lesson 2

Every dealer makes mistakes — even experienced ones, even in real casinos, on a regular basis. What separates a dealer who gets hired from one who doesn't isn't a mistake-free table. It's knowing exactly when to stop, call the floor, and let someone else make the call, and it's having your fundamentals drilled in deeply enough that the small habits auditioners are quietly watching for are never in question in the first place.

The rule that matters more than any other: don't fix it yourself

The single biggest instinct you need to train out of yourself is the urge to quietly correct a mistake on your own. The moment you realize something's gone wrong — you mucked the wrong hand, moved chips you shouldn't have, missed a rake, pitched without burning — the correct response is always the same: stop touching anything, and call the floor. Once a supervisor is at the table, you freeze completely. You don't keep moving chips, you don't keep talking to players, and you wait for their exact instructions before doing anything else.

When you explain the situation to the floor, give them the actual facts in order, not your interpretation of what they mean. If a player said something ambiguous, repeat their exact words rather than summarizing what you think they intended — the floor needs the raw information to make a correct ruling, and secondhand interpretation can send them in the wrong direction. Stay honest about exactly what happened, even when it makes you look bad, and stay neutral if a player disputes your account: only state something as certain if you genuinely are certain, and say so plainly if you might be wrong. Once the floor rules, that ruling stands, even if a player pushes back — resistance from a player is itself a reason to involve the floor, not a reason to argue your own case.

This applies well beyond big errors. An ambiguous verbal action, an unfamiliar situation, a stray chip that's drifted somewhere it shouldn't be — any time you're genuinely unsure, the floor is the answer, not a guess. New dealers in particular should lean on this heavily: when in doubt, call it. Nobody is impressed by a dealer who guesses confidently and gets it wrong.

Why small habits get noticed more than you'd expect

An experienced auditioner isn't just grading whether you got the math right. They're watching for whether your motions look like they came from real training — and a single small deviation from standard procedure, even something as minor as how you pick the deck back up after a cut, can read as a loud warning sign. The logic is simple from their side: if you're missing one fundamental, it raises the question of what else you might be missing that just hasn't come up yet. A dealer who's been trained properly doesn't have those gaps, so a visible one stands out immediately, even in an otherwise clean audition.

That's exactly why the small procedural details matter as much as they do: the burn card goes underneath the pot, never stacked visibly on top. When bringing bets in, count the number of players and the per-player amount first, keep any dead money separate in your head, and only combine everything into one number once you've actually added it — don't push chips together before you've done the math. Tap the table gently before burning or dealing a new street, enough to get attention without startling anyone. Use a clean thumb-and-finger motion to square and flip cards rather than an improvised grip. None of these are difficult on their own. What makes them matter is that an auditioner will clock the ones you get wrong, every time.

Turning a correction into a habit, not a thought

Knowing a correction and actually having it under pressure are two different things. The way you close that gap is repetition — not until you can do it correctly once, but until you can't do it wrong. That might mean 20 reps, it might mean 100, depending on the habit and how deeply the old version is ingrained. The test isn't whether you can execute it when you're thinking about it carefully; it's whether it happens automatically when your attention is somewhere else, because that's exactly the condition you'll be in during a real audition.

This is also why it's worth being honest with yourself about which habits are actually fixed and which ones you've only been told about. Being shown the correct method once doesn't make it a habit — it just means you now know what to practice. Go back through anything you've been corrected on and drill it specifically and repeatedly until your hands do it without any conscious thought, well before you're relying on it in front of someone deciding whether to hire you.

Commonly corrected in training
  • Continuing to move chips after calling the floor. The moment a supervisor is at the table, stop completely and wait for their exact instructions — don't keep handling chips while you wait.
  • Arguing with a player instead of calling the floor. Once a player resists a ruling, even one you're confident is correct, that's the floor's job to handle, not yours to defend.
  • Guessing on an unclear or obscure situation. An ambiguous verbal action or an unusual scenario should go to the floor rather than being interpreted on your own.
  • Summarizing instead of relaying a player's exact words to the floor. Give the floor the raw facts and exact language used, not your own conclusion about what it meant.
  • Apologizing for something that isn't actually your fault. Stay neutral and factual when something goes wrong at the table rather than reflexively saying "I'm sorry."
  • Treating a single correction as a finished habit. Being shown the right way once just means you know what to drill — it isn't a habit until it happens automatically, under pressure, without having to think about it.
Gear for practicing this at home
  • A couple of card decks — drilling the burn-card and pitching motions to automatic requires real repetition, not just reading about it.
  • Clay poker chips — practice the dead-money-first counting method until it's faster than combining chips first and fixing the math after.
