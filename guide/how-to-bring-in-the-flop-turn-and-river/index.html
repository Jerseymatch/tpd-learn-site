How to Bring In the Flop, Turn & River

True Poker Dealer · Module 3, Lesson 2

Bringing in a street — the flop, the turn, or the river — is one of the most repeated motions in the entire job, which means small habits here compound fast, good or bad. The core idea behind every step is the same: protect the card until it's safe to reveal, then present it so the whole table can read it instantly.

Tap the table first

Before bringing any street, tap the table — not loud, not a hammer, just enough that everyone notices. Players miss their turn to act more often than you'd expect, especially the big blind in Hold'em, who's frequently the last to act and sometimes assumed (incorrectly) to have already checked. The tap is a quick, universal signal: if you haven't acted yet, the next street is coming.

Burning and protecting the card

Every street starts with a burn card, and the entire point of burning is making sure nobody can ever identify the top card of the deck before it's dealt. Take it with your thumb, index finger underneath it and on top of your thumb, and keep your deck hand low and close to the felt the whole way down — the card should be fully shielded from every angle until it's safely under the pot. Dealers who rush this step sometimes let the card bend or tilt as it travels, exposing a corner; take the extra half-second to keep it flat and covered.

Bringing the cards with space between them

Bring each community card down and out with your thumb and index finger, laying the flop's three cards (or the single turn or river card) with real space between them — a little felt visible between each card. Cards that are touching or overlapping are genuinely harder for the whole table to read at a glance, and it's one of the clearest visual tells of an inexperienced dealer. There's more than one accepted technique for the physical motion itself; what matters is the result: clean, evenly spaced, fully visible cards every time.

Bringing in bets and the rake

After all action completes on a street, bring every bet into the pot before moving to the next card. Update the rake as you go — on the flop, the turn, and the river, each time — rather than trying to calculate the whole thing at the end of the hand, which is both slower and more error-prone. When you do take rake, convert it to fewer, higher-denomination chips where you can, so the drop box doesn't fill up quickly. And remember the pot itself, not whatever's freshly been bet, is where change for the rake should come from whenever that's possible.

Counting the stub along the way

A typical hand that goes to the river uses three burn cards and five community cards — eight cards total beyond what's dealt to players. That's the foundation for verifying your deck stays at 52 cards, covered in full in the next lesson on counting the stub.

Commonly corrected in training
  • Not tapping the table before a street. It's an easy habit to drop under pressure, but it's the single cheapest way to prevent a missed action from turning into a real dispute.
  • Exposing a corner of the burn card. Rushing the burn motion, or grabbing the card without fully shielding it, is one of the fastest tells of an inexperienced dealer — take the half-second to do it right.
  • Cards touching or overlapping when the flop is brought in. It looks fine in the moment but makes the board genuinely harder to read at a glance — always leave a little felt between each card.
  • Taking the rake at the wrong time, or forgetting it on a given street. The habit to build is updating the rake after every single street, not catching up on it later — being short on a rake count is one of the most unrecoverable mistakes a dealer can make in an audition.
  • Bringing a card before bringing in the bets from the previous street. Sequencing matters — finish collecting action before the next card comes out, not the other way around.
Gear for practicing this at home
  • A couple of card decks — the burn-and-bring motion needs real reps to become smooth and fully protected.
  • A felt layout — cards present differently on felt than on a hard table; train on the surface you'll actually work on.
  • Clay poker chips — for drilling bringing in bets and tracking the rake at full speed.
