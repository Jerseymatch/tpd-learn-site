How to Deal Triple Draw & Lowball Games

True Poker Dealer · Module 5, Lesson 4

Draw games — 2-to-7 triple draw, single draw, and the lowball and Badugi variants that share the same skeleton — run on a rhythm hold'em and stud dealers aren't used to: a full betting round, then every player gets a turn to swap cards, then another full betting round, repeated as many times as the game calls for. The mechanics are simple once the pattern clicks, and one habit — burning before every single replacement card — matters here more than in any other game you'll deal.

The cycle: bet, then draw, then bet again

Nothing about discarding happens until the current betting round is completely finished and every bet's been brought in. Once that's done, it's time to draw. Discards go in order starting with whoever acted first in that betting round — they're in the worst position, so they also go first to discard. Each player either announces how many cards they're discarding (anywhere from zero to four) or says they're standing pat, meaning they're keeping all their cards and drawing nothing. Watch for players acting out of turn here — it happens more in draw games than most, since people get eager to toss their cards the moment they decide what they want. A calm, casual reminder to wait their turn is all that's needed; it's not worth making a scene over.

Burn before you replace, every single time

Before pitching replacement cards for any draw round, burn one card first. This isn't a minor formality here the way it might feel in other games — the burn is literally how the table and the dealer both track which draw round is happening. Skip it and pitch straight from the deck, and the correct response isn't to quietly take the cards back and fix it yourself; it's to call the floor. Trying to self-correct a missed burn is one of the fastest ways to turn a small mistake into a much bigger one.

Once a player's discards are collected into their own pile (kept separate from the muck until the round's replacement cards are out), pitch their replacement cards and move to the next player, continuing until everyone who wanted new cards has them. Only then does the next betting round begin.

Badugi and other lowball variants

Badugi, and the other draw-based lowball games you'll run into, use this exact same bet-draw-bet rhythm — nothing about the procedure changes. The only thing that's different is what hand the players are actually trying to make: Badugi's target is four cards of four different suits, all different ranks, with the lowest combination winning, instead of the five-card low hand you're reading in 2-to-7 or A-to-5 lowball. Learn the draw procedure once here and it carries over to every one of these games without any extra dealer mechanics to pick up.

Commonly corrected in training
  • Skipping the betting round after a discard round. Every single discard round needs its own complete betting round after it — it's easy to accidentally fold that into the next draw by mistake.
  • Not verbally announcing each player's pat or discard status in order. Say it out loud for each player as you go — it keeps the whole table, and you, oriented on who's done what.
  • Pitching replacement cards without burning first, then trying to fix it yourself. If you catch that you skipped the burn, call the floor rather than quietly taking cards back and re-dealing.
  • Rushing an unfamiliar draw game instead of slowing down. These games have more individual steps packed close together than most — take the time you need rather than speeding up and pitching a burn card by mistake.
Gear for practicing this at home
  • A couple of card decks — the bet-draw-bet cycle really only clicks once you've run it a few times with real cards.
  • A chip tray or rack — useful for running full betting rounds between each draw.
